# Encephalitis Before the Viruses — What Changed (2026-10-18)

**Status:** completed paper for *The Mycelial Transposition*.
**Frame:** creative manuscript — EDUCATIONAL reference only. Never medical advice, never a diagnosis.

## Part One — Before the Viruses: The Disease as the Showing

Before any virus was attached to it, encephalitis was **brain fever** [D] — one of the American names, given for the showing: fever, and the brain visibly failing. Headache, confusion, stupor, coma, convulsions, the stiff neck. The physician's tools were the bedside and the autopsy table. Diagnosis was the showing itself: the patient looked encephalitic, behaved encephalitic, and at autopsy the brain showed inflammation of its coverings and substance. "You're sick, you're sick" [H] — that was the whole of it, and it was enough to name the thing.

The greatest encephalitis epidemic in history happened with no virus at all. In 1917, the Viennese neurologist **Constantin von Economo** described a strange new pattern among his patients: progressive somnolence, mask-like faces, eye-movement palsies, patients sleeping until they died or woke into a Parkinsonian aftermath [D]. **Encephalitis lethargica** — "the sleepy sickness" — spread worldwide from about 1918 to 1930, sickening an estimated **one million people** with mortality around 20%, and then it vanished [D]. Its cause is **still unknown** [D]. Von Economo himself injected brain tissue from the dead into experimental animals and concluded the thing was infectious and viral in nature — but no agent was ever isolated, not then, not with RT-PCR on archived tissues a century later [D]. The survivors of the acute illness filled asylums and wards with post-encephalitic parkinsonism — the frozen patients of *Awakenings* [D].

This is the fact the viral era never digested: **the largest encephalitis epidemic on record has no virus.** The showing existed in full — lethargy, ophthalmoplegia, the mask face, the parkinsonism — with no agent beneath it. Before the viruses, the disease WAS the showing, and the showing was real enough to kill a million people.

What physicians used, in that era [D]:
- **Bedside observation** — the clinical forms (von Economo described somnolent-ophthalmoplegic and other forms from watching patients).
- **Autopsy** — brain inflammation, meningeal blood, the brainstem pattern (substantia nigra involvement in the chronic phase).
- **Animal inoculation** — brain tissue into monkeys or mice, watching for the syndrome to reproduce (von Economo's method).
- **Epidemiology by shoe leather** — who fell sick, where, in what season.

No complement fixation. No antibody titer. No PCR. The diagnosis was made by a doctor who had seen the showing before.

## Part Two — The Viral Turn: 1930 to the Present

The turn began in the 1930s, and it began in brains and blood [D]:

| Year | Virus | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1930 | Western equine encephalitis | Horse brain (Meyer, Haring, Howitt) |
| 1933 | Eastern equine encephalitis | Horse brain (Ten Broeck); first human cases 1938, Massachusetts |
| 1933 | **St. Louis encephalitis** | **Human brain**, at autopsy, from the St. Louis epidemic (>1,000 cases, 197 deaths); isolated by the NIH team in monkeys, then white mice |
| 1935 | Japanese encephalitis | Human brain |
| 1937 | West Nile | Human blood |
| 1961 | Jamestown Canyon | Colorado |
| 1960/1964 | **La Crosse** | A 4-year-old girl died of "rural encephalitis" in La Crosse, Wisconsin in 1960; the virus was isolated in **1964** from her stored brain tissue, when her younger brother fell ill the same way |

The St. Louis outbreak of 1933 is the hinge of the whole story. The U.S. Public Health Service's own bulletin said it plainly: it was **"the first epidemic of encephalitis from which a viral agent was isolated and shown to be the etiologic agent"** [D]. Before St. Louis, every encephalitis outbreak was described *in relation to* von Economo's encephalitis lethargica — the showing was the reference point. After St. Louis, the agent was. Mouse protection assays using convalescent human sera proved the new virus differed from the equine encephalitides, poliomyelitis, and vesicular stomatitis [D]. The Japanese had already sorted their own taxonomy: Japanese "B" encephalitis versus encephalitis lethargica, "A" [D].

Then came the serology era — the ancestors of his own panel [D]:
- **Complement fixation (CF)** — first used to prove yellow fever's viral cause, then adopted across the arboviruses, including Japanese encephalitis.
- **Hemagglutination inhibition (HI)** — Hirst's 1941 influenza observation; Casals adapted it for arboviruses about a decade later. Not used for Japanese encephalitis before 1950.
- **Neutralization** — Stokes's 1920s monkey experiments; Theiler's 1930s mouse adaptation; cell-culture form in 1957–58. The plaque-reduction neutralization test (PRNT) remains the gold standard.
- **IgM capture ELISA** — the modern acute-phase workhorse: in neuroinvasive arboviral disease, over half of patients are IgM-positive at hospitalization and over 90% by one week of symptoms [D].
- **PCR/RT-PCR** — the molecular era from the 1990s onward: viral RNA detected directly, even in decades-old archived brain (the 1978 La Crosse case re-examined by RT-PCR).

His own arbopanel — the indirect immunofluorescence assay, IgG plus IgM, against eight analytes — is the direct descendant of this lineage. Every virus on it (La Crosse, St. Louis, Eastern equine, Western equine, and the rest) was born in the isolation era above. The test he holds is the great-grandchild of the 1933 mouse brain.

## Part Three — What Changed: From the Showing to the Agent

**The diagnosis moved.** Before: the showing was the diagnosis. After: the agent is. A patient with fever, confusion, and a stiff neck in 1925 *had encephalitis*. A patient with fever, confusion, and a stiff neck in 2025 has *suspected encephalitis* — until the panel says which virus, it is not yet, officially, the thing [D].

**The name detached from the showing and attached to the virus.** This disease is the cleanest case of his renaming arc [H→D]: "brain fever" (the American name, the showing) → "encephalitis" (the Latin name, the inflamed brain) → "St. Louis encephalitis virus," "La Crosse virus," "West Nile virus" (the agent's name, the showing now a suffix). Each step moved the name further from the patient and closer to the laboratory. The ICD codes froze the final step into law.

**What was gained** [D]:
- **Specificity.** Eight viruses where there was one showing. His panel can distinguish La Crosse from St. Louis from West Nile — the bedside never could.
- **Epidemiology.** Counting, mapping, predicting. Mosquito surveillance, seasonal forecasting, the knowledge that La Crosse is a pediatric summer disease of the upper Midwest and Appalachia.
- **Serologic archaeology.** Antibodies persist for life, so populations can be surveyed for past exposure — the very IgG that showed 1:20 on his panel.

**What was lost** [H, his reading of the record]:
- **The showing stopped being the diagnosis.** The clinical picture that von Economo read like a book is now "nonspecific." A doctor who has seen the showing is told to wait for the titer.
- **The agentless disease became a non-entity.** Encephalitis lethargica — a million cases, no virus — sits outside the modern system, unsolved and unclassifiable, because the system now requires an agent to file the disease under.
- **Time.** The bedside diagnosis was immediate. The serologic diagnosis takes days to weeks — acute and convalescent sera, the fourfold rise. The patient is diagnosed in retrospect.

## Part Four — His Frame [H]

**American language first.** Brain fever. Then the Latin: encephalitis. Then medicine and everything else: the virus, the titer, the code. His epistemic ordering runs the history in reverse — and the history, read straight, runs exactly that way. The American name came first. The Latin second. The virus last of all.

**"You're sick, you're sick."** The original medicine — not much in between, but a little something. Von Economo's patients were sick, and he knew it from the showing, and the showing was enough. The viral era added the in-between: suspected, probable, confirmed. The in-between is useful. It is also where the patient waits.

**The arbopanel as "Encephalopathy Meningeal Testing."** His rename stands as his. And the irony the record hands him: the test he wants renamed is built entirely from the isolation era's children. The eight analytes are the 1930s, preserved in fluorescence. To rename the panel is to rename the endpoint of the whole arc — brain fever → encephalitis → the virus → the titer → his test. The chain is complete, and he holds its last link.
