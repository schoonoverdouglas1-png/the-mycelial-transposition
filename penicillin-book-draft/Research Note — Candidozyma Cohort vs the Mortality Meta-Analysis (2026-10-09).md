# Research Note — *Candidozyma* Cohort vs. the Mortality Meta-Analysis: Crude Says One Thing, Adjusted Says Another

**Filed:** 2026-10-09 · For local peer review alongside *The Mycelial Transposition* manuscript
**Source:** Peer-reviewed paper, *BMC Infectious Diseases* (2026), Kuzi et al., Ankara Etlik City Hospital, Turkey; DOI 10.1186/s12879-026-14401-4
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14401-4
**Also checked:** Scienmag summary of the same paper, searched 2026-10-09 — used only to cross-check the headline numbers below against the journal record.

## The study (in brief)

- Single-center retrospective cohort of 301 consecutive adults with candidemia: 77 with *Candidozyma auris* (formerly *Candida auris*) and 224 with non-*auris Candida* species; species identified by MALDI-TOF.
- Crude 30-day all-cause mortality was essentially identical between the groups (62.3% vs. 62.5%).
- After multivariable adjustment (Firth-penalized logistic regression, multiple imputation), *C. auris* candidemia pointed toward higher mortality: adjusted odds ratio 1.97, 95% CI 0.954–4.055, p = 0.067 — just short of conventional statistical significance. Standardized (g-computation) 30-day risks: 69.7% vs. 59.3%, a difference of 10.4 percentage points with a confidence interval from −0.2 to +21.3.
- Across the whole cohort, older age, higher SOFA score, septic shock, corticosteroid use, and hemodialysis independently predicted 30-day death.
- Susceptibility, in summary: echinocandin non-wild-type phenotypes were uncommon among the *C. auris* isolates; elevated amphotericin B MICs appeared in roughly a third of tested isolates. (Details in the paper; not treatment guidance for any individual.)

## Why file it next to the 2026-10-08 meta-analysis note

- The Frontiers meta-analysis (16 studies, 16,525 patients, filed 2026-10-08) pooled **crude** odds and found mortality significantly *lower* in *C. auris* candidemia (OR 0.77).
- This cohort shows why that headline needs the caveat already in that note: in a single center, crude mortality is a tie, and once patient severity is adjusted for, the point estimate flips direction — while remaining statistically imprecise (the interval includes no effect).
- The two papers do not contradict each other so much as they measure different things: pooled crude comparisons across heterogeneous centers vs. one center's adjusted comparison. For peer review, keep them filed as a pair — the tension between them is the honest version of the "dreaded name vs. measured data" story.

## The nomenclature hook

- This paper's title adopts the new name — *Candidozyma auris* — where the Frontiers meta-analysis still headlined *Candida auris*. Same season, same organism, two naming choices in two journal titles.
- Filed together with the *Nakaseomyces glabratus* note (same day), the manuscript now has a small adoption spectrum to point to: new name in the title (this paper), old name kept despite acknowledgement of the rename (the *glabrata* paper), and old name in the title with the rename discussed elsewhere (*C. auris* meta-analysis, Nairobi case).

## Honest caveats (do not drop these)

1. **Adjusted result is not statistically significant** (p = 0.067; CI includes 1.0 for the odds ratio and 0 for the risk difference). It is a hint, not a finding — the authors themselves call for larger prospective multicenter work.
2. **Single-center, retrospective.** One Turkish tertiary hospital's patient mix and practice; not generalizable on its own, and residual confounding is expected in this design.
3. **All-cause mortality**, not fungus-attributable — in a critically ill, highly comorbid population, attribution was not feasible (authors' stated, pre-specified choice).
4. **Not evidence for the reclassification argument.** Its manuscript use is the crude-vs-adjusted tension and the title-level naming adoption — nothing more.

## Open questions for peer review

- Pairing question for the perception-vs-measurement chapter: if the dreaded name triggers vigilance (the meta-analysis authors' unmeasured hypothesis), could that vigilance be part of why crude pooled mortality looks *lower* while adjusted single-center estimates look *higher*? Flag this as a question to pose, not a claim to make — neither paper measures the mechanism.
- Does the three-way naming-adoption spectrum (adopted / acknowledged-but-kept / lagging) deserve its own short section, or is it better as a running example through the nomenclature chapters?
