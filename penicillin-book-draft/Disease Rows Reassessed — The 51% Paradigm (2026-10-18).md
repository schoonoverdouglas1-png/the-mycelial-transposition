## Part I — Group 1, rows 1–20 (Tuberculosis through Anthrax)

### Tuberculosis
- **Fungal connections:** [D] Residual cavitary disease after treated pulmonary TB is the classic bed for *Aspergillus* colonization — aspergilloma ("fungus ball") and chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis, with hemoptysis as the signature complication [D — Wikipedia "Aspergilloma"; Uzair et al., SAGE Open Med Case Rep 2024]. [D] Histoplasmosis mimics TB clinically and radiographically and is a documented diagnostic confounder in endemic regions [D — IDSA histoplasmosis context, StatPearls]. [D — 51%] New at the 51% bar: more than 1 million people treated for TB go on to develop chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) each year, usually misdiagnosed as relapsed TB because symptoms and X-ray features are nearly identical [D — Denning et al., Bulletin of the WHO; Manchester/Toronto report]. Denning (Sri Lanka J Respir Med 2025) estimates 10–20% of patients diagnosed with presumed pulmonary TB actually have a chronic fungal lung infection [D — Denning 2025]. In an India cohort, 38 of 100 post-TB CPA patients had been re-treated for TB; in Vietnam, 13 of 38 CPA cases had prior repeated TB treatment [D — PMC9143176]. [H] A prospective Ethiopia cohort (Bitew et al., SAGE 2021) found potential fungal pathogens associated with PTB in 77.9% of PTB patients, but the TB–fungal association was NOT statistically significant — caveat: sputum co-culture can reflect colonization rather than true infection [H — Bitew et al., SAGE Open Med 2021].
- **Yeast connections:** [D — 51%] New at the 51% bar: a global systematic review/meta-analysis (ResearchSquare rs-5475; published S0882401019316651) found a pooled Candida co-infection prevalence of 25.7% (95% CI 23.7–27.9) among pulmonary TB patients, with *C. albicans* accounting for 65.8% of Candida spp. [D]. A Chinese hospital cohort found fungal infection in 39.9% of pulmonary TB patients, with *C. albicans* the dominant isolate (42.65% of fungal isolates) [D — PMC11909571]. The Ethiopia cohort recovered 148 yeast isolates (80.9% of fungi) from TB-associated samples — *C. albicans*, *C. tropicalis*, *C. krusei*, non-albicans spp., plus 2 *Cryptococcus neoformans* [D — Bitew et al., SAGE 2021]. Documented risk factors for Candida co-infection: smoking, diabetes, advanced age, low BMI [D — rs-5475 meta-analysis]. Caveat filed beside every figure: co-occurrence vs true infection is debated (sputum Candida is often colonization), and the HIV-co-infection confound is real — a separate meta-analysis found no significant difference in oral candidiasis between HIV patients with vs without pulmonary TB (OR 1.77, 95% CI 0.69–4.52) [D — ScienceDirect S088240102031086X].
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] *M. tuberculosis* is itself the bacterium of record; documented secondary bacterial pneumonias in TB patients (e.g., *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*) occur with cavitary lung disease [D — StatPearls/Merck Manual TB complications]. Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (*M. avium* complex) can co-exist in structurally damaged lungs [D — ATS mycobacterial guideline].
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC/ATS/IDSA 2016 updated treatment guideline; CDC 2025 update] Drug-susceptible TB: 2 months isoniazid + rifampin + pyrazinamide + ethambutol (2HRZE), then 4 months isoniazid + rifampin (4HR), total 6 months. New 4-month option: 2 months isoniazid + rifapentine + pyrazinamide + moxifloxacin, then 2 months isoniazid + rifapentine + moxifloxacin (2HPZM/2HPM) for adults/adolescents ≥12 years. MDR/rifampin-resistant TB: all-oral 6-month BPaLM (bedaquiline + pretomanid + linezolid + moxifloxacin) or BPaL if fluoroquinolone resistance.

### Streptococcus pneumoniae (drug-resistant invasive)
- **Fungal connections:** [D — 51%] New at the 51% bar: invasive pulmonary aspergillosis is a documented secondary complication in the severe-CAP pathway where *S. pneumoniae* is the classic superinfecting bacterium. In the Dutch-Belgian ICU cohort, 19% of 432 ICU influenza patients developed influenza-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (IAPA), and in ICU community-acquired pneumonia the detection of influenza strongly predicted subsequent IPA — with ~50% ICU mortality in IAPA cases [D — Rijnders/Schauwvlieghe, Clin Infect Dis 2020, PMC7583400]. A case report documents the full chain: influenza → pneumococcal pneumonia → *S. aureus* bacteremia → acute invasive pulmonary aspergillosis [D — Seitz 2019, Case Rep Infect Dis]. COPD comorbidity and inhaled corticosteroid use independently raise IPA risk ~3–3.8-fold in severe non-influenza CAP [D — PMC11127357]. Caveat: influenza co-infection is the driver in the population-scale data; pneumococcal-only (non-influenza) severe CAP with IPA is documented at case-report level, not population scale [H].
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast co-pathogen of invasive pneumococcal disease at 51%. (Candida recovery from sputum in pneumonia patients is typically airway colonization, not infection, and is not a documented *S. pneumoniae* connection.)
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Post-influenza secondary bacterial pneumonia is the classic pattern: *S. pneumoniae* follows influenza virus damage to airways, and it co-occurs with *S. aureus* (incl. MRSA), *H. influenzae*, and *M. catarrhalis* in community-acquired pneumonia [D — IDSA/ATS community-acquired pneumonia guideline context]. Up to 20% of severe CAP episodes are polymicrobial [D — PMC7152161]. In meningitis it is one of several bacterial differentials (N. meningitidis, Listeria, H. influenzae) [D — IDSA bacterial meningitis guideline 2004].
- **Medication choices:** [D — IDSA meningitis guideline 2004; IDSA CAP summaries] Empiric meningitis: vancomycin (15–20 mg/kg IV q12h) + ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h (or cefotaxime 2 g IV q6h); add rifampin 600 mg BID in dual penicillin/cephalosporin resistance; duration 10–14 days. Non-meningitic invasive disease: high-dose amoxicillin (susceptible), or ceftriaxone/cefotaxime; resistant strains: respiratory fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), vancomycin, or linezolid [D — IDSA CAP guidance]. Prevention is vaccine-based (PCV/PPSV), but no antibiotic prophylaxis is standard.

### Measles
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal co-infection or colonization data for measles at 51%. (Mechanistic context only, not a connection: measles causes immune amnesia — depletion of memory lymphocytes with increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections lasting 2–3 years [D — Mina et al., Science 2015; Laksono et al., PMC6251901] — but no measles-specific fungal co-infection figures are documented.)
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51% (same immune-amnesia context applies; no measles-specific candidiasis data documented).
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Measles's documented bacterial sequelae are secondary bacterial pneumonia (often *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, *H. influenzae*) and acute otitis media — the leading causes of measles mortality in children [D — NEJM measles review, Strebel et al.]. The virus itself also causes giant-cell pneumonia directly.
- **Medication choices:** [D — NEJM measles review; CDC measles guidance] No specific antiviral; management is supportive plus vitamin A — AAP recommends two age-specific doses (200,000 IU ≥12 mo; 100,000 IU 6–11 mo; 50,000 IU <6 mo), with a third dose 2–4 weeks later if deficiency signs persist. Antibiotics only when secondary bacterial pneumonia, otitis media, or sepsis is diagnosed. Post-exposure prophylaxis: MMR within 72 hours of exposure, or immune globulin within 6 days for high-risk persons [D — CDC measles PEP guidance].

### AIDS
- **Fungal connections:** [D] AIDS-defining fungal opportunists: cryptococcal meningitis (*Cryptococcus neoformans*), Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP, a fungal pathogen), invasive aspergillosis, histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, and talaromycosis (penicilliosis) — all tracked in the DHHS opportunistic-infection guidelines [D — DHHS OI guidelines; IDSA 2016 cryptococcal guideline]. [D — 51% enrichment] Population-scale numbers: PCP was the AIDS-defining event in ~65% of cases with an ultimate prevalence of ~85% in the pre-prophylaxis era; cryptococcosis has an ultimate prevalence of 5–13% in HIV-infected individuals [D — PMC3328018].
- **Yeast connections:** [D] Oropharyngeal and esophageal candidiasis are among the most common AIDS-associated yeast infections; recurrent vulvovaginal candidiasis is also documented [D — DHHS OI guidelines]. *Candida* esophagitis is an AIDS-defining condition. [D — 51% enrichment] Over 90% of patients with AIDS develop oropharyngeal candidiasis at some point during their illness; Candida esophagitis occurs in more than 10% of AIDS patients [D — PMC3218701].
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Recurrent bacterial pneumonia (*S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*), tuberculosis and *M. avium* complex, non-typhoidal salmonellosis, and syphilis co-infection are all documented bacterial burdens in advanced HIV [D — DHHS OI guidelines].
- **Medication choices:** [D — DHHS antiretroviral guidelines; StatPearls AIDS ART review] Antiretroviral therapy (ART): preferred initial regimens are integrase-strand-transfer-inhibitor (InSTI)–based — bictegravir/tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine (BIC/TAF/FTC) or dolutegravir + TAF/FTC (or TAF/lamivudine). Drug classes: NRTIs, NNRTIs, protease inhibitors, InSTIs, CCR5/fusion/entry inhibitors. Second-line after InSTI failure: boosted protease inhibitor (e.g., darunavir/ritonavir) + 2 NRTIs, guided by resistance testing. Opportunistic fungal/yeast disease is treated with its own antifungals (e.g., fluconazole for candidiasis; amphotericin B + flucytosine induction then fluconazole for cryptococcal meningitis) [D — IDSA cryptococcal guideline].

### Meningococcal disease
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen of *N. meningitidis* invasive disease at 51%; it is typically a monomicrobial invasive infection.
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC meningococcal clinical guidance; CDC Pink Book ch. 14; IDSA meningitis guideline] Empiric therapy: extended-spectrum cephalosporin (ceftriaxone or cefotaxime). Once susceptibility is confirmed, penicillin G or ampicillin; treatment duration ~7 days per IDSA meningitis guidance. Chemoprophylaxis for close contacts (ideally <24 h): ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone, or rifampin (90–95% effective at eradicating nasopharyngeal carriage); since 2024, CDC advises preferring ceftriaxone/rifampin/azithromycin over ciprofloxacin in areas with ciprofloxacin-resistant strains [D — CDC MMWR 2024].

### Hepatitis B
- **Fungal connections:** [D — 51%] New at the 51% bar: chronic HBV infection and HBV-cirrhosis are documented with increased fungal translocation — elevated serum fungal beta-glucan vs controls, and patients with chronic HBV hepatitis or HBV-cirrhosis more frequently test positive for *Aspergillus* spp., *Candida* spp. (*C. albicans*, *C. tropicalis*), and *Saccharomyces* spp. in stool than controls [D — Journal of Hepatology review 2022, S0168-8278(22)03415-8]. A systematic review/meta-analysis of fungal infections in cirrhosis found a pooled prevalence of 10.2% (6.0–16.9), with *Candida* then *Aspergillus* the commonest pathogens [D — Verma et al., meta-analysis]. Caveat: these data belong to the cirrhosis/advanced-liver-disease stage, not to uncomplicated chronic HBV; stool/beta-glucan signals are mycobiome-translocation data, not proven invasive infection in most cases [H].
- **Yeast connections:** [D — 51%] New at the 51% bar: *Candida albicans* infection rate of 9–16% is documented in cirrhosis (skin, mucous membranes, GI tract, bloodstream) [D — PMC11292070]; in an ICU cirrhosis cohort, fungal evidence (predominantly *Candida*, especially *C. albicans*/*glabrata*, with some *Aspergillus fumigatus*) was found in 54% of cirrhotic ICU patients, mostly lung, urine, blood, and ascites [D — PMC3642762]. Caveat: cirrhosis-stage data — HBV is one etiology among several (alcohol, NASH, HCV) in these cohorts [H].
- **Bacterial connections:** [D — 51%] New at the 51% bar: at the cirrhosis/advanced-liver-disease stage of chronic HBV, documented secondary bacterial infection burden — spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, bacteremia, and sepsis are established complications of decompensated cirrhosis regardless of viral etiology [D — cirrhosis complication literature; PMC11903580 ICU context]. Caveat: this is a cirrhosis-stage connection, not a feature of uncomplicated chronic HBV infection [H].
- **Medication choices:** [D — AASLD hepatitis B guidelines; standard-of-care reviews] Chronic HBV first-line: nucleos(t)ide analogues with high resistance barrier — entecavir, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), or tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), usually indefinite. Alternative: pegylated interferon-α for a finite 48-week course in selected patients (finite duration, higher seroconversion, but poorer tolerability; contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis). Older agents (lamivudine, adefovir, telbivudine) are supplanted by resistance concerns. Acute hepatitis B: supportive care only. HBV vaccine and hepatitis B immune globulin are the preventive/post-exposure tools.

### Rabies
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented bacterial co-pathogen connection to rabies encephalitis itself at 51%. (Wound management includes tetanus prophylaxis per wound-care protocol [D — CDC rabies PEP materials], but that is wound care, not a rabies–bacterium connection.)

### Diphtheria
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Cutaneous diphtheria wounds are frequently polymicrobial, with *C. diphtheriae* co-isolated alongside *S. aureus* and *S. pyogenes* in chronic skin lesions [D — CDC diphtheria surveillance manual context]. Respiratory diphtheria itself is monomicrobial toxin disease.
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC diphtheria clinical guidance; CDC surveillance manual ch. 1] Respiratory diphtheria: diphtheria antitoxin (DAT, equine; obtained from CDC) given on clinical suspicion without awaiting culture, PLUS erythromycin or penicillin for 14 days (e.g., erythromycin 40 mg/kg/day max 2 g/day; procaine penicillin G IM). Cutaneous diphtheria: antibiotics (erythromycin or penicillin × 14 days); antitoxin rarely needed. Close contacts: antimicrobial prophylaxis — single IM benzathine penicillin G or erythromycin 7–10 days — plus toxoid vaccine updating.

### Yellow fever
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] No characteristic co-pathogen, but secondary bacterial infections are documented complications of severe yellow fever and are treated with antibiotics per WHO/PAHO guidance [D — WHO yellow fever fact sheet; PAHO yellow fever topic page]. (Note the author's six-track interest: jaundice/severe cases can resemble leptospirosis, a bacterial disease, as a diagnostic differential [D — WHO].)
- **Medication choices:** [D — WHO yellow fever fact sheet; CDC Yellow Book] No specific antiviral; management is supportive — rest, hydration, management of liver and kidney failure, fever control, with hospitalization (often ICU) for jaundice/severe disease. ### Pertussis
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Secondary bacterial infections documented in pertussis — sinusitis, otitis media, bronchitis, pneumonia — with pathogens *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, *H. influenzae*, and *M. catarrhalis*, because *B. pertussis* denudes ciliated epithelium and impairs local phagocytosis [D — Mandell-type Bordetella reference, oncohemakey].
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC 2005 pertussis treatment guideline (MMWR RR-14); CDC pertussis clinical care] First-line: macrolides — azithromycin (preferred, incl. infants), clarithromycin, or erythromycin; alternative: trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Same agents/doses for post-exposure prophylaxis of high-risk contacts. Antibiotics eradicate nasopharyngeal carriage and shorten infectiousness (to ~5 days of treatment) but do not substantially alter the paroxysmal cough course once established. Ampicillin, amoxicillin, fluoroquinolones, and cephalosporins are NOT recommended.

### Tetanus
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented specific bacterial co-pathogen of tetanus toxin disease at 51%. (The portal is typically a soil-contaminated, polymicrobial wound, but no named co-pathogen relationship is documented in guidelines.)
- **Medication choices:** [D — Tintinalli's Emergency Medicine; Drugs.com TIG monograph; Hesperian/WHO-aligned protocols] Active tetanus: human tetanus immune globulin (TIG) to neutralize unbound toxin (treatment doses 3,000–6,000 U IM in classic texts; 500 IU commonly used in modern practice — the toxin already bound to neurons is unaffected), metronidazole 500 mg IV q6h preferred as the antibiotic (penicillin G is an alternative but may worsen spasms via GABA antagonism) for 10–14 days, benzodiazepines (diazepam/midazolam) for spasm control, wound debridement, and ICU support with ventilation. Recovery does NOT confer immunity — active tetanus toxoid vaccination is still required [D — Drugs.com TIG monograph].

### Hepatitis C
- **Fungal connections:** [D — 51%] New at the 51% bar: chronic HCV infection, including HCV-cirrhosis, is documented with a significantly higher fecal fungal load and more frequent *Candida* spp. positivity than controls [D — Journal of Hepatology review 2022, S0168-8278(22)03415-8]. The cirrhosis-stage pooled fungal-infection prevalence of 10.2% (Candida then Aspergillus) and the ICU-cirrhosis fungal-evidence data apply to HCV-cirrhosis as they do to HBV-cirrhosis [D — Verma et al., meta-analysis; PMC3642762]. Caveat: mycobiome/cirrhosis-stage data, not proven invasive infection in uncomplicated chronic HCV [H].
- **Yeast connections:** [D — 51%] New at the 51% bar: documented via the same cirrhosis-stage pathway as hepatitis B — *Candida* infection rate 9–16% in cirrhosis, with candidemia and multi-site fungal evidence in decompensated cirrhotic ICU patients (predominantly *C. albicans*/*glabrata*) [D — PMC11292070; PMC3642762]. Caveat: cirrhosis-stage data; HCV is one etiology among several in these cohorts [H].
- **Bacterial connections:** [D — 51%] New at the 51% bar: at the cirrhosis/advanced-liver-disease stage of chronic HCV, documented secondary bacterial infection burden — spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, bacteremia, sepsis are established complications of decompensated cirrhosis regardless of viral etiology [D — cirrhosis complication literature]. Caveat: cirrhosis-stage connection, not a feature of uncomplicated chronic HCV [H].
- **Medication choices:** [D — AASLD/IDSA HCV guidance; WHO 2022 chronic HCV guideline; 2025 KASL guideline] Pan-genotypic direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) are the standard: sofosbuvir/velpatasvir × 12 weeks, or glecaprevir/pibrentasvir × 8 weeks for treatment-naïve patients without cirrhosis or with compensated cirrhosis. DAA classes: NS3/4A protease inhibitors, NS5A inhibitors, NS5B polymerase inhibitors. For DAA-experienced or resistant cases: sofosbuvir/velpatasvir/voxilaprevir. Sustained virologic response rates ≥95%; interferon-based therapy is obsolete. No vaccine exists.

### Smallpox
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Secondary bacterial infections of skin lesions are documented smallpox complications (alongside sepsis, corneal ulceration, encephalitis) [D — emDocs ToxCard: Smallpox].
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC smallpox clinical treatment page; FDA smallpox preparedness page] Mainstay is supportive care (hydration, nutrition, secondary-infection prevention). FDA-approved antivirals: tecovirimat (TPOXX, oral, 14 days weight-based, approved 2018) and brincidofovir (Tembexa, two weekly oral doses on days 1 and 8, approved 2021) — both stockpiled in the Strategic National Stockpile; cidofovir has anti-orthopoxvirus activity but is not FDA-approved for smallpox. Vaccination within 2–3 days of exposure can prevent or lessen disease [D — DoD public-health reference sheet]. (Smallpox declared eradicated 1980; no natural cases since 1977 [D — WHO/FDA].)

### Cholera
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen at 51%. (Note: zinc supplementation, 20 mg/day × 10–14 days in children 6 mo–5 yr, is documented adjunct care for watery diarrhea [D — GTFCC/choleraoutbreak.org].)
- **Medication choices:** [D — WHO; Cochrane review "Antimicrobial drugs for treating cholera" (PMC4468928); GTFCC technical note 2022] Rehydration is the mainstay — oral rehydration salts for mild/moderate dehydration, IV Ringer's lactate for severe dehydration; antibiotics are adjunct for severe cases (halving stool volume/duration): first-line doxycycline single oral dose 300 mg (adults), or azithromycin 20 mg/kg single dose (max 1 g) where tetracycline resistance is documented; tetracycline 500 mg q6h × 3 days is the older alternative. Choice is guided by local susceptibility testing due to documented resistance to tetracyclines, cotrimoxazole, and fluoroquinolones.

### Plague
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen at 51%; secondary pneumonic plague is a progression of *Y. pestis* itself, not a second organism. Buboes respond to antibiotics and should not be incised/drained routinely [D — WikEM/CDC clinician guidance].
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC 2021 plague treatment guideline (MMWR); CDC clinician guidance] First-line: aminoglycosides (gentamicin 5 mg/kg IV/IM daily, or streptomycin) or fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin); doxycycline is first-line-acceptable especially for bubonic plague. Duration 10–14 days total (transition IV→oral when improving). Dual therapy with two distinct classes is recommended for bioterrorism scenarios with possible engineered resistance. Post-exposure prophylaxis: doxycycline or ciprofloxacin. Chloramphenicol or TMP-SMX are considered effective alternatives in special situations.

### Anthrax
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 51%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 51%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen at 51%; anthrax is monomicrobial *B. anthracis* toxin disease.
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC 2023 anthrax prevention/treatment guideline (MMWR RR-6); StatPearls anthrax infection] Uncomplicated cutaneous: ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO q12h or doxycycline 100 mg PO q12h (14 days if no systemic signs). Systemic/inhalational/GI/injection anthrax: IV combination therapy — one bactericidal agent (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, or meropenem) PLUS one protein-synthesis inhibitor (clindamycin or linezolid; doxycycline), ≥14 days IV then oral to complete 60 days total. Add antitoxin if toxemia is suspected: raxibacumab 40 mg/kg IV single dose, obiltoxaximab 16 mg/kg IV single dose, or anthrax immune globulin (Anthrasil). Post-exposure prophylaxis: doxycycline 100 mg q12h, ciprofloxacin 500 mg q12h, or levofloxacin 500 mg daily × 60 days plus anthrax vaccine series.

### Haemophilus influenzae (invasive)

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — No documented fungal co-pathogen or secondary fungal relationship in invasive H. influenzae disease (meningitis, bacteremia, epiglottitis) in the standard literature. Fungal coinfection is not a recognized feature of the disease course.

### Group A streptococcal disease (invasive)

- **Fungal connections:** [D] — Fungal necrotizing fasciitis (classified as Type IV necrotizing fasciitis) is a documented rare entity, with reported cases caused by Candida albicans and other fungi [D — case literature / clinical reviews]. Caveat: extremely rare — a distinct entity and isolated co-detection, not a co-pathogen of GAS disease; most fungal NF occurs in immunocompromised or diabetic hosts.

For invasive disease (necrotizing fasciitis, toxic shock syndrome), guidelines add clindamycin to penicillin to suppress toxin production, and recommend aggressive surgical debridement [D — IDSA necrotizing fasciitis / STSS guidance]. ### Salmonellosis

### Typhoid fever

- **Fungal connections:** [D] — Fungal superinfection complicating typhoid intestinal perforation is documented at case level: a typhoid patient developed post-operative peritonitis with pure Geotrichum species isolated from peritoneal fluid after pre-pyloric perforation repair [D — Journal of Clinical and Diagnostic Research case report]. ### Staphylococcus aureus disease
- **Fungal connections:** Documented fungal co-pathogens of *S. aureus* disease itself are uncommon [D]. In the literature, the documented mixed infections with this organism tend to involve *Candida* species rather than filamentous fungi — see the yeast line. - **Yeast connections:** The documented *S. aureus*–*Candida albicans* mixed biofilm is one of the best-characterized polymicrobial pairings in the literature: the two organisms co-colonize indwelling catheters and devices, and mixed bloodstream infections show increased virulence and antimicrobial tolerance [D]. Caveat: colonization vs true infection is not separable in these figures, and detection tracks diagnostic capacity [D].
- **Yeast connections:** Documented candidemia and invasive *Candida* bloodstream infections in prolonged ICU COVID-19 stays [D]; documented *Candidozyma auris* outbreaks on COVID-19 wards and ICUs, facilitated by strain on infection-control resources [D]. New at the 51% bar: population scale — a systematic review/meta-analysis (351,268 hospitalized COVID-19 patients) estimated COVID-19-associated candidemia prevalence of 4.33% in ICUs and 2.09% among hospitalized patients overall, roughly tenfold higher than pre-pandemic ICU candidemia prevalence, with *C. albicans*, *C. parapsilosis* complex, and *C. auris* the most reported species; a pooled mortality meta-analysis put candidemia-plus-COVID-19 in-hospital mortality at 62.6% [D]; the Israel multicenter cohort found invasive candidiasis at 10.86 cases per 1,000 critical-severity admissions [D]. The meta-analysis further estimated *C. auris* ICU prevalence of 1.22% among COVID-19 patients, with near-universal multidrug resistance [D]. Caveat: heavy ICU confounding — catheters, broad-spectrum antibiotics, corticosteroids, and prolonged stay drive much of the signal; species shifts (rising *C. parapsilosis*) track healthcare exposure [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented secondary bacterial pneumonia in severe COVID-19 — *S. aureus* (including MRSA), *S. pneumoniae*, *Klebsiella* spp., and ventilator-associated bacterial infections [D]; documented guidelines caution against routine antibacterials without confirmed/suspected bacterial infection [D]. New at the 51% bar: population scale — an updated global systematic review/meta-analysis (38 studies, 153,778 patients) found pooled bloodstream-infection prevalence of 7.1% overall and 12.2% in ICU settings, with BSIs raising mortality (OR 2.8) [D]; candidemia-COVID cohorts document bacterial co-infection rates of ~26% Enterobacterales, ~19% *Stenotrophomonas*, and ~10% MDR *Pseudomonas* in COVID-associated candidemia [D]. Caveat: preprint data in part of the pool; heterogeneity is high across centers [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per NIH COVID-19 guidelines: nirmatrelvir/ritonavir for early mild-moderate disease in high-risk patients [D]; remdesivir for hospitalized or early disease [D]; dexamethasone for patients requiring supplemental oxygen [D]; IL-6 receptor blockers (tocilizumab) and baricitinib for progressive severe disease [D]. Antifungals are documented for CAPA/CAM per the invasive-fungal guidelines above, and antibacterials only for confirmed/suspected secondary infection — not routine [D].

### Mpox
- **Fungal connections:** No documented systematic fungal co-pathogen connection in immunocompetent mpox [GAP as a systematic viral-link]. New at the 51% bar: the documented opportunistic-infection layer in advanced-HIV mpox — the Lancet 2023 global case series (382 mpox cases in people with advanced HIV, 19 countries) recorded concurrent opportunistic infections in 8% of patients, including histoplasmosis (2 cases) and *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (6 cases) [D]. Caveat: these track with CD4 <350 immunosuppression, not with the mpox virus itself — all deaths in the series occurred at CD4 <200; the fungal organisms ride the immune deficit, and the series' authors explicitly frame severe mpox in this context as behaving like an AIDS-defining condition [D].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link as a viral co-pathogen [GAP as a viral link]. New at the 51% bar: the same Lancet series recorded oesophageal candidiasis in 4 of 382 advanced-HIV mpox patients [D]; caveat as above — this is the HIV opportunistic layer, not an mpox-yeast mechanism [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented bacterial superinfection of mpox skin lesions — staphylococcal and streptococcal cellulitis, abscesses, and sepsis reported in the 2022–2024 outbreak literature [D]; documented severe proctitis and pharyngitis complications in the clade IIb outbreak [D]. New at the 51% bar: the Lancet advanced-HIV series documented tuberculosis as a concurrent opportunistic infection in 8 of 382 cases (2%), plus disseminated *Mycobacterium avium* intracellulare in 3 [D]; caveat: again the advanced-HIV layer, not mpox-driven [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per CDC: tecovirimat (TPOXX) is available under an expanded-access investigational protocol for severe mpox or patients at risk of severe disease [D]; the randomized STOMP trial of tecovirimat was documented as conducted during the outbreak [D]. Brincidofovir and cidofovir have documented investigational use in severe cases [D]; vaccinia immune globulin (VIG) is documented for severe disease in select cases [D]. Most cases are managed with supportive care per guidelines [D]; the JYNNEOS vaccine is documented for prevention and post-exposure prophylaxis [D].

### Mucormycosis
- **Fungal connections:** The fungus IS the disease — caused by Mucorales molds (*Rhizopus*, *Mucor*, *Lichtheimia* spp.) [D]. Documented clinical forms include rhino-orbital-cerebral, pulmonary, cutaneous, and disseminated disease [D]. COVID-associated mucormycosis (CAM) is the documented bridge to the COVID-19 row [D]. New at the 51% bar: documented mixed-mold co-infection — biopsy-confirmed simultaneous CAPA and CAM in one COVID-19 patient (Korean case report) [D]; caveat: single-case level [D].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented systematic yeast pairing [GAP]; mixed mold infections (mucormycosis plus aspergillosis) are rarely documented [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** No primary documented bacterial co-pathogen pattern [GAP]; occasional polymicrobial wound infections are reported in cutaneous cases [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per the ECMM/MSG global guideline (2019): liposomal amphotericin B first-line (5 mg/kg/day; 10 mg/kg/day with CNS involvement) [D]. Documented step-down or salvage: isavuconazole or posaconazole [D]. Prompt surgical debridement is documented as essential alongside antifungals, and delayed surgery is documented to worsen outcomes [D]. Glycemic control, reversal of immunosuppression, and corticosteroid optimization are documented adjunctive measures [D].

### SARS — paired with SARS-CoV AND SARS-CoV-2 per §217 [H]
- **Fungal connections:** His pairing rule [H]: both viruses are presented together. Documented — on the SARS-CoV (2003 SARS) side, secondary *Aspergillus* infections were documented in SARS ICU patients, particularly with corticosteroid use, including the fatal corticosteroid-associated aspergillosis case reported in the *New England Journal of Medicine* (Wang et al., 2003) [D]. On the paired SARS-CoV-2 side, CAPA and COVID-associated mucormycosis are documented (see the COVID-19 row) [D].
- **Yeast connections:** Documented candidemia in prolonged ICU courses on both the SARS (2003) and SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) sides [D]; the population-scale candidemia numbers are carried on the COVID-19 row [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented secondary bacterial infections in 2003 SARS — including MRSA and ventilator-associated pneumonia pathogens [D]; the SARS-CoV-2 side mirrors this with documented secondary bacterial pneumonia [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented — no specific proven therapy for SARS was ever established; 2003 management was supportive, with ribavirin and corticosteroids used empirically without demonstrated efficacy [D]. Lopinavir/ritonavir and interferon were studied afterward without becoming standard [D]. For the paired SARS-CoV-2 side, documented agents are nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, remdesivir, and dexamethasone per NIH guidelines [D]. No vaccine was ever licensed for SARS-CoV [D].
### Respiratory syncytial virus infection
- **Fungal connections:** Rarely documented as a systematic viral link — but the 51% bar now counts the documented ICU pattern: a Chicago retrospective cohort over 9 RSV seasons (224 adult ICU patients with severe RSV pneumonia) diagnosed invasive pulmonary aspergillosis in 3.5% (8/224) by EORTC/MSG or AspICU criteria, with longer hospital stays in IPA patients and all treated with voriconazole [D]; and a European multicenter study of RSV in hematological-disease patients found invasive fungal co-infections in 10.4% of cases — including 14 probable/possible invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, one *Rhizopus microsporus*, and one *Pneumocystis jirovecii* [D]. Caveat: the hematology cohort is profoundly immunocompromised, so the signal tracks immune deficit; IPA clustered with hematological malignancy, stem-cell transplant, and neutropenia [D].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link as a viral co-pathogen [GAP as a viral link]. New at the 51% bar: the same European hematology cohort documented candidemia in 5 RSV patients with hematological disease [D]; caveat: severely immunocompromised hosts only, not a general RSV-yeast connection [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented secondary bacterial pneumonia complicating severe RSV — *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, *H. influenzae* [D]; documented bacterial otitis media following RSV in children [D]; documented bacterial co-infection increases severity in hospitalized infants [D]. New at the 51% bar: the Chicago cohort documented bacterial co-infections in 13.7% of ICU RSV patients and non-IPA fungal pathogens in 8.5% [D]; caveat: single-center, severe-ICU denominator [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented — management is supportive (oxygen, fluids) per guidelines; bronchodilators and corticosteroids are documented as not routinely effective [D]. Palivizumab is documented for prophylaxis in high-risk infants [D]; nirsevimab is documented as a newer long-acting monoclonal antibody for infant prophylaxis [D]. Ribavirin has documented limited use in severely immunocompromised patients [D]. Antibiotics are documented only for confirmed bacterial co-infection [D].

### Varicella / herpes zoster
- **Fungal connections:** No documented fungal co-pathogen connection [GAP].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link [GAP].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented secondary bacterial skin infections of varicella lesions — *Streptococcus pyogenes* and *S. aureus*, including documented invasive group A streptococcal disease and necrotizing fasciitis as complications of chickenpox [D]; documented bacterial superinfection of zoster lesions in immunocompromised patients [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per the IDSA 2018 VZV clinical practice guideline: acyclovir, valacyclovir, or famciclovir for herpes zoster, ideally started within 72 hours of rash onset [D]; valacyclovir or acyclovir documented for varicella in at-risk patients [D]. The recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) and varicella vaccines are documented preventive measures per CDC/ACIP [D]. Documented postherpetic neuralgia is managed with gabapentinoids, tricyclics, or topical agents — a documented non-antiviral medication layer [D].

### Candidozyma auris disease
- **Fungal connections:** The organism is the disease — *Candidozyma auris* (formerly *Candida auris*) is the yeast pathogen itself; no separate non-yeast fungal connection is documented [D]. Its clades (I–VI) carry documented clade-specific resistance patterns, with >90% of global isolates fluconazole-resistant [D]; documented pan-resistant isolates (resistant to all three antifungal classes) exist at low frequency (~4% in some datasets) [D].
- **Yeast connections:** The yeast IS the disease [D]. Documented co-colonization with other *Candida* species in outbreak settings [D]; documented skin colonization that persists after clinical cure, sustaining transmission [D]. New at the 51% bar: the documented COVID-ICU candidemia pattern — a New Delhi ICU study found candidemia in 15 of 596 COVID-19 ICU patients (2.5%), with *C. auris* the agent in 10, mortality 53% overall and 60% in the *C. auris* cases despite micafungin [D]; the Qatar *C. auris* bloodstream-infection series found 89% of cases admitted for severe COVID-19 [D]; the regional literature documents COVID-era outbreak amplification with mortality up to 80% despite adequate therapy [D]. Caveat: healthcare-exposure signal — strain on infection control, catheters, and prolonged stays, not a viral mechanism [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** The documented *S. aureus*–*C. auris* mixed biofilm pairing — biofilm synergy documented in the literature — is the concrete bacterial connection [D]. This documented pairing is the foothold for his infection-track grouping of the three tracks together [H→D]. New at the 51% bar: documented bacterial co-infection within *C. auris* candidemia — COVID-associated candidemia series report Enterobacterales co-infection in 26.3%, *Stenotrophomonas maltophilia* in 18.8%, and MDR *Pseudomonas* in 10% of cases [D]; caveat: ICU denominator, polymicrobial sepsis pattern rather than an auris-specific bacterial partnership [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per CDC clinical treatment guidance: echinocandins are recommended initial therapy for clinical infection in adults — anidulafungin, caspofungin, or micafungin [D]. Liposomal amphotericin B is documented for echinocandin resistance or failure to improve after ~5 days [D]. Voriconazole is documented as a possible option only with confirmed susceptibility [D]. CDC documents that colonization alone (without clinical infection) should not be treated, and no decolonization regimen is documented as effective [D]. Infectious-diseases consultation and public-health reporting are documented as recommended [D].

### Cryptococcosis
- **Fungal connections:** The fungus IS the disease — *Cryptococcus neoformans* / *C. gattii* meningitis and pulmonary disease, the leading documented cause of adult meningitis in advanced HIV [D].
- **Yeast connections:** The pathogen is itself a yeast-like encapsulated fungus, so the disease sits on the yeast track by its documented taxonomy — the foothold for his grouping [H→D]. No separate yeast co-pathogen is documented [GAP for a distinct co-pathogen].
- **Bacterial connections:** No documented systematic bacterial co-pathogen of the organism itself [GAP]. New at the 51% bar: the documented cryptococcal-TB co-occurrence pattern in advanced HIV — a cohort of 870 HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis patients found 21% receiving contemporaneous TB treatment (6% prevalent, 8% concurrent, 9% incident during follow-up), with TB co-infection carrying an adjusted hazard of death of 1.75 [D]; the ACTA Malawi nested analysis found pulmonary tuberculosis among the top co-prevalent infections (12 of 187 episodes, 6%), with 56% of cryptococcal meningitis patients carrying at least one co-prevalent infection [D]. Caveat: the cohort era had little systematic TB screening, so many diagnoses were clinical rather than confirmed — under- or over-diagnosis is openly debated in the papers; and the signal is confounded by advanced HIV, the shared driver of both [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per WHO 2022 and the ECMM/ISHAM/ASM 2024 guideline (endorsed by IDSA): induction with single high-dose liposomal amphotericin B (10 mg/kg) plus 14 days of flucytosine and fluconazole for HIV-associated meningitis in resource-limited settings, per the AMBITION trial [D]; 14-day daily amphotericin B–based induction is recommended in high-resource settings per CDC/NIH/IDSA/HIVMA 2024 [D]; followed by fluconazole consolidation (800 mg daily × 8 weeks) then maintenance [D]. The older IDSA 2010 regimen documented amphotericin B deoxycholate (0.7–1.0 mg/kg/day) plus flucytosine for 2 weeks [D]. Documented cryptococcal antigen (CrAg) screening and deferred ART initiation are part of the documented management package [D].

### Pneumocystis pneumonia
- **Fungal connections:** The fungus IS the disease — *Pneumocystis jirovecii* (formerly *P. carinii*), an atypical fungus that cannot be cultured [D]; it was historically misclassified as a protozoan, a documented taxonomic correction [D].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link [GAP].
- **Bacterial connections:** No primary documented bacterial co-pathogen pattern specific to PJP [GAP]. New at the 51% bar: the documented TB–PCP association in advanced HIV — a Southern African cohort of 175,212 HIV patients starting ART found a history of TB associated with incident *Pneumocystis* pneumonia (adjusted HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.27–2.04) and with cryptococcal meningitis (aHR 1.28) in the first year of ART [D]; caveat: association, not causation — TB history here marks advanced HIV disease and low CD4, the shared driver, and the same study found no TB link for the non-infectious AIDS cancers, consistent with an immune-deficit signal rather than a bacterial interaction [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per CDC/NIH/IDSA opportunistic-infection guidelines: trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) is first-line for treatment and for prophylaxis (documented prophylaxis threshold: CD4 <200) [D]. Adjunctive corticosteroids are documented for moderate-to-severe disease with hypoxemia [D]. Documented second-line/alternative regimens: primaquine plus clindamycin, dapsone plus trimethoprim, or atovaquone [D]; documented pentamidine reserved for severe cases unable to take first-line options [D].

### Jamestown Canyon virus disease
- **Fungal connections:** No documented fungal connection [GAP].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link [GAP].
- **Bacterial connections:** No documented bacterial co-pathogen pattern [GAP].
- **Medication choices:** Documented — management is supportive only; no specific antiviral therapy is documented for Jamestown Canyon virus [D]. The disease is documented as under-recognized, with reported cases likely undercounting true incidence [D]. Prevention is documented via mosquito avoidance per CDC [D]; it is a documented nationally notifiable arboviral disease [D].

### Histoplasmosis
- **Fungal connections:** The fungus IS the disease — *Histoplasma capsulatum*, a dimorphic fungus endemic in the documented Ohio–Mississippi River valley and parts of Latin America [D]. Documented severe disseminated disease occurs in immunocompromised hosts [D].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented systematic yeast co-pathogen [GAP]; the organism's yeast phase in tissue is its own morphology, not a second organism [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented mimicry of tuberculosis in clinical and radiographic presentation — a documented diagnostic pitfall, and the "mimic" angle of his method [H→D]. New at the 51% bar: the documented histoplasmosis–TB co-occurrence/misdiagnosis numbers — a 2026 *Journal of Fungi* systematic review of published pulmonary cases documented both misdiagnosis (histoplasmosis treated as TB despite negative AFB/culture/PCR) and true co-infection; Ekeng et al. (Nigeria) found Histoplasma antigen/PCR positivity in 7.4% of TB-PCR-positive patients (probable co-occurrence) and 16.8% of TB-PCR-negative patients (probable misdiagnosis); Indonesian studies found Histoplasma galactomannan in 11.5% of TB patients' sera (Wijaya et al., 2024) and anti-*H. capsulatum* antibodies in 16.9% of bacteriologically confirmed TB patients and one-third of MDR-TB patients (Dewi, Kusmiati, Soeroso et al., 2023–2024); and a 2025 Mexican multicenter study (415 hospitalized HIV patients with suspected histoplasmosis) confirmed histoplasmosis in 26% by culture/histopathology, with TB the principal differential [D]. Caveat: antibody tests cannot separate active disease from past exposure, and antigen cross-reactivity limits certainty — these are co-occurrence/misdiagnosis signals at population scale, not per-patient confirmed dual infections [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per the IDSA 2007 histoplasmosis guideline: itraconazole for mild-to-moderate pulmonary and disseminated disease (with documented therapeutic drug monitoring in select cases) [D]; liposomal amphotericin B for severe pulmonary, disseminated, or CNS disease, with step-down to itraconazole [D]. Chronic cavitary disease is documented with prolonged itraconazole courses [D]; documented lifelong suppressive itraconazole for relapsed disease in ongoing immunosuppression [D].

### Dengue
- **Fungal connections:** No systematic population-scale fungal connection [GAP as a systematic connection]. New at the 51% bar: the documented post-dengue invasive fungal pattern at case-report/series level — published cases include post-dengue mucormycosis in immunocompetent hosts (Journal of Acute Disease case report; first reported Vietnamese rhino-orbital mucormycosis after severe dengue, PMC); three cases of invasive fungal rhinosinusitis (*Aspergillus* and mucormycosis) in immunocompetent hosts recovering from dengue (Singhal et al., cited in review); Lin et al. finding 2 of 10 dengue patients serum-galactomannan positive with confirmed invasive pulmonary aspergillosis coexistent with dengue; dengue-associated invasive CNS aspergillosis in an immunocompetent host; and a 2026 case of triple fungal pneumonia (*Aspergillus* + mucormycosis + candidiasis) in dengue shock syndrome [D]. Caveat: case-level evidence only — no population denominator, corticosteroid use and critical-care exposure confound, and the "virus-mediated host changes" mechanism proposed by authors is hypothesis, not established [D].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link [GAP]. New at the 51% bar: candidiasis appears as one component of the documented triple-fungal pneumonia case in dengue shock syndrome (2026, IJMMB) [D]; caveat: single case, critically ill denominator [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented bacterial co-infections (including bacteremia) occur in a subset of severe dengue cases in the literature [D]; documented secondary infections complicate prolonged hospitalization [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per WHO dengue guidelines: no specific antiviral — management is supportive with careful fluid resuscitation guided by documented warning signs (plasma leakage, shock, severe bleeding) [D]. NSAIDs are documented as contraindicated (bleeding risk); acetaminophen is the documented antipyretic [D]. Dengue vaccines (Dengvaxia, Qdenga) are documented with restricted indications — documented serostatus requirements for Dengvaxia per WHO [D].
### West Nile virus disease
- **Fungal connections:** No documented fungal connection [GAP].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link [GAP].
- **Bacterial connections:** No documented systematic bacterial co-pathogen pattern [GAP]; documented secondary bacterial infections can complicate prolonged ICU care but are not disease-linked [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented — no approved specific antiviral; management is supportive [D]. IVIG, interferon, and ribavirin have been studied but are not documented as standard therapy [D]. Documented neuroinvasive forms (encephalitis, meningitis, acute flaccid paralysis) carry documented long-term sequelae [D]. A vaccine is documented for horses but not humans per CDC [D].

Prevention is documented via mosquito avoidance and safe sexual practices, given documented sexual transmission [D].

Effective vaccines (inactivated and live-attenuated) are documented and recommended for travelers to endemic areas per CDC/WHO [D]. ### MERS
- **Fungal connections:** Rarely documented as a systematic pattern [GAP as systematic]. New at the 51% bar: documented invasive pulmonary aspergillosis as a super-infection of severe respiratory illness including MERS-CoV (review literature cites IPA cases documented as super-infections in severe influenza and MERS-CoV patients) [D]; and the MERS treatment review literature documents corticosteroid-associated aspergillosis as a recognized harm signal [D]. Caveat: case-level and indirect — no MERS-dedicated aspergillosis cohort exists, so the record supports occurrence, not incidence [D].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link [GAP].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented secondary bacterial infections, including ventilator-associated pneumonia pathogens, in severe MERS ICU cases [D]; documented nosocomial outbreaks amplified transmission in healthcare settings [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented — no approved specific therapy; management is supportive [D]. Ribavirin plus interferon was used empirically in some outbreaks without established benefit [D]. Corticosteroids were documented as used but with uncertain benefit and possible harm [D]. The documented dromedary-camel reservoir and documented camel-to-human spillover are the epidemiologic anchors [D].

### Q fever
- **Fungal connections:** No documented fungal connection [GAP].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link [GAP].
- **Bacterial connections:** The disease IS the bacterium — *Coxiella burnetii*, an obligate intracellular organism [D]. Documented severe forms include Q fever endocarditis (chronic Q fever) and documented infection in pregnancy [D]; documented phase I/phase II serology distinguishes acute from chronic disease [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per CDC: doxycycline is first-line for acute Q fever [D]. For Q fever endocarditis/chronic Q fever, documented regimens are doxycycline plus hydroxychloroquine for prolonged courses (18+ months), with documented serologic monitoring [D]. Documented alternatives in pregnancy (where doxycycline is avoided) include TMP-SMX per the literature [D].
## Part V — Group 2, rows 22–42 (Colorado tick fever through Trench fever)

### Colorado tick fever
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — No documented fungal co-pathogen, secondary fungal infection, or fungal mimic is established for Colorado tick fever in the literature. It is a tick-borne coltivirus (family *Sedoreoviridae*) illness of the Rocky Mountain region, and the documented complications are hematologic (leukopenia, thrombocytopenia) rather than infectious [D].
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] — no documented bacterial co-pathogen. Antibiotics have no role in Colorado tick fever, and the CDC documents supportive care only [D].
- **Medication choices:** Supportive care only — no antiviral exists; hydration, rest, antipyretics per CDC/StatPearls [D]. Antibiotics and antifungals are not indicated [D]. (Note: Colorado tick fever is frequently confused with Rocky Mountain spotted fever, which IS treated with doxycycline — the tick-fever virus itself has no drug target [D].)

### Rheumatic fever
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — no documented fungal connection.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — the bacterium IS the trigger: rheumatic fever is a post-infectious autoimmune sequela of Group A *Streptococcus pyogenes* pharyngitis (or skin infection), via molecular mimicry. Secondary bacterial endocarditis is a documented risk of the damaged valves, but the initiating organism is streptococcal [D].
- **Medication choices:** Acute attack: full therapeutic penicillin course to eradicate residual GAS (benzathine penicillin G single IM dose) even if cultures are negative, then long-term secondary prophylaxis [D]. Gold standard per AHA/CDC: benzathine penicillin G 1.2 million units IM every 4 weeks (every 3 weeks for high-risk), duration 5 years or to age 21 if no carditis; 10 years or to age 21 if carditis without residual disease; 10 years or to age 40 (often lifelong) with residual valvular disease [D]. Penicillin-allergic: oral penicillin V, sulfadiazine, or macrolide [D]. Arthritis/carditis symptoms: NSAIDs or aspirin; corticosteroids for severe carditis are debated and not guideline-mandated [D]. Sources: CDC Clinical Guidance for Acute Rheumatic Fever (2025); AHA prevention statement.

### Ebola virus disease
- **Fungal connections:** [H→D] — no disease-characteristic fungal co-pathogen, but a documented nosocomial yeast foothold: in the medically evacuated Frankfurt EVD case, anidulafungin-resistant *Candida parapsilosis* was recovered from multiple body sites (tracheal fluid, stool, urine) over ~3 weeks of ICU care; antifungal therapy was switched to fluconazole, successfully avoiding nosocomial candidemia (per BMC/MC case reporting, PMC4609678) [H→D]. Caveat: single severe ICU case — colonization/containment, not a characteristic EVD fungal syndrome.
- **Yeast connections:** [H→D] — same documented case basis as the fungal entry: yeast (*C. parapsilosis*, *C. albicans*) colonization at multiple body sites in the ICU-managed Frankfurt EVD patient, with antifungal management documented [H→D]. Caveat: nosocomial/ICU setting only; no general EVD–yeast association.
- **Bacterial connections:** [H→D] — secondary bacterial bloodstream infections are documented in EVD case series (patients treated empirically with broad-spectrum antibiotics per sepsis protocols), but no single bacterial co-pathogen is characteristic of the disease; treat per culture, not per disease [D]. Documented: a registered trial (NCT04250168, INRB/ITM) measuring frequency, causative pathogens, and resistance profiles of bacterial bloodstream infections among confirmed EVD patients [D]. Caveat: co-occurrence in severe/critical patients, not a disease-specific bacterial partner.
- **Medication choices:** WHO 2022 therapeutics guideline (first EVD therapeutics guideline) strongly recommends two monoclonal antibodies for Zaire ebolavirus: mAb114 (Ebanga/ansuvimab) and REGN-EB3 (Inmazeb: atoltivimab + maftivimab + odesivimab), both FDA-approved 2020 after the PALM trial [D]. Core documented position: optimized supportive care — aggressive IV fluid/electrolyte repletion, symptom management, treatment of co-infections — significantly improves survival and remains the backbone [D]. No approved therapy exists for Sudan ebolavirus [D]. Sources: WHO therapeutics guideline (Aug 2022); FDA approvals Oct/Dec 2020.

### Hepatitis D
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Medication choices:** [D] — Bucclevirtide (Hepcludex, bulevirtide-gmod), a first-in-class entry inhibitor blocking HDV/HBV hepatocyte entry via the NTCP receptor, became the first FDA-approved therapy for chronic hepatitis D in May 2026 (accelerated approval; adults without cirrhosis or with compensated cirrhosis; boxed warning on severe HDV/HBV flares after discontinuation) [D]. Before that, pegylated interferon alfa was the only option, used off-label [D]. Since HDV is a satellite virus requiring HBV, concurrent HBV-directed therapy (nucleos(t)ide analogues) is part of documented management [D]. Sources: FDA approval announcement; Healio/Pharmacy Times coverage (May 2026); WHO hepatitis guidance.

### Hepatitis E
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Medication choices:** Acute hepatitis E is self-limited: supportive care, no antiviral, per WHO [D]. ### Rotavirus gastroenteritis

### Human papillomavirus infection
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — no documented fungal connection.
- **Yeast connections:** [H→D] — in a 72-month prospective cohort of 329 pregnant women (MBRL-documented study), vaginal yeast infection (22.9%) was significantly associated with prevalent cervical HPV positivity (p=0.007) alongside mixed flora (OR 2.75) [H→D]. Caveat: association with HPV prevalence, not persistence (BV, not yeast, predicted persistence); single cohort, directionality unproven.
- **Bacterial connections:** [H→D] — bacterial vaginosis (BV) is documented as associated with uterine cervical HPV infection: Gillet et al. meta-analysis (BMC Infect Dis 2011), 12 studies, 6,372 women, pooled OR 1.43 (95% CI 1.11–1.84) [H→D]. Treatment targets the lesions: provider-applied (cryotherapy, trichloroacetic acid, surgical excision, laser) and patient-applied (imiquimod, podofilox, sinecatechins) per CDC STI Treatment Guidelines [D]. Prevention: HPV vaccines (Gardasil 9) are the documented intervention [D]. Cervical dysplasia follows ASCCP management guidelines, not antivirals [D]. Sources: CDC STI Treatment Guidelines; WHO.

### Infectious mononucleosis (Epstein-Barr)
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Yeast connections:** [H→D] — EBV-driven oral hairy leukoplakia is documented as commonly co-infected/colonized with *Candida*: dermatopathology literature (DermNet NZ) notes oral hairy leukoplakia "is often colonised by candida which can obfuscate the pathology," and oral candidiasis is a documented differential diagnosis of the lesion (CMAJ 2011 review) [H→D]. Caveat: documented as colonization/diagnostic-mimic in immunocompromised patients (especially HIV), not as a yeast co-pathogen of mono itself.
- **Bacterial connections:** [H→D] — EBV does not need antibiotics, but concurrent Group A streptococcal pharyngitis is documented in a minority of mono cases and is treated per strep guidelines when confirmed [D]. - **Medication choices:** Supportive care is the documented position: rest, hydration, analgesics; no antiviral (acyclovir and others) has shown benefit for EBV mono in trials [D]. ### Blastomycosis
- **Fungal connections:** [D] — the fungus IS the disease: *Blastomyces dermatitidis*, *B. gilchristii* (eastern North America), and *B. helicus* (western North America) are dimorphic fungi causing pulmonary and disseminated disease [D].
- **Yeast connections:** [H→D] — his grouping keeps yeast separate, and there is a documented foothold: *Blastomyces* is thermally dimorphic — it grows as a mold in the environment and converts to a yeast phase at body temperature, and the yeast form (broad-based budding yeast on microscopy) is what the pathologist sees in tissue [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] — no characteristic bacterial co-pathogen; secondary bacterial pneumonia can complicate severe pulmonary blastomycosis, but nothing disease-specific is documented.
- **Medication choices:** IDSA blastomycosis guidelines (2008, still referenced): mild-to-moderate disease — oral itraconazole 200 mg three times daily for 3 days then once or twice daily for 6–12 months (first-line) [D]. Moderately severe to severe, disseminated, CNS, immunocompromised, or pregnancy: lipid amphotericin B 3–5 mg/kg/day (or deoxycholate 0.7–1 mg/kg/day) for 1–2 weeks (4–6 weeks for CNS), then step-down to itraconazole to complete at least 12 months [D]. Azoles are contraindicated in pregnancy; amphotericin B is the drug of pregnancy [D]. Voriconazole is used for CNS disease for its CSF penetration [D]. Sources: IDSA Clinical Practice Guidelines (Clin Infect Dis 2008); AccessMedicine CMDT 2025.

### Cytomegalovirus infection
- **Fungal connections:** [H→D] — in immunocompromised hosts (especially lung and stem-cell transplant recipients), CMV pneumonia and invasive fungal pneumonia (aspergillosis, Pneumocystis) are documented co-travelers and mimics: CMV/Aspergillus co-infection is described in the transplant literature, and CMV reactivation is a documented risk factor for subsequent invasive aspergillosis [D]. ### Scrofula
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] for scrofula itself — but per his grouping the TB showing carries a note: TB's documented fungal connection is chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (aspergilloma) complicating healed TB cavities and TB–histoplasmosis co-infection in endemic areas [D]; nothing fungal is documented for the cervical lymphadenitis form itself.

### Lupus vulgaris
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — no documented fungal connection; lupus vulgaris is the most common form of cutaneous tuberculosis and its mimics include fungal infections (sporotrichosis, chromoblastomycosis) — a documented diagnostic-mimic relationship, not a co-pathogen [D].
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — none documented.

### Systemic lupus erythematosus
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — SLE is autoimmune, not infectious; no fungal agent causes it. (Documented secondary note: immunosuppressive SLE therapy raises opportunistic fungal-infection risk — a treatment consequence, not a disease connection [D].)
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — none; same treatment-consequence caveat as above (e.g., candidiasis risk on immunosuppression) [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] — no bacterial cause; infections are mimics and triggers of flares, not the disease. Mark honestly: autoimmune [D].

### Elephantiasis
- **Fungal connections:** [H→D] — elephantiasis itself is filarial, not fungal, but chronic lymphedematous limbs have a documented fungal relationship: toe-web intertrigo (dermatophyte and Candida colonization of skin folds) is a documented entry lesion that precipitates recurrent acute attacks, and fungal skin infection is part of the documented morbidity-management picture [D].
- **Yeast connections:** [H→D] — *Candida* spp. colonize macerated skin folds and toe webs of lymphedematous limbs; documented as intertrigo/entry lesions rather than the disease itself [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — recurrent streptococcal cellulitis (acute dermatolymphangioadenitis, ADLA) is the documented bacterial complication driving lymphedema progression; entry lesions admit streptococci [D]. - **Medication choices:** WHO preventive chemotherapy: triple-drug IDA (ivermectin 150 mcg/kg + DEC 6 mg/kg + albendazole 400 mg, single annual dose) where onchocerciasis is not co-endemic, or two-drug DEC + albendazole; individual treatment classically DEC 6 mg/kg/day for 12 days [D]. Doxycycline 200 mg/day for 4–6 weeks is the documented anti-*Wolbachia* macrofilaricidal regimen [D]. Established elephantiasis is managed by lymphedema care (hygiene, compression, elevation), not drugs — drugs clear microfilariae but do not reverse fibrosis [D]. Sources: WHO/PAHO lymphatic filariasis guidance.

### Trench fever
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
