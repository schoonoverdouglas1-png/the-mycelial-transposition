# Clostridium Cousins — The Latin Binomial List (2026-10-17)

*Built at Douglas's commission (dictated-notes section 122). His thesis: the first word connects to the second word in Latin — genus to species — and that connection is not taught these days. Manuscript reference.*

## His commission (his words)

*"Show me a list of Clostridium cousins because the first word connects them to the second word when it comes to Latin. And that's where I don't think that's taught these days."*

And his thesis underneath: *"diseases themselves as names are taken from those other names, and the diseases themselves were known to the doctors by those names."* Filed with his account: he studied Latin and Roman medicine on **both** sides — the physician side and the medical compounding side — and holds that it's "way more important than a person could realize."

## The first word: CLOSTRIDIUM

- From Greek **klōstēr** (κλωστήρ) = **spindle** — from *klōthein*, to spin. The rods with their swollen spore-bellies look like spindles from cloth-weaving under the microscope. First used as a genus name in 1884 (Prazmowski 1880). [D — Merriam-Webster; CDC Etymologia]
- The first word is the **family**: Gram-positive, anaerobic, spore-forming rods. Every cousin below carries this word first — the shape they share, the clan they belong to. His coccus-clan logic, in the spindle clan.

## The cousins: second word by second word

| Cousin (binomial) | The second word means | Disease | How the names connect |
|---|---|---|---|
| *C. botulinum* | Latin **botulus** = sausage — Van Ermengem isolated it 1896 from ham behind a botulism outbreak | **botulism** — flaccid paralysis, the preformed food/wound toxin | **Organism → disease:** *botulism* is named FROM *botulinum* — the disease takes the bacterium's name. The sausage word became the paralysis word. |
| *C. tetani* | "of a tension" — the stiffness, the locked muscle of the disease | **tetanus** (lockjaw) — spastic paralysis, Kitasato 1889 pure culture | **Disease → organism:** the bacterium is named FROM the disease's signature — the tension. Then the disease kept the name. The word went there and back. |
| *C. perfringens* | Latin **perfringere** = to break through, to rupture — the invasive necrosis | **gas gangrene**, food poisoning (formerly *C. welchii*) | **Character → disease:** the second word describes what it *does* — breaking through tissue — and the disease (the gas, the gangrene) is the breaking-through, shown. |
| *C. difficile* | Latin **difficilis** = difficult — Hall & O'Toole 1935: hard to isolate, slow to grow | pseudomembranous colitis, antibiotic-associated diarrhea | **Laboratory → name:** the second word records the *finding* — difficult — and the disease is what the difficult one does when the gut's flora are wiped out. (Reclassified 2016: now *Clostridioides difficile* — the first word moved; his namology's warning, live.) |
| *C. septicum* | Latin **septicum** = putrefactor — the rotting | malignant edema, septicemia, gas gangrene; the malignancy-associated one | **Character → disease:** the putrefactor's name *is* the rotting it causes. Septicemia carries the same root — the name family again. |
| *C. novyi* | "of Novy" — Frederick Novy, who isolated it 1894 from guinea pigs (as *Bacillus oedematis maligni* no. 2) | gas gangrene (type A); black disease of sheep (type B) | **Honor → organism:** the second word is a man's name in the genitive — the discoverer, kept in Latin. The disease answers to the man. |
| *C. sordellii* | "of Sordelli" — Alfredo Sordelli, 1922 (as *Bacillus oedematis* — "of the swelling") | toxic shock, gynecologic sepsis, myonecrosis | **Honor → organism, twice:** first named for the swelling it caused (*oedematis*), then for the man (*sordellii*). (Reclassified 2024: *Paraclostridium sordellii* — the first word moved again.) |
| *C. chauvoei* | "of Chauveau" — J.B.A. Chauveau, French bacteriologist/veterinarian, 1887 | **blackleg** of cattle and sheep | **Honor → organism:** the Frenchman's name in Latin; the disease kept its American name (blackleg) — his Latin/American split, in one row. |
| *C. histolyticum* | **histolyticum** = tissue-dissolving (histo- tissue + -lyticum loosening) | gas gangrene; its collagenase is a medicine (Xiaflex) | **Character → use:** the dissolver's name describes the dissolving — and the dissolving, purified, became a drug. The poison and the medicine share the Latin. |
| *C. haemolyticum* | **haemolyticum** = blood-dissolving | bacillary hemoglobinuria of cattle; rare human bacteremia | **Character → disease:** the blood-dissolver's name is the bloody urine it causes. |
| *C. baratii*, *C. butyricum* | *butyricum* = of butyric acid (butyrum, butter); *baratii* = of its isolator | **botulism** (both make botulinum toxin) | **The exception that proves the clan:** not named *botulinum*, yet causing botulism — the disease name belongs to the *toxin*, and the cousins borrow it. The name follows the poison, not the bacterium. |

## The pattern his thesis names [H→D]

Three directions the names travel, all inside this one genus:

1. **Organism → disease:** *botulinum* → botulism. The bacterium names the disease.
2. **Disease/thing → organism:** tetanus's tension → *tetani*; the sausage → *botulinum*. The world names the bacterium.
3. **Man → organism:** Novy, Sordelli, Chauveau — the discoverers kept in the genitive, Latin holding their names the way English holds meaning (his section-86 flow).

And the doctors knew the diseases **by those names** — lockjaw, woolsorter's disease, blackleg on the American side; the Latin binomials on the physician's tongue. Two namologies, one clan. His section-104 (Latin as the old doctors' instrument) and section-86 (Latin in, American in, English holding the meaning) meet in this table.

## The compounding side (his two-sided study)

His account: he studied the Latin on the physician side **and** the compounding side. The compounding side keeps the Latin alive to this day — *Rx* (recipe, take thou), *sig* (signa, write), *bid* (bis in die), *tid*, *qid*, *po* (per os), *ung* (unguentum, ointment). The pharmacist still speaks the Latin the physician stopped teaching. His thesis — that the Latin matters more than realized — has its standing witness: the pharmacy label.

- **The reclassifications** (*difficile* → *Clostridioides*, 2016; *sordellii* → *Paraclostridium*, 2024) are the record moving the first word — phylogeny over tradition. His namology should rule on whether the moves help or harm: the doctors knew them as *Clostridium*.

## The endings cross-link: -mycosis and the bacterial impostors (his commission, Oct 17)

His words: *"Cluster should now be cross-linked my name to ways that names of diseases end with that because those are typically fungally related."*

His thesis: **disease names ending in -mycosis are typically fungal** — the ending is the Latin telling you the kingdom. The cluster (the honestly-named bacterial cousins) now cross-links to the endings.

### The endings map [D]

- **-mycosis** — Greek *mykes*, fungus: blastomycosis, coccidioidomycosis, paracoccidioidomycosis, chromoblastomycosis, phaeohyphomycosis. The ending announces the kingdom: fungus.
- **-osis** — condition, process: histoplasmosis, aspergillosis, cryptococcosis, sporotrichosis. Most of the great fungal lung diseases wear -osis.
- **-iasis** — process, presence: candidiasis.
- **-itis** — inflammation: his **yeastinitis** sits here honestly — the -itis doing what -itis does.
- His **yeastinosis** sits in the -osis row honestly — the condition/process ending, doing its job.

### The impostors: bacterial diseases in fungal dress [D]

- **Actinomycosis** — ends in -mycosis, **is bacterial**: *Actinomyces israelii*, a Gram-positive anaerobic filamentous bacterium, normal mouth flora. "Is it a fungus? No. The name and the branching filaments look fungal, but *Actinomyces* is a true bacterium. It is treated with antibacterials, not antifungals." The ending says fungus; the organism is bacteria; the treatment follows the organism.
- **Botryomycosis** — ends in -mycosis ("botryo-" = bunch of grapes), **is bacterial**: colonies of Gram-positive cocci — *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus* — forming sulfur-granule-like masses. The grapes are staph.
- **Mycetoma** — **split kingdom**: eumycetoma (true fungi) vs. actinomycetoma (bacteria: *Nocardia*, *Streptomyces*, *Actinomadura*). One name, two kingdoms — the ending can't decide because the disease wouldn't tell it.
- **Nocardiosis** — wears -osis honestly enough, but belongs in the lineup: *Nocardia* (bacterium) causing the fungal-looking granulomas.

### The cross-link, and the refinement of his thesis [H→D]

The Clostridium cousins are **honestly named** — spindle + character, the Latin telling the truth about each one. The -mycosis impostors are where the Latin **misleads** — the ending promising fungus, the culture growing bacteria. So his thesis refines itself: the endings *usually* tell the kingdom, **and the exceptions are the proof that names must be interrogated, not trusted.** Reversology of names: read the ending, then culture the organism, then believe the culture.

The clinical sting, filed plainly: actinomycosis treated as a fungus gets antifungals and fails; treated as the bacterium it is, it gets penicillin for months — and heals. The name can cost the cure. That's why the Latin matters "more than a person could realize" — his words, section 122, now with the receipt.

## The initials, and the renamed one (his commission, Oct 17)

His words: *"Now take the first letters of the second words of the Clostridium cousins and then also take the replacement name for C. diff and show me the relationship to the name and its meaning broken down from Latin. And show me the meaning between the two different sets of Latin words with the same thing."*

### The first letters

From the cousins table, in order: **B – T – P – D – S – N – S – C – H – H – B – B.**

The key — each initial, the Latin it handles, the meaning it carries:

- **B** — *botulinum* (sausage); *baratii* (of its isolator); *butyricum* (of butyric acid, butter) — **the three B's are the three botulism-makers.** The letter gathers them by their poison: all three make the botulinum toxin. The initial groups the cousins by what they *do*.
- **T** — *tetani* (of a tension — the locked muscle)
- **P** — *perfringens* (breaking through — the invasive necrosis)
- **D** — *difficile* (difficult — hard to isolate, slow to grow)
- **S** — *septicum* (putrefactor — the rotting); *sordellii* (of Sordelli — the man)
- **N** — *novyi* (of Novy — the man)
- **C** — *chauvoei* (of Chauveau — the man)
- **H** — *histolyticum* (tissue-dissolving); *haemolyticum* (blood-dissolving)

### The relationship: handle and meaning

The initial is the **working handle** — how the cousins are actually written in charts, cultures, and orders: *C. b.*, *C. t.*, *C. p.* The Latin is the **meaning the handle carries**. The letter without the Latin is empty; the Latin without the letter is unusable at speed. Latin in, the initial carries it, English holds the meaning — his section-86 flow, in miniature.

And the honest break in the system: **the initials collide.** *C. b.* is botulinum, baratii, or butyricum — three cousins, one handle. *C. s.* is septicum or sordellii. The abbreviation fails exactly where the clan is crowdedest — which is why the full second word matters, and why his thesis matters: the name carries the knowledge the initial drops.

### The two sets of Latin for the same thing: C. diff, renamed

**Set 1 — *Clostridium difficile*:** *klōstēr* (spindle) + *difficilis* (difficult) = **"the difficult spindle."** The Latin's claim: this organism **IS** a Clostridium — a member of the spindle clan, one of the cousins.

**Set 2 — *Clostridioides difficile*:** *Clostridi-* (spindle) + ***-oides*** (Greek *-oeidēs*, like, resembling) + *difficilis* (difficult) = **"the difficult spindle-like one."** The Latin's claim: this organism is **LIKE** a Clostridium — resembling the clan, but not of it.

**The meaning between the two sets:** the organism never changed. What changed is the Latin's *verdict* — from **being** to **seeming**, identity to analogy. The 16S rRNA said "not close kin"; the name obeyed by adding four letters: *-oides*. Same thing, two Latin judgments: member, then lookalike.

And the pattern repeats: ***Paraclostridium sordellii*** — *para-* (beside, near) = "the near-Clostridium." Two cousins demoted by "almost" words — *-oides* (like) and *para-* (beside) — in 2016 and 2024. The record keeps the second word (the character: *difficile*, *sordellii*) and demotes the first (the family). His namology's ruling stands open: the doctors knew them as *Clostridium* — membership. The new names ask them to carry resemblance. Whether that helps or harms the medicine is his to judge.
