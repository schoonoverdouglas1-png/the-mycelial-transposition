# Research Note — ATE Nanoparticles and the Word "Antifungal Antibiotic"

**Filed:** 2026-10-06 · For local peer review alongside *The Mycelial Transposition* manuscript
**Source:** Secondary writeup in scienmag of a peer-reviewed study in *Materials Today Bio*, 41, Article 103707 (2026); DOI 10.1016/j.mtbio.2026.103707
**Link:** https://scienmag.com/smart-nanoparticles-unleash-antifungal-drug-only-where-infection-burns/

## The study

- Chen, Chen, Wang, Dai, Jin, Jia & Ye built ATE nanoparticles: amphotericin B (AmB) plus epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG, the green-tea catechin), crosslinked into a boronate-ester carrier that releases its cargo in acidic, reactive-oxygen-rich tissue — i.e., where the infected inflammation "burns."
- The carrier's exposed boronic-acid groups bind the cis-diol-rich polysaccharides (β-glucans, mannans) carpeting fungal cell walls, so particles accumulated on *Candida albicans* surfaces while free AmB showed almost no signal.
- In vitro MIC against *C. albicans*: 0.16 µg/mL in AmB equivalents — eightfold lower than free AmB at 1.28. >99% kill in 4 hours at 2 µg/mL; also dismantled biofilms (77% biomass reduction in immature biofilms, ~80% biomass eradication in mature two-day biofilms).
- In a murine *C. albicans* keratitis model over 15 days, treated corneas recovered transparency with a clinical score of 6 vs. 14 in untreated controls and 8 for AmBisome (the clinical liposomal AmB formulation); corneal thickness fell to 93.6 µm vs. 185.9 µm in controls. Parallel results in an infected skin-wound model.
- The EGCG half is real work, not garnish: particles scavenged ROS, suppressed TNF-α/IL-1β/IL-6 and upregulated IL-10 in macrophages, and at 8 µg/mL AmB-equivalent kept cell viability above 80% where free AmB drove it below 60%.

## The nomenclature hook

- Read the framing: the writeup calls amphotericin B "**the most potent antifungal antibiotic in the clinic**" and "**the polyene macrocyclic antibiotic**." In ordinary biomedical prose, the word "antibiotic" already crosses the bacteria/fungus line — a drug used against fungi is called an antibiotic without anyone blinking.
- This is live ammunition for the manuscript's opening terrain: your reclassification does not have to fight the language. Clinical usage already treats "antibiotic" as wider than "antibacterial." The rename work then becomes formalizing what practitioners already do — drawing the map where the footpath already is.
- It also rhymes with the MOB-015 note's route argument: here too, the innovation is *delivery context* (release gated on the infected microenvironment), not a new molecule. Classification by route and context again does the work.

## Honest caveats (do not drop these)

1. **Secondary source.** The note is built from the scienmag summary, not the full *Materials Today Bio* paper. Before citation, the paper's methods need a direct read — especially nanoparticle characterization, evaluable-animal definitions, and whether the AmBisome comparison was fair.
2. **Preclinical only.** In vitro plus two mouse models. The authors themselves flag that this remains preclinical; "superior to AmBisome" in mice is not a clinical claim.
3. **Amphotericin B is bacterial-derived** (from *Streptomyces nodosus*), not fungal-derived like the penicillins your reclassification centers on. The fit here is the *nomenclature* argument — how the word "antibiotic" is actually used — not the origin claim. Do not present it as a fungal-origin data point.
4. The dual-trigger release (acidic pH + ROS) is elegant in vitro; in vivo relevance at clinical ocular-surface conditions still needs human data.
