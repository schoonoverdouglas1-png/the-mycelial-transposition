## Testing the thesis three ways

### 1. Infection genuinely reshuffles the body's cholesterol [D]

The acute-phase response to infection and inflammation alters lipid metabolism in a documented, reproducible pattern: HDL-cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, and total cholesterol fall; triglycerides rise. The pattern is consistent across reviews of the acute-phase reaction and sepsis cohorts (Feingold & Grunfeld, PMC2842157; PMC5625127; PMC3322446).

Mechanisms documented: reduced liver ApoA-I secretion, impaired reverse cholesterol transport, cholesterol delivery redirected to immune cells, HDL remodeled into pro-inflammatory "acute-phase HDL" (serum amyloid A displacing apolipoproteins). The cholesterol moves — it does not vanish. His "liquids of the body, the tissue of the body, or a combination of both" maps onto the documented picture: plasma levels drop while cholesterol accumulates in tissues and immune cells.

A clinical corollary, documented: in sepsis, the deeper the hypocholesterolemia, the worse the severity — cholesterol levels run inversely to procalcitonin and pro-inflammatory cytokines. Low cholesterol as a readout of how far the infection has gone. [D]

### 2. The tuberculosis pillar touches cholesterol directly — and has since 1911 [D]

Hypocholesterolemia was first reported in 1911, when Chauffard and coworkers described decreased cholesterol in patients in "very bad general condition" during the febrile phase of **tuberculosis** (PMC374382). The TB–cholesterol line is 115 years old.

Modern confirmation: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* uses host cholesterol as a carbon source inside macrophages. Infection increases macrophage LDL/cholesterol uptake, enhances de novo cholesterol synthesis, blocks cholesterol-ester degradation, and suppresses cholesterol efflux transporters — driving cholesterol accumulation, lipid droplets, and foam-cell formation, which support persistent infection and granuloma formation (PMC3292763). New drug candidates (e.g., GSK2556286) aim to starve Mtb by blocking its cholesterol catabolism.

This is his TB pillar intersecting his cholesterol thesis on documented ground — the very disease his book treats as foundational has been linked to cholesterol since before antibiotics existed.

### 3. The honest seam [D]

Cholesterol is also made by the liver and absorbed from diet — infection is not the whole story of where it comes from. And one documented counter-line exists inside the TB literature itself: a Journal of Bacteriology paper argues cholesterol is *not* an essential nutritional source for Mtb during infection (mutants unable to catabolize cholesterol still grow in macrophages and guinea pigs) — journals.asm.org. So the TB–cholesterol relationship is real but contested in its details, not settled in one direction.

## Assessment

The thesis holds at the 51% bar: infection measurably moves cholesterol between body fluids and tissues [D], tuberculosis has done so on the record since 1911 [D], and severity tracks the shift [D]. What remains [H] is the strong reading — cholesterol as *the* byproduct of infection rather than one of several; and [GAP] where the record is silent — whether his six-track framework (bacterial/fungal/yeast elements) predicts different cholesterol signatures per element.

## Simply put (his dual register, 6th–8th grade)

When the body fights an infection, cholesterol doesn't just sit still — it moves. Blood levels drop, and cholesterol piles up in tissues and in the immune cells doing the fighting. Scientists first noticed this in tuberculosis patients back in 1911, and the tuberculosis germ itself actually feeds on cholesterol while hiding inside immune cells.
