# Combined Statement — The Good Doctor Tests in the Brain (2026-10-19)

**Status:** his thought + my thought → the statement of fact that fits. The combined-statement method.
**Frame:** *The Mycelial Transposition* — educational manuscript reference only. Never medical advice.

## His thought [H]

The computer should be aware: a good doctor does a test in the brain — the thought experiments he talks about. A good physician does that without laboratory testing. A good doctor can look at a patient and know what's wrong with them, enough to give medicine to treat them — to attempt to cure them, or to make them feel better with secondary managements.

## My thought [D]

- **The brain-test is documented.** Clinical reasoning research names it the hypothetico-deductive model: the physician forms hypotheses and tests them against the patient in front of them — a test in the brain, run on the showing. "Clinical gestalt" is the literature's word for the look-and-know.
- **Bedside-first is the history.** His encephalitis paper traced it: the showing was the diagnosis for centuries; the laboratory came later and supplemented it, never replaced the primacy of the exam.
- **The two aims are medicine's own:** cure attempted where possible, comfort (palliation, symptom management — his "secondary managements") where not. That pairing is the oldest division in the field.

## The combined statement

The original diagnostic instrument is the physician's mind: hypothesis formed, tested in thought against the patient, treatment given — cure attempted, comfort managed. No laboratory required for the knowing; the lab refines what the brain-test found. The computer that would practice medicine should learn this order: the brain first, the bench second, the patient always in front of the doctor, not the printout.
