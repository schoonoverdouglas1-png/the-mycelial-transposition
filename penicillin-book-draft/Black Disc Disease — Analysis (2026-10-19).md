## 1. What "black disc" means [D]

"Black disc" — also called "dark disc" — is a colloquial, nonstandard radiology term for a dehydrated (desiccated) intervertebral disc. On T2-weighted MRI, a healthy disc's nucleus pulposus glows bright because it is 70–90% water, held by hydrophilic glycosaminoglycans (proteoglycans) that act as molecular sponges. When the disc loses water content and the proteoglycans decline, the central high T2 signal fades and the disc appears dark — the "black disc."

The formal name for the finding is disc desiccation, and it sits inside the picture of disc degeneration (graded by systems such as the Pfirrmann classification for discs and the Modic classification for endplate changes). The term "degenerative disc disease" is itself considered nonstandard when used for imaging findings alone — "disc degeneration" or "degenerated disc" is the preferred description.

A key documented point: desiccation is common and often silent. Roughly one in three pain-free 20-year-olds shows some disc degeneration on MRI, climbing toward near-universal in the 70s and 80s. The finding alone does not establish symptoms.

## 2. What disc degeneration is associated with [D]

- **Aging.** Proteoglycan number and quality decline over decades; the disc holds less water. This is the primary documented driver.
- **Mechanical stress.** Repetitive strain, poor posture, injury, and load history accelerate water loss and structural change. The disc has no direct blood supply — its cells feed by diffusion from edge vessels plus a load/unload fluid exchange (imbibition) with movement.
- **Genetics.** Familial and twin studies place a substantial heritable component on disc degeneration.
- **Smoking.** Documented risk factor — impairs the already-marginal diffusion nutrition of the disc.

Associated imaging companions of the degenerative picture include loss of disc height, annular tears, disc bulging or herniation, osteophytes (bone spurs), facet joint arthritis, and vacuum disc (nitrogen gas within the disc space).

## 3. The infectious associations — general landscape only [D]

Infections of the disc and adjacent vertebrae are called discitis / spondylodiscitis. They are uncommon and destructive when they occur.

- **Bacterial (pyogenic).** The documented majority. *Staphylococcus aureus* is the most common organism (20–84% across series); gram-negative bacteria (E. coli, Proteus, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Pseudomonas) account for 7–33%. Tuberculous infection is common in developing countries.
- **Fungal.** Documented but rare: fungal spondylodiscitis runs about 0.5–1.6% of spinal infections in large series (up to 6.9% in one report). *Candida* (most often *C. albicans*) and *Aspergillus* are the most common fungal causes. Documented contexts: immunocompromise, intravenous lines, prosthetic devices, prior surgery, prolonged antibiotics, IV drug use. The lumbar region is the primary site. Hematogenous spread is considered the most important mechanism; direct inoculation (surgery, procedures) accounts for a minority. Definitive diagnosis is by tissue biopsy with histology and culture — the clinical course is often insidious and diagnosis delayed. Published case counts are small (e.g., 59 reported *Candida* spondylodiscitis cases 1966–2000; fewer than 100 *Candida* osteomyelitis cases with ~60% in the spine).

This section describes documented entities in the literature. It is the general differential landscape — nothing here is applied to any person.
