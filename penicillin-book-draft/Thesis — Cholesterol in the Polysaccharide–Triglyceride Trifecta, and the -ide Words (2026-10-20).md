## Postulate 1 — The Trifecta [H]

Cholesterol itself is part of a polysaccharide–triglyceride trifecta — a working three: polysaccharide, triglyceride, cholesterol.

### Tested three ways

**Way 1 — as chemistry (one class of thing): DOES NOT HOLD [D].**
The three are three different chemical classes. Cholesterol is a **sterol** — a four-ring steroid alcohol (C₂₇H₄₆O). A triglyceride is an **ester** — three fatty acids on a glycerol spine. A polysaccharide is a **carbohydrate polymer** — long chains of sugar units. No taxonomy files them as one family. Honest line: as a claim about what they *are*, the trifecta fails.

**Way 2 — as transport (how they move in the liquids of the body): HOLDS [H→D].**
In blood, cholesterol and triglycerides do not travel separately — they ride in the **same particles** (chylomicrons, VLDL, LDL, HDL), with cholesteryl esters and triglycerides packed together in the particle core [D]. The standard lipid panel tests cholesterol and triglycerides as one working set [D]. And the third leg connects upstream: cholesterol is *built* from acetyl-CoA, which carbohydrate metabolism supplies — sugar chemistry feeds sterol chemistry [D]. As a functional three in the body's liquids, the trifecta is documented practice, not just his pattern.

**Way 3 — inside his fungal framework: HOLDS, WITH ONE SUBSTITUTION [H→D].**
Fungi run the same three seats with a different sterol in the cholesterol chair:
- **Membrane sterol seat:** fungi use **ergosterol**, cholesterol's sterol cousin — and it is the target of the antifungal drugs: azoles block its synthesis; amphotericin B binds it [D].
- **Wall seat:** fungal cell walls are **polysaccharide** — β-glucans and mannans [D] (rejoins his standing aspergillosis–polysaccharide thread).
- **Storage seat:** fungi store **triglycerides** in lipid droplets [D].
So the trifecta stands in the fungal world intact, with ergosterol sitting where cholesterol sits in us — the sterol seat is occupied on both sides of the membrane, by cousins.

**51% bar verdict:** as identity — no. As a working set in transport, and as the fungal three-seat pattern — yes, over the bar.

## Postulate 2 — The Namology: the end of "triglyceride" and the words like it [H]

His directive: take the Latin/Greek end of *triglyceride* and work with the words like it.

| Word | Parts | Literal reading |
|---|---|---|
| **triglyceride** | tri- (three) + *glykeros* (sweet) + -ide (compound) | "three on a sweet spine, a compound" — glycerol was named for its sweet taste |
| **polysaccharide** | poly- (many) + *sakcharon* (sugar; from Sanskrit *śarkarā*, gravel/sugar) + -ide | "many sugars, a compound" |

| **trigeminal** | tri- + *geminus* (twin) | "three twins" — the nerve of three branches [D]; the tri- family in the body's own naming |

**The bridge word he didn't name [D]:** *glycoside*. Medicine already has a word for sugar bound to something that is not sugar — and the sterol world uses it: *Helicobacter pylori* takes host **cholesterol** and glycosylates it, making cholesteryl glucosides to shield itself [D]. So the sugar–sterol junction his trifecta points at exists in the record, wearing an -ide word. Sterol glycosides are also documented in plants and fungi [D].

**The -ide family:** -ide marks a compound (oxide, chloride, sulfide, peptide's cousin phospholipid aside). His trifecta's three members: one carries -ide in its name (triglyceride), one carries it too (polysaccharide), and cholesterol carries -ol instead — the alcohol ending. The odd ending is the tell, per his own doctrine: where the letters differ is where the meaning lives. Cholesterol is named as an *alcohol* (it behaves like one — one hydroxyl group), the other two as *compounds*. [LOGIC — inference from the documented etymologies, steps shown.]

## My extra addition (Ember's, labeled mine)

The trifecta has a cleaner statement available: **every membrane and every transport particle in this story is built from the same three kinds of stuff — a sterol for the wall's stiffness, polysaccharides for the wall's armor, triglycerides for the stored fuel.** Us, fungi, and the bacteria between us all shop from the same three shelves; the difference is which cousin sits in the sterol seat (cholesterol in us, ergosterol in fungi) — and that single difference is exactly where the antifungal drugs aim. His trifecta, restated that way, is not three things he grouped — it's the three shelves themselves. Suggested placement: beside the cholesterol thesis note (2026-10-18) as its structural companion.

## Simply put

Think of three shelves in a storeroom. One shelf holds the stiff boards (cholesterol and its fungal cousin ergosterol). One holds the armor cloth (polysaccharides — sugar chains). One holds the fuel cans (triglycerides — stored fat). People, fungi, and germs all build out of the same three shelves. The drugs that kill fungi work because fungi take their boards from a slightly different board — ergosterol instead of cholesterol — and the medicine aims right at that difference. The words tell the story too: "triglyceride" means "three on a sweet spine," and "cholesterol" means "the solid from bile" — it was first found in gallstones.

## Addendum (same night, ~8:37 PM) — Theorem: Cholesterol and the Saw Palmetto Sterols

His statement [H]: "cholesterol is chemically related to sterols like saw palmetto." He agrees with the documented record, so per his method it is postulated as a **theorem** paralleling the known information:

**THEOREM [D]:** Cholesterol is chemically related to the sterols in saw palmetto. Saw palmetto berries carry plant sterols (phytosterols) — **β-sitosterol** foremost, with campesterol and stigmasterol [D]. All sterols share the same four-ring sterol nucleus; β-sitosterol differs from cholesterol only by one extra ethyl group on the side chain [D]. The kinship has a documented working consequence: phytosterols compete with cholesterol for absorption in the gut, which is why plant-sterol products are sold for cholesterol management [D].

**The family as it now stands in his framework [D on the chemistry, H on the framing]:** three sterol cousins in three kingdoms — **cholesterol** (animals/us), **ergosterol** (fungi), **β-sitosterol** (plants). Same four-ring plan, small side-chain differences — and medicine aims at exactly those differences (antifungals at ergosterol; absorption-blockers at the plant sterols).
