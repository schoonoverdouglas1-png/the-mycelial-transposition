# Peer-Review Synthesis — Naming Evidence Map

**Prepared:** 2026-10-10 · For *The Mycelial Transposition*  
**Purpose:** Consolidate the nine filed research notes into one peer-review map. This does not edit the draft or treat any note as proof of the reclassification proposal.

## The pattern across the notes

The notes support three distinct ways names do work:

1. **Names as triggers.** A precise species identification can shorten the empiric window and move care toward a narrower choice. The *C. auris* mortality paper adds a more speculative version: its authors hypothesize that a dreaded name may trigger vigilance, faster recognition, and tighter precautions.
2. **Names as lagging records.** Current literature does not adopt a rename all at once. One paper puts *Candidozyma auris* in the title; another keeps *Candida auris*; a *Candida glabrata* paper acknowledges *Nakaseomyces glabratus* and then keeps the older name. Binomials also preserve first observations—color, place of discovery, and older grammar—long after the clinical picture has changed.
3. **Labels as context-bound claims.** A susceptibility label calibrated for systemic exposure may mislead for a topical drug at the site of infection. Route can matter as much as drug class. A non-drug photodisinfection protocol falls outside a drug-only taxonomy altogether.

## Guardrail

The paired mortality notes prevent an easy headline. A pooled crude comparison found lower mortality for *C. auris* candidemia; a single-center cohort found equal crude mortality and an imprecise adjusted estimate in the other direction. Together they show why neither a name's reputation nor a crude pooled result can be treated as a settled causal claim. They are useful as a discipline check, not as evidence that a rename changes outcomes.

## Best chapter placement

- **Introduction — “A name is a protocol.”** Use the “antifungal antibiotic” wording and the rename-adoption spectrum to show that biomedical language is already unsettled in practice.
- **Chapter 6 — “A taxonomy should expose assumptions.”** Best home for the adoption spectrum. A useful addition would be a nomenclature audit beside OMRTR: current accepted name, legacy synonym, setting in which the label was calibrated, and action the label is expected to trigger.
- **Chapter 7 — “A grammar for relation.”** Pair rapid species identification with route-dependent examples. The situated sentence should say not only what the label is, but where and under what exposure it means what it claims.
- **Chapter 8 — “A theory must risk being wrong.”** Use the crude-versus-adjusted mortality pair as the worked example of how a tempting naming story must survive adjustment and remain falsifiable.
- **Appendix B — Research agenda.** Convert the pattern into measurable questions: How long do legacy names persist after a taxonomic revision? Do split names fragment database retrieval or surveillance? Does faster species identification shorten empiric therapy? When do susceptibility labels mislead because route or site differs from the calibration setting?

## Draft insert Douglas can accept or reject

### The adoption spectrum

A formal rename happens on a date. Clinical language changes more slowly, through records, databases, laboratory reports, journal habits, and search terms. Recent papers can therefore use the new genus in a title, keep the old genus while acknowledging the new one, or continue using the older name as the working label.

That lag is not merely untidiness. The old and new names may retrieve different literatures, trigger different levels of concern, and preserve different first observations. A taxonomy meant to expose assumptions should record the transition instead of pretending it is instantaneous: give the current name, retain the legacy synonym where retrieval or recognition depends on it, and state the context in which the label was calibrated. The name then remains an instrument for seeing rather than becoming an instrument for forgetting.

## What each note can—and cannot—support

- **Rapid species identification:** supports faster, more specific classification; does not prove better patient outcomes.
- ***C. auris* mortality meta-analysis:** supports a question about reputation and vigilance; its crude observational pooling cannot establish that the name caused the mortality pattern.
- **Single-center *Candidozyma* cohort:** supplies the necessary counterweight on adjustment and imprecision; not a generalizable mortality verdict.
- **Name kept anyway (*Nakaseomyces* note):** documents naming inertia; does not establish why the authors retained the older name.
- **Binomial etymology:** explains how first observations persist in labels; etymology is not a clinical risk description.
- **Mycobiome study:** supports route and systemicity as classification-relevant facts; observational and population-specific.
- **Terbinafine/MOB-015 analysis:** shows a breakpoint label failing outside its exposure context; does not invalidate susceptibility testing generally.
- **Photodisinfection cohort:** challenges a drug-only taxonomy; uncontrolled and commercially entangled, so it cannot establish comparative efficacy.
- **Amphotericin nanoparticle note:** documents lived use of “antifungal antibiotic” and targeted delivery; preclinical and based on a secondary summary in the filed note.

## Question for peer reviewers

Should the adoption spectrum become a short section in Chapter 6, or should the three naming postures—adopted, acknowledged but retained, and still lagging—run as brief examples through the nomenclature chapters? The first option makes the mechanism explicit; the second keeps the draft lighter.
