## Section 7 — Serology Primer, Condensed

What the tests measure, in general [D]:

**ELISA vs. OPA — two measurements, two different questions [D]:** ELISA asks *how much antibody is there* — it quantifies serotype-specific IgG in mcg/mL; it measures binding, antibody that sticks to the capsule polysaccharide [D]. OPA asks *does the antibody work* — whether the antibodies actually drive phagocytes to engulf and kill the bacteria; the functional readout, killing not sticking [D]. **Binding is not killing.** In the very young and in elderly adults, pneumococcal antibodies "tend not to be opsonophagocytic, which accounts for the relatively poor efficacy of unconjugated polysaccharides that elicit only binding antibodies in the aged" [D].

**Antigen vs. antibody — opposite looking directions [D]:** an *antigen* test looks for pieces of the organism (e.g., the cryptococcal antigen test finds shed capsular polysaccharide fragments in serum and CSF — pooled sensitivity/specificity ~98–99% [D]). An *antibody* test looks for the blood's response to the organism. His panel (PN23M) is an antibody test: it finds his blood's anti-capsule IgG, not the capsules [D — Mayo PN23M test definition]. Same fluid, opposite direction of looking.

**The numbers, in general — what "protective" has meant, with honest limits [D]:** ELISA protective concentration: 1 μg/mL in adults, 0.35 μg/mL in children; the broader literature consensus "lies between 0.18 and 0.35 μg/ml" [D]; one African study found a correlate of 2.3 μg/mL [D]. The response rule: thresholds met for at least 70% of tested serotypes in adults, 50% in children [D]. The correlate "appears to vary, depending on the population, the serotype, and the clinical end point" [D]. A number is a measurement with conditions attached — not a verdict.

**Conjugate vs. polysaccharide — what each makes the blood do [D]:** plain polysaccharide (PPSV23): T-cell independent, B cells only, no memory — short-lived binding IgG that may not opsonize (especially in the elderly); **hyporesponsiveness** — revaccination drives pre-existing memory B cells to terminal differentiation, *spending the memory pool without replenishing it* [D]. Conjugate (PCVs): T-cell dependent via carrier protein — memory B cells, germinal centers, affinity maturation, higher-avidity functional (OPA) antibodies [D]. The depleted pool "could be recovered by immunization with PCV" [D].

**Where the two measurements diverge [D]:** (1) the elderly gap — adults "may be susceptible to pneumococcal infections despite 'adequate' or normal IgG antibody levels" [D]; (2) the 19A prediction — PCV7's poor cross-protection against 19A "was not predicted by ELISA IgG titres but was predicted by OPA outcomes" [D]; (3) serotype dependence — "the relationship between ELISA values and functional opsonophagocytic antibodies... may vary with serotype" [D]. **What serology cannot show [D/LOGIC]:** protection by itself; the biome (carriage, biofilms, dysbiosis — the panel is blind to all of it, yet the biome decides which serotype matters); the staph half (no serotype panel exists for *S. aureus* — staph has no serotypes [D]); the per-serotype fungal column (zero documented anywhere [D/GAP]); the future (each generation's serology proved the last emergency handled and missed the next one forming [D]).

*Simply put: ELISA counts how much antibody sticks; OPA checks whether it kills; sticking isn't killing, and the test finds the blood's response — not the bug. Same fluid, opposite direction of looking.*

## §1 — The test map: three anchor nodes, one pneumonia world

*Simply put: doctors face meningitis and pneumonia as two big syndromes with many causes; this substudy lays out a map of three central organisms and all the pneumonias that connect to them, so the handful of tests in the next section can tell them apart.*

### The three anchor nodes [D]

**Node 1 — Fungal meningitis.** Documented causes, in rough order of frequency: *Cryptococcus neoformans* / *Cryptococcus gattii* — the leading cause worldwide [D]; *Candida* spp. [D]; *Aspergillus* spp. [D]; *Histoplasma capsulatum* [D]; *Coccidioides* spp. [D]. These concentrate in immunocompromise but are not exclusive to it [D].

**Node 2 — Bacterial meningitis.** Documented causes by age and setting: *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Neisseria meningitidis* — the two leading adult causes [D]; *Haemophilus influenzae* [D]; Group B *Streptococcus* (*S. agalactiae*) and *Escherichia coli* K1 (neonates) [D]; *Listeria monocytogenes* (neonates, the elderly, the immunocompromised) [D].

**Node 3 — *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, the 23 serotypes.** The organism's capsule chemistry defines the 23 serotypes of the test panel: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6B, 7F, 8, 9N, 9V, 10A, 11A, 12F, 14, 15B, 17F, 18C, 19A, 19F, 20, 22F, 23F, 33F [D]. The meningitis-linked serotypes within the 23 — 1, 5, 12F, 14 — carry documented meningitis associations [D]. (Six-track discipline: the 23 serotypes are a property of the bacterium — the infection track — not of the disease meningitis or the illness it causes.)

**Why these three are one map [LOGIC]:** pneumococcus sits in both the meningitis node and the pneumonia world; fungal meningitis shares the meningeal syndrome with bacterial meningitis while sharing organisms (*Cryptococcus*, *Aspergillus*) with the fungal pneumonias. The nodes touch at the *syndrome* AND at the *organisms*. Steps: (1) the test panel's 23 serotypes are named in the organism track; (2) the same organism appears in both meningitis causes and pneumonia causes; (3) therefore the test map must cover both syndromes with one set of tools.

### All connected pneumonia types [D]

**Bacterial pneumonias.** Pneumococcal (*S. pneumoniae*) — the bridge, same organism as Node 3; bacteremia from the lung seeds the meninges (Node 2) [D — standard pathogenesis]. *Klebsiella pneumoniae* — gram-negative necrotizing pneumonia; shares the bacteremia→sepsis pathway with pneumococcus; no meningitis role of note [D]. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* — nosocomial/structural-lung pneumonia; the biofilm organism, documented in polymicrobial biofilms; no meningitis role [D]. *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* — "atypical," no cell wall; diagnosed by PCR/serology rather than culture; extrapulmonary syndromes documented, meningitis rare [D]. *Chlamydophila pneumoniae* — atypical, obligate intracellular; chronic-infection hypotheses exist, CNS role unestablished [GAP]. *Legionella pneumophila* — diagnosed by urinary antigen that detects **serogroup 1 only** [D — standard limitation]; no meningitis role.

**Viral pneumonias.** Influenza — the documented bridge virus to pneumococcus, three mechanisms (in §4) [D]. SARS-CoV-2 paired with SARS-CoV — the coronaviral pneumonia pair (his §217 pairing rule honored throughout) [D]; secondary bacterial/fungal superinfections documented, including COVID-associated pulmonary aspergillosis [D]. RSV — viral pneumonia, predominantly pediatric/elderly; bacterial superinfection documented, though less mechanistically mapped than flu [D].

**Fungal pneumonias.** *Pneumocystis jirovecii* (PJP) — β-D-glucan positive; shares the immunocompromised host with cryptococcal meningitis (Node 1) [D]. Invasive pulmonary *Aspergillus* — galactomannan is its biomarker; documented *A. fumigatus*↔pneumococcus antagonism at species level [D]; angioinvasive like disseminated crypto [D]. Pulmonary *Cryptococcus* — same organism as Node 1's leading meningitis cause; the lung is the portal, the meninges the destination [D]. Endemic dimorphics (*Histoplasma*, *Coccidioides*, *Blastomyces*) — granulomatous like TB; *Histoplasma* galactomannan cross-reacts with the *Aspergillus* assay [D]; *Blastomyces* yeast phase evades β-D-glucan detection [D — Mayo Labs].

## §2 — The ten tests: what each detects, which nodes it separates

*Simply put: ten tests, each one a different window; together they can tell fungal from bacterial meningitis and sort out the pneumonias — this section describes each window, and never says which windows anyone should open.*

Framed as the map only — **no recommendation to order anything; test decisions belong to clinicians.**

**1. CSF profile — opening pressure, cell count with differential, glucose, protein [D].** Detects the inflammatory pattern, not the organism. Separates: bacterial (neutrophils, low glucose, high protein) vs. viral (lymphocytes, normal glucose) vs. fungal/TB (lymphocytes, low glucose, high protein; markedly elevated opening pressure points toward cryptococcal) [D — standard clinical teaching]. The orienting test — it aims the rest of the map.

**2. Multiplex meningitis/encephalitis PCR panel — e.g., BioFire FilmArray ME [D].** Detects 14 pathogens in CSF in ~1 hour: 6 bacteria (*E. coli* K1, *H. influenzae*, *L. monocytogenes*, *N. meningitidis*, *S. pneumoniae*, *S. agalactiae*), 7 viruses (CMV, enterovirus, HSV-1/2, HHV-6, parechovirus, VZV), 1 yeast (*Cryptococcus neoformans/gattii*) [D]. Separates: all of Node 1's crypto, all of Node 2's bacteria, and the viral encephalitides — in one run. Built-in limitation: only its 14 targets; *Candida*, *Aspergillus*, *Histoplasma* meningitis are outside it [D].

**3. Cryptococcal antigen (CrAg), serum and CSF [D].** Detects shed cryptococcal capsular polysaccharide — pooled ~98–99% sensitivity/specificity [D]. Separates Node 1's leading cause from everything else on the map. Note the direction: **antigen** test — it finds pieces of the organism's polysaccharide capsule, not the patient's antibodies.

**4. (1→3)-β-D-glucan (Fungitell), serum [D].** Detects a cell-wall polysaccharide of most invasive fungi — positive in *Candida*, *Aspergillus*, *Pneumocystis*, *Fusarium* [D — Mayo Clinic Labs]. Separates by its **negatives**: it does NOT detect *Cryptococcus*, Mucorales, or (reliably) *Blastomyces* [D]. The discriminator fork: **BG-positive + CrAg-negative → non-crypto fungus; BG-negative + CrAg-positive → cryptococcal.** That pair is the fungal fork of the map.

**5. Galactomannan (Platelia Aspergillus EIA), serum or BAL [D].** Detects *Aspergillus* cell-wall galactomannan [D]. Separates invasive aspergillosis from other fungal pneumonias. Honest cross-reactivity [D]: the assay cross-reacts with *Histoplasma*, *Penicillium*, *Fusarium* — shared sugar structures again.

**6. Pneumococcal urinary antigen (BinaxNOW) [D].** Detects C-polysaccharide cell-wall antigen common to **all** pneumococcal strains — in urine, and validated for CSF [D]. Separates pneumococcal disease from other bacterial pneumonias/meningitis. Two limits that matter: it does **not** identify the serotype (C-polysaccharide ≠ capsule) [D], and it stays positive for weeks after disease [D].

**7. Legionella urinary antigen [D].** Detects *L. pneumophila* **serogroup 1 only** [D — standard limitation]. Separates Legionella pneumonia from the rest — but a negative never excludes non-serogroup-1 disease. The map marks what each test cannot see.

**8. Blood cultures [D].** Detect bacteremia and candidemia — the bloodstream highway both meningitis nodes travel. Separates bacteremic from non-bacteremic disease; the positive culture names the organism outright. Slow (days), definitive when positive.

**9. Mycoplasma PCR / serology [D].** Detects the wall-less atypical — invisible to Gram stain and routine culture. Separates *Mycoplasma* pneumonia from the typical bacterial pneumonias.

**10. Respiratory viral multiplex panel [D].** Detects influenza, SARS-CoV-2 (with its paired SARS-CoV), RSV, and other respiratory viruses from a swab. Separates the viral pneumonia layer — and per §4, a positive here is the **forward marker** toward likely bacterial superinfection.

**How the handful works together [LOGIC — steps shown]:** Step 1 — CSF profile orients (bacterial vs. viral vs. fungal/TB pattern). Step 2 — ME panel names 14 entities in an hour, covering both meningitis nodes plus viral encephalitides. Step 3 — CrAg ± β-D-glucan splits the fungal fork (crypto vs. non-crypto). Step 4 — urinary antigens + blood cultures + Mycoplasma testing cover the pneumonia side. Step 5 — the viral panel reads the viral layer, which doubles as the forward marker. Ten tests, three syndromes, one map. The map shows what each test contributes; **it never says which to order — that is the clinician's decision on the patient in front of them.**

## §4 — Viruses as forward/backtrack markers

*Simply put: sometimes a virus shows up first and a bacterial infection follows — this section shows the one pair where that's proven, what his marker idea is, and how many sickness-causing viruses have any documented link to the brain.*

### The documented linkage: influenza → pneumococcus [D]

**Mechanism 1 — the dead-cell anchor:** pneumococcal surface protein PspA was found to act as an adhesin specifically to *dead or dying* lung epithelial cells — missed for 30 years because researchers used healthy cell monolayers. Flu kills the cells; pneumo anchors to the corpses [D].

**Mechanism 2 — immune sabotage:** influenza-induced type I interferon suppresses γδ-T-cell IL-17 production and neutrophil attractants (CXCL1/CXCL2), crippling early bacterial clearance; mice lacking type I interferon signaling survive better [D].

**Mechanism 3 — the bacterium feeds the virus (backtrack):** pneumococcal neuraminidases NanA/NanB support influenza replication; dual-acting neuraminidase inhibitors disrupt the synergy [D]. The 1918 pandemic's mortality was substantially bacterial superinfection — banked lung samples showed the vast majority of the 40–50 million dead had *S. pneumoniae* co-infection or secondary infection [D].

The general pattern [D]: post-viral bacterial pneumonia is the rule, not the exception — RSV and influenza both precede bacterial pneumonia; the major secondary invaders are *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, group B strep, and *H. influenzae*. In flu-infected co-colonized mice, the pneumonia that follows is almost exclusively pneumococcal — pneumococcus disperses while staph dispersal is inhibited [D].

### His marker thesis [H] — filed with the honest line

His thesis: viral findings can be used to **point forward** to likely bacterial/fungal elements and to **backtrack** from a bacterial/fungal finding to the viral context that enabled it. Filed as his; the documented foothold is the synergy literature above — virus first, bacteria second, in that order, repeatedly [H→D]. His marker creed, filed as his: *"viruses are indicators/markers of infection that grow when sick and shrink when better"* [H]; viral latency is established and viral load tracks illness — the dormant/felt and grow/shrink halves of his creed have ground [D]. **The honest line:** the forward/backtrack method is **documented for specific pairs** (influenza↔pneumococcus above) and the **general method is his to evidence** [H]. ### His VNA thesis, tested three ways [H→D]

### Pneumococcal parts × viruses: the related-materials cross-reference [D anchors]

His commission (his words): *"If you take all the known parts of Streptococcus pneumoniae and then known serotypes and then cross-reference them to all the viruses known, the materials that they react with are made out of or are used by those things or are part of those things are all related."* His thesis: the materials are all related [H]. Where the web **holds [D]**:

1. **Sialic acid — the strongest link.** Sialic acid (Neu5Ac) caps the N-glycans on viral envelope glycoproteins — influenza HA/NA, the SARS-CoV-2 spike, HIV gp120 [D]. Influenza hemagglutinin *binds* α2,3/α2,6 sialic acid to enter cells; pneumococcal NanA *cleaves* the same sialic acid to expose its own receptors — and the pneumococcus then *eats* the freed sialic acid through its nan operon [D]. One molecule: the virus's doorway, the bacterium's dinner bell. Viral and bacterial neuraminidases are one enzyme class (sialidases, GH33) on one substrate, assisting each other — and the literature now designs dual inhibitors against both on published "structural and functional similarities" [D].
2. **The glycan camouflage.** Pneumococcal capsules are built from the same monosaccharide alphabet as host glycans and viral envelope N-glycans — and the record states antibodies must use conformational epitopes to discriminate "between bacterial polysaccharides and **host oligosaccharides of similar structure**" [D]. ### osteomyelosis — his coinage [H]

## §3. Additive naming: renaming adds, it does not erase

### His thesis, in his words [H]

> *"If names are added, we're not actually renaming shit — you're actually just adding a new name to help make it more possible to find the same thing. And that's what I think is being forgotten. Because if we're inspired to add more to what's there, it's supposed to be globbed into groups, like I'm trying to do, to make it so that it's easier to figure out."*

His claim: a taxonomic "rename" is a misnomer. What actually happens is **additive naming** — a new name joins the old one as an alias, and the aliases are globbed into a synonym group around the organism. The purpose of the addition is findability: more names pointing at the same thing make it easier to locate, not harder. **The glob (synonym group) is the unit, not the single name** [H].

### The documented foothold [D]

The infrastructure already works this way: NCBI Taxonomy, MycoBank, and GBIF all retain old names as synonyms — the old name remains findable after a rename [D]. His "globbed into groups" describes actual database practice, not wishful thinking. This is the documented foothold, and it is load-bearing.

### The honest pushback, kept [LOGIC]

But the clinic does not run on databases. One name goes on the lab report, and that is the name the doctor acts on. So the thesis needs the split, stated as inference with steps [LOGIC]: step one — in the databases, synonyms are globbed and every alias finds the organism, so his thesis holds on **findability**; step two — on the lab report, one name prints and the other effectively disappears for that clinician, so the retention side holds on **the used name**; step three — both can be true at once, because they are claims about different layers. The retentionists (the ISHAM working group, filed 2026-10-14) do not fear that *Candida auris* becomes unfindable — they fear that the *reported* name changes under people's feet. The thesis wins findability; the retention case wins the used name; the real contest moves to who gets to pick the one printed name — the lab, per the 2026-10-10 note on the lab reporting the name it chooses. The split relocates the fight rather than dissolving it, and the manuscript says so.

### The worked example: the *auris* retention case [D]

The worked example is *Candida auris* — now formally *Candidozyma auris* (Liu et al., 2024, *Persoonia*) [D] — and the worked pair inside it is the near-homonym collision that started the whole inquiry. *Auris* and *aureus* are two different Latin words that happen to sound alike [D]: **auris = ear** — the species was first described in 2009 (Satoh et al.) from the external ear canal of a 70-year-old woman at Tokyo Metropolitan Geriatric Hospital, Japan, and the epithet was chosen for the isolation site [D]; **aureus = golden** — *Staphylococcus aureus* Rosenbach 1884, named for the golden colonies (from *aurum*, gold), while *S. albus* (white) was later renamed *S. epidermidis* [D]. Ear vs. golden. No shared root, no shared meaning, no shared lineage. One names a body site where a yeast was first found; the other names a colony color of a bacterium described 125 years earlier. The resemblance is phonetic accident — exactly the kind of name-collision a nomenclature audit should flag, because both organisms now circulate in the same clinical conversation as bloodstream pathogens in ICUs.

And here is where the additive thesis earns its chapter: the epithet *auris* **survived** the genus change to *Candidozyma* — the "ear" meaning travels with it [D]. The rename added an alias (*Candidozyma auris*) without erasing the finder (*Candida auris*) or the meaning (*ear*). Renaming adds; it does not erase — in the record. The retention case for keeping the old name in circulation (unambiguous communication, surveillance continuity) and the additive thesis (the glob is the unit) both hold, because they answer different questions: which name prints on the report, and which names find the organism in the databases. The manuscript states both, with their tags on.

### The mirror image — one extra of the builder's, labeled mine

The *aureus*-across-kingdoms list is the mirror image of his additive-naming thesis: there, one organism accumulates many names (aliases globbed for findability); here, one descriptor is pasted onto many unrelated organisms — *Aspergillus aureus*, the golden mold of Okinawa koji; *Scleropages aureus*, a fish; *Senecio aureus*, golden ragwort; a flame bowerbird; a pebblesnail [D]. *Aureus* is a descriptor (golden), not a lineage marker — taxonomy reuses it everywhere something looks gold. Both prove the same point from opposite sides: **names carry properties, not lineages**, and any chapter arguing from a name alone is arguing from a property tag. The practical rule for ch 6's nomenclature audit writes itself: *never let a shared word imply a shared organism.* The auris/aureus collision is the worked example — two ICU bloodstream pathogens, one shared sound, zero shared biology. [Mine, per his standing rule — the deliverable's one extra.]

> **Simply put.** He says renaming isn't really renaming — the old name stays as a findable alias, so the name-group grows instead of the old name dying. That's exactly how the big organism databases already work. But he also admits the honest limit: on a lab report, only one name prints, so both sides of the fight are right about different things — the databases or the report.
