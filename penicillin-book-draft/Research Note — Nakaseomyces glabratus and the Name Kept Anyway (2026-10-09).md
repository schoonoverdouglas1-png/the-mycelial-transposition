# Research Note — *Nakaseomyces glabratus* and the Name Kept Anyway

**Filed:** 2026-10-09 · For local peer review alongside *The Mycelial Transposition* manuscript
**Source:** Peer-reviewed paper, *Frontiers in Cellular and Infection Microbiology* 16:1874660 (published 29 September 2026); Yang, Zhang, Zhang, Wang & Liu
**Link:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cellular-and-infection-microbiology/articles/10.3389/fcimb.2026.1874660/full

## The study (in brief)

- Laboratory work on isobavachalcone, a plant-derived chalcone, tested against the *Candida glabrata* standard strain ATCC 2001 and clinical isolates, in planktonic and biofilm growth.
- Reported MIC of 4–5 μg/mL, inhibition of biofilm formation/development, and a mechanism involving intracellular reactive-oxygen-species overproduction and plasma-membrane damage, supported by staining, electron microscopy, and an N-acetyl-cysteine rescue test.
- In vitro only — no animal or human data in this paper.

## The nomenclature hook

- The title itself carries both names: *Candida glabrata* (*Nakaseomyces glabratus*). The abstract and introduction acknowledge the recent reclassification, and then the authors state they kept the old name in this study — without giving a substantive reason.
- That is a clean, current specimen of naming inertia in the primary literature: the rename is known, printed in the title, and still not adopted in the body of the work. Compare the *C. auris* mortality meta-analysis filed 2026-10-08, which also carried the superseded name in its title.
- A smaller detail worth a footnote: the species epithet changes ending with the genus move (*glabrata* → *glabratus*). A genus rename is not a simple find-and-replace; the binomial's grammar moves with it — one more friction point that helps the old name persist, and a concrete example for the "binomials as compressed field notes" thread filed 2026-10-09.
- Context the paper itself gives for why the species matters separately from *C. albicans*: distant phylogenetics between the two, and this species' low intrinsic susceptibility to fluconazole — i.e., the (new) name marks a clinically different organism, not just a relabelled one. That is the manuscript-frame point only; it is not a treatment suggestion for anyone.

## Honest caveats (do not drop these)

1. **Preclinical, in vitro.** A promising antifungal candidate in dishes and biofilm assays is not a therapy; most such compounds never reach patients.
2. **The old-name retention is unexplained.** The paper does not argue for keeping *Candida glabrata*; it simply does. Do not attribute a motive (clinical pragmatism, habit, journal style) that the authors did not state.
3. **Not evidence for the reclassification argument itself.** As with the other notes, this is a specimen of naming practice doing work in the literature — useful for the nomenclature chapters, not proof of the penicillin-reclassification claim.
4. **Keep separate from personal medical care.** This note is manuscript material only.

## Open questions for peer review

- Two 2026 papers, two different responses to a rename — one adopts the new genus in the title (see the BMC *Candidozyma auris* cohort note filed the same day), one acknowledges it and keeps the old name. Is adoption-vs-retention itself a usable axis for the chapter on how names move through the literature?
- Does the epithet-ending change (*glabrata* → *glabratus*) earn a sentence in the manuscript? It is a small, concrete reason renames are sticky in databases, lab reports, and search results.
