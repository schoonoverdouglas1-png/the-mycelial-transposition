# The Coliseum — Where Etymology Fits the Solution

**Filed:** 2026-10-18 · For *The Mycelial Transposition* (creative manuscript)
**Frame:** educational manuscript reference only. Never medical advice. No diagnosis, no application to any person.

## His thesis [H]

The difficult ground — **the coliseum** — is the Latin words in between: between the American names of people's sicknesses and the viruses at the end. And that is where the etymology fits the solution: **the answer is inside the Latin word's own pieces.** Break the word, and the shape, the site, the symptom, or the behavior is sitting there — the solution was in the name the whole time.

**Naming directive [H]:** "Lou Gehrig's disease" first, Latin second — American first, Latin second, medicine/science after.

## The coliseum — the difficult ground, and where it opens

The Latin in-between is difficult ground for five reasons, and each one is a fight in the arena [D on the name histories; his lens on the readings]:

1. **The eponyms.** *Neisseria, Rickettsia, Brucella, Shigella, Bordetella, Klebsiella, Listeria, Salmonella, Nocardia, Escherichia* — the Latin names the MAN, not the disease. The word carries zero showing. This is the hardest ground: you must fight past the discoverer's name to the root that matters — and sometimes there is no other root. The solution here is negative: the word tells you it is hiding something.

2. **The renames that stripped the showing.** In 1887 the meningitis organism was *Diplococcus intracellularis meningitidis* — *diplo-* (double) + *intra-* (inside) + *cellularis* (of cells): the microscope image, in the name. In 1901 Albrecht & Ghon renamed it *Neisseria meningitidis* — and the showing (double, inside, of cells) was traded for the discoverer's name. The exact error, fossilized [D].

3. **The honest differentials.** *Bilious fever, spotted fever, bloody flux* — the American name honestly covered two or three diseases under one showing, and the Latin split them. Here the difficult ground is honest: the American kept the differential, the Latin did the sorting. Both kept [H].

4. **The dropped.** *Nostalgia* — homesickness, listed as a disease and a cause of death — got no Latin replacement. It was dropped, not renamed. The error of replacement, shown by absence [D].

5. **Where the word IS the solution.** *Streptococcus* (twisted berries), *pyogenes* (pus-maker), *lyssa* (rage), *tetanos* (stretched), *anthrakis* (coal), *kynanche* (dog-choking), *diphthera* (leather), *choreia* (dance), *koryne* (club), *trepo-nema* (turning thread). Where the Latin kept its Greek root, the word is the diagnosis — shape, site, symptom, or behavior, sitting in the open. This is where the etymology fits the solution, exactly as he said [H→D].

## The entries — Latin in-between, etymology as solution

### 0. Throat distemper × KLB × *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* (template)

- **KLB** — *Klebs-Löffler Bacillus*: two eponyms (Klebs, Löffler) + Latin *bacillus* (from *baculus*, staff, stick — the rod) [D]. The solution: **"bacillus" tells the family** — rod-shaped, at a glance [H→D]. The men's names tell nothing; the rod tells the family.
- ***Corynebacterium*** — Greek *koryne* (club) + Latinized Greek *bakterion* (small staff, rod) [D]. The solution: **club-shaped rods** — the microscope image, in the word.
- ***diphtheriae*** — Greek *diphthera* (leather, hide, prepared skin) + *-iae* (of) [D]. The solution: **the leather membrane** — the pathology named inside the organism's own name. The throat's leather is the disease's signature.
- **Coliseum note:** eponym + shape + pathology. The difficult ground (two men's names) opens onto the solution (club, rod, leather).

### 1. Consumption × *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*

- ***Mycobacterium*** — Greek *mykes* (fungus, mushroom) + *bakterion* (rod) [D]. The solution: **the waxy, fungus-like rod** — *mykes* explains the waxy coat that makes it hold the acid-fast stain. The word tells you why the stain works.
- ***tuberculosis*** — Latin *tuberculum* (small swelling, diminutive of *tuber*, lump) + *-osis* (condition, process) [D]. The solution: **the disease of small swellings** — the tubercles, the granulomas, named in the word. ("Tuberculosis" coined 1839; the organism named 1882 [D].)
- **Coliseum note:** agent + product — the rod and what it makes. The American (*con-* thoroughly + *sumere* to take: "to take completely") says what it DOES; the Latin says what it IS and what it MAKES.

### 2. Phthisis × *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*

- ***Phthisis*** — Greek *phthisis*, from *phthino* (to waste away, to dwindle, to decay, to pine) [D]. The solution: **the verb IS the disease** — wasting named as an action, not a thing. The patient dwindles; the word dwindles.
- **Coliseum note:** the Greek verb and the Latin noun describe the same dwindling from opposite ends — the patient's experience and the pathologist's finding.

### 3. King's evil × tuberculous cervical lymphadenitis (scrofula)

- ***scrofula*** — Latin *scrofulae* (plural), diminutive of *scrofa* (a breeding sow) — "little pigs," the glandular swellings of the neck [D]. The solution: **the swelling named for the animal** — the folk eye, inside the Latin.
- ***tuberculous*** — of tubercles [D]. ***cervical*** — Latin *cervix* (neck), "of the neck" [D]. ***lymphadenitis*** — Greek *lympha* (clear water) + *aden* (gland) + *-itis* (inflammation) [D]. The solution: **water-gland-inflammation of the neck** — the full anatomy, spelled out in three roots.
- **Coliseum note:** the Latin is a complete anatomical sentence; the American keeps the royal-touch history. The difficult ground here is length — the sentence must be walked piece by piece.

### 4. Brain fever × *Neisseria meningitidis*

- ***Neisseria*** — after Albert Neisser (eponym) [D]. The solution: **none in the word** — the difficult ground at full hardness. The man, not the showing.
- ***meningitidis*** — Greek *meninx* (membrane) + *-itis* (inflammation) + *-idis* (of) [D]. The solution: **"of the membrane-inflammation"** — site and process, in the open.
- **Coliseum note:** the 1887 name *Diplococcus intracellularis meningitidis* carried the microscope image (*diplo-* double, *intra-* inside, *cellularis* of cells); the 1901 rename traded it for the eponym [D]. The fight: restore the dropped showing — read *intracellularis* back into the word.

### 5. Lung fever / winter fever × *Streptococcus pneumoniae*

- **Coliseum note:** the easy ground — shape + site, no eponym, nothing hidden. The word is pure showing.

### 6–9. Throat distemper / sore throat distemper / malignant sore throat / bladder in throat / membranous croup × *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*

## The in-between organisms — the stuck-to-the-chain names carry solutions too

- ***Streptococcus*** — twisted berries (entry 5). ***Staphylococcus*** — Greek *staphyle* (bunch of grapes) + *kokkos*: **grapes vs chains** — the two great coccus genera distinguished by their geometry, in the words.
- ***Cryptococcus neoformans*** — Greek *kryptos* (hidden) + *kokkos* (berry) + *neo-* (new) + *formans* (forming): **the hidden berry, newly forming** — the fungus you cannot see until it forms.
- ***Candida albicans*** — Latin *candidus* (glowing white) + *albicare* (to whiten): **the whitening white** — the thrush membrane's color, twice.
- ***Aspergillus fumigatus*** — Latin *aspergillum* (the holy-water sprinkler) + *fumigare* (to smoke): **the sprinkler that smokes** — named for the conidiophore's shape, like the priest's sprinkler; the colonies smoke gray-green.
- ***Pneumocystis jirovecii*** — Greek *pneumon* (lung) + *kystis* (bladder, cyst) + after Jírovec: **the lung-bladder** — the cyst in the lung, in the word.
- ***Trichophyton*** — Greek *thrix* (hair) + *phyton* (plant): **the hair-plant**. ***Microsporum*** — *mikros* (small) + *sporos* (seed): **the small seed**. ***Epidermophyton*** — *epi-* (upon) + *derma* (skin) + *phyton*: **the upon-skin plant**. The dermatophytes name their address: hair, skin, seed [D].
- ***Histoplasma capsulatum*** — *histo-* (tissue) + *plasma* (formed) + *capsula* (little box): **the tissue-formed little box**.
- ***Coccidioides*** — Greek *kokkis* (berry) + *eidos* (form): **the berry-formed**. ***Blastomyces*** — *blastos* (bud, sprout) + *mykes* (fungus): **the budding fungus**.

- ***Haemophilus*** — Greek *haima* (blood) + *philos* (loving): **the blood-lover**. ***Mycoplasma*** — *mykes* (fungus) + *plasma*: **the fungus-formed** (no wall — the solution is the absence). ***Legionella*** — after the American Legion (1976 outbreak): the difficult ground, pure circumstance [D].

## The etymology families — pieces that echo across more than one thing

**Shape-words** (Greek/Latin, mostly Latin-side): *streptos* (twisted), *kokkos* (berry), *staphyle* (grapes), *koryne* (club), *baculus* (staff), *kloster* (spindle), *fusus* (spindle), *kampylos* (curved), *trepo-nema* (turning thread), *tainia* (ribbon), *toxon* (bow), *rota* (wheel), *aspergillum* (sprinkler), *kystis* (bladder). The shape vocabulary is the microscope's vocabulary — almost entirely Latin-side. The American names rarely say shape (*bladder*, *eel* excepted). His "bacillus tells the family" sits here [H→D].

**Site-words**: *pneumon* (lung), *meninx* (membrane), *osteon* (bone), *myelos* (marrow), *nephros* (kidney), *hepar* (liver), *enteron* (gut), *gaster* (stomach), *derma* (skin), *thrix* (hair), *cervix* (neck), *kystis* (bladder), *polios* (gray matter), *lateralis* (side). Site is the American track's home ground — but the Latin carries its own site-map underneath [D].

**Symptom-words**: *tetanos* (stretched), *lyssa* (rage), *phobos* (fear), *diphthera* (leather), *anthrakis* (coal), *chloros* (green), *flavus* (yellow), *typhos* (smoke), *putridus* (rotten), *sepsis* (decay), *mors* (death), *dolor* implied in *ache*, *tussis* (cough), *febris* (heat), *algos* (pain), *choreia* (dance), *eklampsis* (shining-forth), *epilepsia* (seizing-upon). The symptom is the oldest diagnostic instrument, and it is embedded in the taxonomy [D].

**Behavior-words** (diagnosis by behavior): *lyssa*/madness, *choreia*/dance, *epilepsia*/falling, *hydrophobia*/fear-of-water, *grippe*/seizing. The behavior-as-diagnosis family [H].

**Color-words** (color as diagnosis): *flavus* (yellow), *chloros* (green), *erythros* (red), *pallidus* (pale), *anthrakis* (coal-black), *candidus* (glowing white), *niger* implied in black vomit. Color diagnoses across entries [D].

**Eponym-words** (the difficult ground): Neisser, Ricketts, Prowazek, Bruce, Shiga, Bordet, Klebs, Löffler, Lister, Salmon, Nocard, Escherich, Jírovec, Fowler, Naegler, Sydenham, Bright, Shiga. The Latin memorializes discoverers; the American memorializes patients, trades, and places. Two different memorials, one list [D].

**Time-words**: *chronos* (chronic), *galloping* (speed), *day* (duration), *undulare* (wave), *apoplexia* (suddenness). Time diagnoses too [D].

## The coliseum verdict [H — his thesis, restated as the paper's close]

The difficult ground was the Latin in between. The etymology fit the solution: **the answer was inside the word's own pieces all along** — the twisted berries, the pus-maker, the rage, the leather membrane, the coal-black eschar, the dog-choking, the turning thread, the hardening on the side from muscle without nourishment. Where the Latin kept its Greek root, the word was the diagnosis. Where the Latin named a man, the word was the hiding place — and knowing it hides is itself the solution. The coliseum is not the enemy of the names. It is the arena where the names prove themselves.
