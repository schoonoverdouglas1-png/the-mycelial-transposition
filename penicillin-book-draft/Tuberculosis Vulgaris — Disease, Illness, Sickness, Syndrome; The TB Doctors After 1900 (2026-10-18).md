# Tuberculosis Vulgaris — Disease, Illness, Sickness, Syndrome; The TB Doctors After 1900

**Filed:** 2026-10-18 · For *The Mycelial Transposition* (creative manuscript)
**Manuscript/educational reference only — never medical advice. Test and treatment decisions belong to the clinician and the patient.**

## The upfront honest note (read first, then the build)

**"Tuberculosis vulgaris" is Douglas's construction [H].** There is no historical record of the term as a medical name. The documented neighbor is *lupus vulgaris* — tuberculosis of the skin [D] — where the *vulgaris* tag ("common, ordinary") does reach into the tuberculosis family. *Tuberculosis pneumoniae*, which he names it as the old Latin name for, is likewise his coinage [H]. This paper expands HIS concept along his four tracks — disease, illness, sickness, syndrome — and develops his thesis about the tuberculosis doctors. It is his postulate worked at full length, with the record attached where the record exists.

# Part I — The Four Tracks

## I.1 Tuberculosis Vulgaris AS DISEASE (the entity)

As disease, tuberculosis vulgaris is the common form — the entity itself, named the way old medicine named things: *vulgaris* marks the typical, ordinary presentation, the one the doctors saw most [D — the naming morphology is documented; the application to TB is his]. The disease-entity is *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* infection and what it builds in the body: the tubercle, the small hardened nodule where the immune system walls off the bacillus [D]. Caseation, cavitation, the Ghon complex — the hardened inclusions, calcified in the most literal sense [D].

The disease has carried many names across its history, and the name-history is part of the entity [D]: *phthisis* (Greek, "to waste away," Hippocrates) [D]; *tabes* (Latin, "to waste away") [D]; scrofula; consumption (Latin *consumere*, "to devour, to waste") [D]; and "tuberculosis" itself, coined by Johann Lukas Schönlein in the mid-19th century [D] — a combination of *tubercle* (Latin *tuberculum*, small swelling) and the Greek *-osis* suffix marking abnormal condition [D]. Robert Koch demonstrated the bacillus as the single cause of tuberculosis in all its forms in his March 24, 1882 lecture before the Physiological Society of Berlin [D]; the organism was later renamed *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (Lehmann and Neumann, 1896) [D].

Douglas's point in naming it *tuberculosis vulgaris* is the commonness itself [H]: this is the disease as the world knew it — one in seven deaths in Europe at its height [D — Koch's own 1882 figure: "one seventh of all humans die of tuberculosis"]. The disease-track is the entity stripped of the person: the bacillus, the tubercle, the name-history. His forward-and-backward Latin method starts here, because no disease has a deeper Latin name-record to trace [H→D].

## I.2 Tuberculosis Vulgaris AS ILLNESS (the lived condition)

As illness, tuberculosis vulgaris is what the disease does to a person living inside it — the track of experience, not the track of the entity. The illness is chronicity: months and years of wasting, the evening fever that rises with the day's end, night sweats, the cough that will not leave, blood in the sputum [D — the documented symptom constellation, drawn from the historical clinical record]. The illness has a tempo — slow, consuming — which is exactly what the old names recorded: *phthisis*, the wasting and melting away [D].

The illness-track also carries the culture that grew around it [D]: the Victorian romanticization of consumption — the pale, artistic consumptive — which shaped how the ill person was seen and how they saw themselves [D]. And it carries the sanatorium years: the illness as a managed life — rest in the open air, abundant diet, the *Liegehallen* where patients spent their days lying in sheltered open air [D — Dettweiler's Falkenstein regimen]. The illness is the disease lived at human speed.

Douglas's separation matters here [H]: the illness is not the disease. The disease is the bacillus and the tubercle; the illness is the wasting, the fever, the years. Medicine after 1900 learned to measure the disease exquisitely — and his track-system insists the illness be thought separately, because the person experiences the illness whether or not the disease is measured [H].

## I.3 Tuberculosis Vulgaris AS SICKNESS (the social dimension)

As sickness, tuberculosis vulgaris is the disease as society handles it — the sick role, the institutions, the public apparatus. This is the track where the tuberculosis doctors built the machinery Douglas's thesis is about [H→D].

The sickness-track is the sanatorium as an institution: Brehmer's *Heilanstalt* at Görbersdorf from 1854, Dettweiler's Falkenstein from 1876, Trudeau's Adirondack Cottage Sanitarium at Saranac Lake in the 1880s [D] — the closed-institution approach that became the sanatorium movement, housing the sick apart from the well [D]. It is the dispensary: Calmette's Émile Roux antituberculosis dispensary at Lille, opened February 1901, giving early diagnosis and treatment to those who could not go to a sanatorium, advising on how to minimize spread [D] — modeled on Philip's 1887 Edinburgh dispensary [D]. It is the public-health apparatus: case finding, registries, the training of social and medical assistants, the League structures (Calmette's Ligue du Nord contre la Tuberculose, 1904) [D].

The sickness-track also carries the coercive edge [D]: compulsory notification and isolation of consumptives (Naples, 1735 — the Health Board ordered notification, isolation, and forbade admission to public hospitals) [D]; the stigma of the "white plague" [D]. The sick person was counted, tracked, separated, advised — the template for how modern public health handles the infectious sick [H→D].

Douglas's thesis lives strongest on this track [H]: the sickness machinery — dispensaries, registries, contact-era case finding, institutional care — was built by the tuberculosis doctors, and later medicine inherited it whole.

## I.4 Tuberculosis Vulgaris AS SYNDROME (the constellation)

As syndrome, tuberculosis vulgaris is the pattern — the constellation of findings that hangs together and says the name. The syndrome is the evening fever plus the night sweats plus the wasting plus the chronic cough plus the hemoptysis [D]. No single one of these is the disease; together they are the recognizable shape. Old medicine named syndromes before it knew entities — *phthisis* was a syndrome-name for centuries before Koch found the bacillus [D].

The syndrome-track is where Douglas's "we're talking about syndromes" framing does its work [H]: the syndrome is the level at which the clinician first meets the patient, the level at which his symptomology thesis operates (the Lyme/California-encephalitis/lupus symptom-overlap claim, filed separately) [H]. The syndrome is also the level at which his top/bottom pattern thinking applies — which findings show when others are active [H].

And the syndrome is where the forward-and-backward Latin method pays off [H]: trace the syndrome's names backward (*consumption → phthisis*) and you find the same constellation described for two thousand years [D]; trace forward and you find the constellation dissolving into laboratory entities — and his claim is that something was lost in the dissolving, that the syndrome knew things the entity-measurement forgot [H].

# Part II — Symptomology: The Connective Tissue

## His doctrine [H], stated and paralleled

Douglas's doctrine: **symptoms are the beginning and basis of diagnosis.** Filed as his [H].

The honest parallel [D]: the clinical history of present illness has always been diagnosis's starting point. Medicine's own method begins exactly where his doctrine begins — with what the patient reports, in the patient's words, before any instrument is touched. His principle parallels established clinical method; his formulation is his own.

## The symptom constellation of tuberculosis vulgaris [D]

The documented historical symptomology: wasting/emaciation, evening fever, night sweats, chronic cough, hemoptysis (blood-spitting), chest pain, weak pulse, fatigue, diarrhea [D — drawn from the historical clinical record, e.g., the UC Irvine lecture record of 19th-century TB symptomatology]. Each symptom below is read through all four tracks — this is the connective tissue: one symptom, four readings.

**Wasting (emaciation).** *Disease:* the metabolic cost of chronic granulomatous inflammation; the body consumed [D]. *Illness:* the lived melting-away — *phthisis* named the illness before the disease was known [D]. *Sickness:* the visible mark that sorted the sick from the well — the "white plague" pallor that made the consumptive recognizable in public [D]. *Syndrome:* the anchor finding of the constellation — without wasting, the old physicians did not call it consumption [D].

**Evening fever.** *Disease:* the inflammatory rhythm of the infected body [D]. *Illness:* the day's end bringing the heat — the private, repeated experience [D]. *Sickness:* the measurable sign the sanatorium charted — temperature curves as institutional records [D]. *Syndrome:* the temporal signature — *evening* fever, not morning; the timing is part of the pattern [D].

**Night sweats.** *Disease:* autonomic response to chronic infection [D]. *Illness:* waking drenched — the intimate disruption of rest [D]. *Sickness:* the laundry, the nursing care — the labor the sick imposed on households and institutions [D]. *Syndrome:* paired with evening fever, the diurnal pair that old medicine recognized as one finding [D].

**Hemoptysis (blood-spitting).** *Disease:* cavitary erosion into bronchial vessels [D]. *Illness:* the terrifying visible proof — blood where breath should be [D]. *Sickness:* the alarm that moved a person from the community into the institution — hemoptysis made the sick undeniable [D]. *Syndrome:* the dramatic member of the constellation — the finding that fixed the diagnosis in the old physician's mind [D].

**Chronic cough.** *Disease:* airway irritation and cavitary drainage [D]. *Illness:* the cough that never leaves — months of it, shaping every day [D]. *Sickness:* the public sign — the cough heard across the room, the reason for separation and for the dispensary's hygiene advice [D]. *Syndrome:* the persistent member — chronicity itself as a diagnostic feature [D].

**The connective claim [H]:** run the four tracks and the symptom is the thread that passes through all of them. The disease explains it, the illness lives it, the sickness manages it, the syndrome recognizes it. Douglas's doctrine — symptoms as the beginning and basis of diagnosis — is the claim that the thread should be followed from the symptom outward, not from the laboratory backward [H]. The honest parallel stands: medicine's own history of present illness does exactly this [D].

# Part III — The Tuberculosis Doctors: The Foundation Thesis

## His thesis [H]

"The study for all these other things came from afterwards. All these studies of medicine after 1900 are based upon the tuberculosis doctors."

Filed as his thesis. Tested below against the record — where it holds, where it overreaches. His thesis is kept intact and labeled throughout.

## Foothold 1: Koch's postulates — the method of causation [H→D]

Koch's 1882 tuberculosis work did not just find a bacillus; it built the method for proving any microbe causes any disease. The postulates — the organism found in all cases examined, grown in pure culture, reproducing the disease on inoculation, re-isolated from the newly sick — were formulated in 1882 and 1890 out of the tuberculosis investigation [D]. Armed with this framework, the late 19th century identified the bacterial causes of cholera (1883), typhoid (1884), diphtheria (1884), pneumococcal pneumonia (1887), tetanus (1889), plague (1894) [D] — and the same methodology was later applied to HIV, SARS, and COVID-19 [D]. The postulates remain the gold standard for pathogen identification [D]. Douglas's thesis holds here at full strength: the causation-method of post-1900 infectious medicine is a tuberculosis method.

## Foothold 2: The sanatorium — the institution of systematic observation [H→D]

Brehmer's Görbersdorf (1854; dissertation: "Tuberculosis is a Curable Disease"), Dettweiler's Falkenstein (1876; rest in the *Liegehallen*), Trudeau's Saranac Lake (1880s) [D] — the sanatorium was the first institution built around systematic, long-term clinical observation of a single disease: temperature curves, weight charts, rest and nutrition as therapy, strict medical supervision [D]. Before antibiotics, it was "one of the first useful measures against TB" [D]. The sanatorium taught medicine how to watch a disease over time — the institutional model of the chronic-disease hospital descends from it [H→D]. (Honest seam: Bodington's 1840 essay proposed the regimen earlier and was rejected by the establishment [D] — the idea predates the movement.)

## Foothold 3: The dispensary and the public-health apparatus [H→D]

Philip's Edinburgh dispensary (1887), Calmette's Émile Roux dispensary at Lille (February 1901) [D] — early diagnosis for those who could not go to sanatoria, advice on minimizing spread, training of social and medical assistants [D]. This is the template: case finding, registries, community follow-up, the dispensary as the bridge between the sick and the institution [H→D]. Modern infectious-disease public health — TB control programs' "testing and treating those at risk" as a key function [D] — runs on rails the tuberculosis doctors laid.

## Foothold 4: BCG — the live-attenuated vaccine [H→D]

Calmette and Guérin, 13 years, 230+ serial passages of *Mycobacterium bovis* on bile-potato medium, first human administration July 1921 [D]; League of Nations declared it safe 1928 [D]; WHO's Expanded Programme on Immunization from 1974, more than 4 billion doses administered [D] — the most widely used vaccine in history [D]. The live-attenuated bacterial vaccine as a concept was worked out on tuberculosis [H→D]. (Honest seam: the vaccine concept itself is Jenner's, 1796 [D] — the TB doctors extended it, they did not invent vaccination.)

## Foothold 5: Streptomycin — the antibiotic era's launch [H→D]

Isolated October 19, 1943, by Schatz, Waksman, and Bugie at Rutgers [D]; the first antibiotic effective against tuberculosis [D]; Waksman Nobel Prize 1952 [D]; the UK Medical Research Council's 1946 streptomycin trial was the first randomized controlled clinical trial in medicine [D]; U.S. TB mortality fell from 194 per 100,000 (1900) to 9.1 per 100,000 (1955) within a decade of its release [D]. The deliberate soil-screening search for chemotherapeutic agents — contrasting with Fleming's chance observation of penicillin — was the tuberculosis doctors' method [D]. The antibiotic era's proof that systemic bacterial disease could be cured by a drug is a tuberculosis proof [H→D].

## Foothold 6: Thoracic surgery — collapse therapy and the chest [H→D]

Forlanini's artificial pneumothorax (invented 1882 — the same year as Koch's bacillus [D]), thoracoplasty (1885), plombage, phrenic crush, resection [D] — an entire surgical discipline grew up around the tuberculous chest, performed at first by professors of internal medicine, not surgeons [D]. And when TB came under control, "the techniques they had learned became steppingstones to surgical techniques for other intrathoracic procedures" — lung resection foremost among them [D]. Modern thoracic surgery descends from tuberculosis surgery [H→D].

## Foothold 7: Tuberculin — delayed-type hypersensitivity diagnostics [H→D]

Koch's 1890 tuberculin failed as a cure [D] — and became the diagnostic: Pirquet's scratch test (1907; he also coined "latent TB infection," 1909), Mantoux's intradermal test (1908), Seibert's purified protein derivative (1930s; U.S. adopted 1940) [D]; the test "remained virtually unchanged for almost eighty years" and sits on the WHO essential medicines list [D]. The tuberculin reaction is the classic demonstration of delayed-type (cell-mediated) hypersensitivity — the immunological concept was worked out on tuberculosis [H→D]. Ehrlich, present at Koch's 1882 lecture ("the most important experience of my scientific life"), went on to found immunology and chemotherapy [D] — the TB lecture as the seed of another field.

## Testing the "all" — where the thesis overreaches [D]

The honest seams, kept attached to his thesis:

1. **Germ theory predates the TB doctors.** Pasteur, Lister, and the anthrax work (Koch's own 1876 anthrax work preceded the TB work [D]) built the germ framework; tuberculosis was its greatest application, not its origin.
2. **Surgery has older roots.** The surgical tradition — anesthesia, antisepsis — was built on other battlefields; TB gave thoracic surgery its specific discipline, not surgery itself.
3. **Molecular medicine has other fathers.** Genetics, molecular biology, and later immunology's molecular era trace to other lines (Mendel, the phage school, Burnet) — the TB doctors founded the infectious-disease method, not all of medicine.
4. **The vaccine concept is Jenner's** (1796) [D]; **chemotherapy's concept is Ehrlich's** (Salvarsan, 1909) [D] — though Ehrlich's line runs back through Koch's lecture.

**The tested verdict [H→D]:** "All studies of medicine after 1900" overstates it — but the overstatement is narrow. For infectious-disease methodology — how a pathogen is proven, how the sick are found and managed, how a bacterial vaccine is built, how an antibiotic is proven in trial, how the immune response is read in skin, how the chest is opened — the tuberculosis doctors are the foundation. His thesis holds at full strength inside infectious disease and its methods; outside it, the honest seams above apply. The thesis is kept intact, labeled his, with the record's boundaries marked.

## Closing synthesis

*Tuberculosis vulgaris* — his construction, honestly labeled — run through four tracks: the disease (the entity and its names), the illness (the lived wasting), the sickness (the institutions and the apparatus), the syndrome (the constellation). The symptomology is the connective tissue: one symptom read four ways, and his doctrine that symptoms are the beginning and basis of diagnosis — paralleling medicine's own history of present illness. And behind all of it, the tuberculosis doctors: Koch's postulates, the sanatorium, the dispensary, BCG, streptomycin, collapse therapy, tuberculin — the seven footholds on which post-1900 infectious medicine stands. His forward-and-backward Latin method has its anchor: no disease gives the tracer a deeper line to follow.
