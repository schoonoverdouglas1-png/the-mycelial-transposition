## The etymology — every word unhidden

- ***Neisseria*** — named for **Albert Neisser**, the man [D]. And Neisser's fame was finding the *gonococcus* — so entry 5's genus is named for the man who discovered its **cousin** (*N. gonorrhoeae*). Kinship written into the naming: the genus carries the cousin-finder's name.
- ***meningitidis*** — "of meningitis": *mening-* (membrane) + *-itis* (inflammation), in the genitive [D]. The organism named **for the disease it causes** — the naming direction runs disease→organism, the reverse of the usual.
- ***Bacillus*** — Latin for **little rod / little staff** [D]. The shape, plainly.
- ***anthracis*** — Greek *anthrax*, **coal** [D]. The color of the dead tissue. Shape + color: the whole organism described in two words.
- ***Sputum*** — Latin, from *spuere*, **to spit** [D]. The specimen is literally "the spit" — the sample named for the act that produced it.
- ***Bacilli / cocci*** — little rods / **berries** [D]. Shape-words, crossing kingdoms (§159).
- ***Gram*** — **Hans Christian Gram**, 1884 [D]. The stain that sorts the world by shape-words is named for a man — like *Neisseria*. Two eponyms holding the whole sorting system.
- ***Flora*** — Latin for **flowers** [D]. *Oro-* (mouth) + *pharynx* (throat): **"normal oropharyngeal flora" = "the normal flowers of the throat."**

## The three naming directions

One compilation, three directions the Latin travels [H→D]:

1. **Man → organism** (*Neisseria*, *Gram*) — the discoverer becomes the name.
2. **Disease → organism** (*meningitidis*) — the showing names the bug.
3. **Shape + color → organism** (*Bacillus anthracis*) — the little coal-colored rod.

And the specimen (*sputum*) takes a fourth: **act → sample** — the spit names itself.

## Something interesting (builder's extra — labeled mine)

Read the lab's final line again with the Latin unhidden: **"Growth of normal oropharyngeal flora"** = *"the normal flowers of the throat, growing."*

The laboratory — the establishment, the chart, the system — calls the mouth's bacteria **a garden**. Flowers. Growing. His colonist thesis (§58: "the body made of colonists") isn't smuggled into the record here — **it's the record's own vocabulary**. The lab tech who typed "flora" was speaking his language without knowing it: the throat keeps a garden, the garden has normal flowers, and the report is the gardener's note.

That's why it's named Latinly that way. *Flora* was never just a word for bacteria — it was always the claim that they **live somewhere**, planted, tended, in place. The shapes of disease, growing like flowers in the throat.
