# American Diseases 1776 to the Renaming (2026-10-18)

**Frame:** *The Mycelial Transposition* — educational manuscript reference only. Never medical advice. No diagnosis, no treatment recommendations, no application to any person.

## His thesis, up front [H]

In the beginning medicine was simply put: **you're sick, you're sick. If you're well, you're well.** There isn't a whole lot in between — but there's still a little bit of something. The renaming era sold itself as more accurate data; his read is the data became LESS accurate, because the names stopped saying sick-or-well on the body and started saying committee Latin. His additive-naming thesis is the reversal: keep the old name, ADD the accurate one — both stand.

## Era 1 — 1776 to ~1860: the names said sick or well [D]

American diseases named for what they looked like or did. The name WAS the diagnosis — readable on the body, in plain words.

- **Smallpox** — the war disease of 1776. Washington ordered the inoculation of the Continental Army in 1777 [D]. (Boston had seen inoculation earlier, 1721.) The name says what the body shows: small pox, the raised marks.
- **Yellow fever** — called the **"American plague"** [D]. The 1793 Philadelphia epidemic: first diagnosed August 5 by Benjamin Rush; ~5,000 dead of ~50,000 inhabitants; Washington and Congress fled the capital [D]. *Sources: 4score.org Rush account; spainexchange.com 1793 summary.*
- **Cholera** — spreading from South Asia, struck the United States for the first time in **1831–1832** [D]. *Source: erenow.org Encyclopedia of the New American Nation.*
- **Consumption / phthisis** — tuberculosis. The wasting was the name: phthisis is chronic wasting away; **king's evil** was TB of the neck and glands [D]. *Source: stlgs.org archaic medical terms (Murray 2025).*
- **Throat distemper** — diphtheria. The 1735–1736 New England epidemic: Noah Webster wrote of "a disease among children, commonly called the 'throat distemper,' of a most malignant kind, and by far the most fatal ever known in this country" — "the plague among children"; families lost half their children [D]. Colonial records also called it cynanche, angina, canker, bladders, rattles [D]. *Sources: 3quarksdaily.com (Klass/Smithsonian); glennfay.medium.com; usdeadlyevents.com.*
- **Scarlet fever** — named for the rash, readable on the skin [D]. (Colonial physicians could barely separate it from diphtheria — "sore throats... often unattributable to a specific disease" [D].)
- **Typhoid** — folded into **bilious fever** (typhoid, malaria, or hepatitis — one showing, three modern diseases) [D]. *Source: stlgs.org.*
- **Ague** — malarial fever. The chill was the name [D]. *Source: stlgs.org.*
- **Measles, whooping cough** (*tussis convulsiva*), **mumps** — all swept colonial America in epidemic form [D]. *Source: erenow.org.*
- The rest of the body-readable vocabulary [D]: **brain fever** = meningitis; **lung fever / winter fever** = pneumonia; **dropsy of the brain** = encephalitis; **la grippe** = influenza; **jail fever / camp fever** = typhus; **canine madness** = rabies; **French pox / bad blood** = syphilis; **bladder in throat / malignant sore throat** = diphtheria [D]. *Source: stlgs.org archaic medical terms.*

[LOGIC] The pattern: every name in Era 1 answers his sick-or-well question. The name points at the showing — the yellow skin, the throat membrane, the wasting, the chill. A person who knows the words knows what the Latin... no — knows what the BODY means.

## Era 2 — 1821 to ~1900s: the renaming begins [D]

The first rename was not bacterial but learned: in **1821**, French physician Pierre Bretonneau gave "throat distemper" a new name — ***diphtérite***, from the Greek *diphthera* (leather), for the leathery membrane in the throat [D]. The descriptive American name became a Greek one. *Source: 3quarksdaily.com.*

Then the bacteriologists re-anchored every disease to its microbe, and the renaming became systematic:

- **Consumption → tuberculosis.** Villemin 1865, Koch 1882: the wasting got a bacillus and a Latin name. The showing ("consumption") was retired for the agent (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*) [D].
- **Friedländer's bacillus (1882)** — Carl Friedländer described it in a pneumonia death; for a few years it was called Friedländer's bacillus. In **1885**, Trevisan devised the genus name ***Klebsiella***, honoring Edwin Klebs [D]. The discoverer's name was replaced by a committee honorific. *Source: PMC9562425 (microbial etymology).*
- **KLB → *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*.** Klebs isolated it in 1883, Löffler in 1884; it was called the Klebs–Löffler bacillus (KLB), then renamed *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* [D]. *Source: PMC9562425; dailydosedocumentary.com.*
- **The meningitis bacterium.** Weichselbaum 1887: *Diplococcus intracellularis meningitidis* — "the paired coccus, inside cells, of meningitis" (a description as a name). Renamed ***Neisseria meningitidis***, Albrecht & Ghon **1901** — the showing dropped out, the discoverer's name went in [D]. *Sources: clinicalpub.com; PMC4880075 (Etymologia: Neisseria).*
- **Plague's bacillus — four names for one bug.** *Bacterium pestis* (1894–1900) → *Bacillus pestis* (1900–1923) → *Pasteurella pestis* (1923–~1970) → ***Yersinia pestis*** (van Loghem 1944, adopted ~1970) [D]. The disease the bug causes never changed its name once; the bug changed its four times. *Sources: clinicalmicrobiologyandinfection.org (Zietz & Dunkelberg); en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yersinia_pestis.*
- **"Spanish" influenza, 1918** — named for the country that reported it openly, not the one it came from [D]. (His namology principle: the name encodes politics, not origin.)

[LOGIC] The mechanism of Era 2: the disease name detached from the patient's showing and attached to the laboratory's agent — named for the discoverer (Klebsiella, Yersinia, Neisseria), the shape (Diplococcus, Bacillus), or the committee. Each rename was sold as more accurate data. Each one moved the name one step further from the sick-or-well body.

## Era 3 — 1893 to now: the standardization [D]

- **1893:** the Bertillon Classification of Causes of Death — Jacques Bertillon's committee work from Vienna 1891 — adopted by the International Statistical Institute at its **Chicago** meeting; the International List of Causes of Death begins. Adopted by the American Public Health Association in 1898 for the US, Canada, and Mexico, with revision every ten years [D]. *Source: tandfonline.com (historical development of classification).*
- **1948:** WHO takes over at its creation; **ICD-6 incorporates morbidity for the first time** — no longer just the dead, now the sick too are classified [D]. *Source: whofic.org.za (WHO-FIC history).*
- **1967:** WHO Nomenclature Regulations — member states must use the current ICD for mortality and morbidity statistics [D]. *Source: whofic.org.za.*
- **1990/1994:** ICD-10 endorsed May 1990 by the 43rd World Health Assembly; in use from 1994 [D]. *Source: embryology.med.unsw.edu.au; who.int.*
- **2019/2022:** ICD-11 adopted May 2019; in effect **January 1, 2022** [D]. *Source: who.int.*
- **February 11, 2020:** the split made official — WHO names the disease **COVID-19**; the ICTV names the virus **SARS-CoV-2**, the same day [D]. Disease and agent are now named by two different committees under two different authorities. *Sources: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_naming; asrn.org.*

[LOGIC] The arc: 1893 counts the dead; 1948 classifies the sick; 1967 mandates the classification; 1990–2022 digitizes it. The name becomes a number. The disease and the virus get separate names from separate committees. His thesis reads this as the completion of Era 2's detachment — frozen into law.

## His thesis worked through the arc [H]

- Era 1 said sick-or-well on the body. Era 2 replaced the body's words with the laboratory's Latin. Era 3 froze the replacement into codes and committees.
- The "more accurate data" claim: each rename added precision about the agent and removed information about the showing. Net accuracy for the sick-or-well question — his question — went DOWN, not up [H].
- The reversal [H]: additive naming. Keep "throat distemper" AND "diphtheria" AND "Corynebacterium diphtheriae" AND the ICD code. The old name holds the showing; the new name holds the agent. Both stand. Nothing is stripped.

## My recommendation [mine]

If I have to name when it started getting messed up: **the 1880s–1900s** — the window when the disease name detached from the patient's showing and attached to the laboratory's agent (Klebsiella 1885, the meningococcus 1887→1901, the plague bacillus's four names, KLB's renaming). The ICD then froze that detachment into international law, one revision at a time. His additive-naming thesis is the honest reversal of exactly that move: add, don't strip.
