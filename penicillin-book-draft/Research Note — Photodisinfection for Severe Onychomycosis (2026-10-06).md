# Research Note — Photodisinfection Cleared Severe Onychomycosis in McMaster Cohort Study

**Filed:** 2026-10-06 · For local peer review alongside *The Mycelial Transposition* manuscript
**Source:** Rabinovich, A., et al. "Light Therapy Effectively Treats Onychomycosis and Promotes Healthy Nail Growth in Toenails with Severe Infections: A Longitudinal Cohort Study." *Journal of the American Podiatric Medical Association* 2026, 116(5), 79 — published today (accepted version on MDPI platform)
**Link:** https://www.mdpi.com/1930-8264/116/5/79

## The study

- 165 great toenails in 101 patients (mean age 61) treated with a photodisinfection protocol: topical formula (ToeFX Inc., containing methylthioninium chloride dye plus a botanical extract) followed by 15 minutes of red light (630–670 nm), every 2 weeks for 6 months — 12 sessions total, with debridement of hyperkeratotic nail beforehand.
- 153 of 165 nails (92.7%) had **severe** infections — total dystrophy or dermatophytomas — the forms the authors note are usually *excluded* from onychomycosis trials because they resist therapy.
- Results at 6 months: 120/165 (72.7%) showed ≥3 mm new clear nail or total clearance; 88 (53.3%) showed ≥6 mm or clearance. Mean new clear growth 5.9 ± 4.5 mm. No side effects reported.
- The paper frames this against oral antifungals (~70% mycological, ~40% clinical cure with side effects) and topicals (<20% clinical cure).

## Why it matters for the manuscript

- This sits next to yesterday's terbinafine/MOB-015 note and extends it into **the non-drug quadrant** — same disease (onychomycosis), no drug at all. Your nomenclature chapters argue that classification does policy work: route and class determine what's tried where. A dye-plus-red-light protocol that works via reactive oxygen species disrupting the fungal wall has no drug class, no susceptibility breakpoint, no MIC — it escapes the entire drug taxonomy. A tiered-prescribing framework built only on drug nomenclature has no shelf for it.
- It also inverts the terbinafine note's lesson. There, the lab label (susceptible vs. resistant) failed to predict the outcome — the naming did no work. Here, there is **no label at all**: no pathogen species targeting, no drug classification, just a physical mechanism aimed at the fungal cell wall. Two adjacent examples that both diminish the primacy of drug-class naming — worth deciding in peer review whether they belong in the same chapter.
- The "severe cases were neglected in prior trials" point is a taxonomy-adjacent critique too: eligibility criteria — who a study bothers to name as a patient — shape which treatments get evidence. Severe onychomycosis was invisible to the evidence base because trials defined it away. That is naming doing quiet policy work, and it is yours to say.

## Honest caveats (do not drop these)

1. **Longitudinal cohort, not an RCT.** No control group; treatment history prior to the 30-day washout was self-reported. Promising is not proven.
2. **Commercial involvement.** The topical formula and light source are both ToeFX Inc. products, and a company-affiliated author (Francesca Sarzetto, ToeFX Inc./Canadian Centre for Clinical Trials) selected the measurement points on the photographs — a potential source of bias, even with graph-paper calibration.
3. The outcome is measured **clear-nail growth**, not mycological cure (the paper lists mycological cure as a secondary outcome at 52 weeks — check the full text before citing it).
4. Patients with diabetes were excluded, and ~95% of the severe-case evidence is in great toenails — the generalizability claims should stay narrow.
5. Read the endpoint details before leaning on the 72.7%: "≥3 mm clear nail or total clearance" is a low bar relative to complete cure, and nails with unclear healthy/infected boundaries were excluded from analysis.

## Open questions for peer review

- Where does a non-drug modality live in a reclassification framework — a new top-level category, a sidebar on "mechanism vs. molecule," or a counterexample to drug-centered naming?
- With the MOB-015 note: same disease, opposite directions (topical drug where the label failed vs. no drug where no label exists). Pair them in the resistance-nomenclature chapter, or separate them to let each carry its own point?
