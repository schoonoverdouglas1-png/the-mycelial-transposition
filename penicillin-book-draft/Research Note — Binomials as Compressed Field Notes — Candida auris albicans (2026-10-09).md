# Research Note — Binomials as Compressed Field Notes: Candida, albicans, auris (2026-10-09)

*Captured from Douglas's Oct 9, 2026 chat thread while working through the name behind the C. auris mortality note. For the nomenclature chapters of The Mycelial Transposition. Creative manuscript material — not medical advice.*

## Douglas's starting guess

That the second half of the name described its color and a description of the illness.

## What the names actually encode

- **Candida** (genus): from Latin *candidus*, "shining white" — a colony-appearance note: white, smooth growth on the plate. The genus word is the color note.
- **albicans** (*Candida albicans*): present participle of *albicō*, "becoming white." Paired with the genus, the binomial reads nearly tautologically as "shining white, becoming white" — white on white, a double color note that stuck.
- **auris** (*Candida auris*, now also *Candidozyma auris*): Latin for "ear." First described in 2009 from an isolate taken from the external ear canal of a 70-year-old patient in Japan (Satoh & Makimura). The species epithet is a place-of-discovery note, not an illness note.
- **candidiasis**: the illness word is borrowed downstream from the genus name, not the other way around.

## Why it fits the manuscript argument

Binomials are little compressed field notes — color on a plate, a body site in a Tokyo hospital — that outlive the moment they were written. They keep doing clinical and policy work long after the note stops being accurate: *albicans* doubles its own color claim, *auris* points to an ear while the organism now drives bloodstream outbreaks on multiple continents, and *Candida auris* persists in 2026 papers even after the taxonomic move to *Candidozyma auris*. The name carries the first observation forward and shapes how urgently the organism is seen — the same "name does work" mechanism the Frontiers mortality note's authors hypothesized for vigilance.

## Caveats to keep

- Etymology explains the origin of the label, not current biology or risk — do not read an epithet as a clinical description.
- "First isolated from an ear in Japan, 2009" is the first *description*; retrospective work has suggested earlier archived isolates elsewhere. Keep the distinction.
- This note is nomenclature history for the manuscript, kept separate from Douglas's or Cara's personal medical care.
