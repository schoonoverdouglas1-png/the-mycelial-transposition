## The subject

Pleural pneumonia: pneumonia with the pleura — the lung's wrapping — in it. The paper's three parts, his:

### Part 1 — How it's missed (his thesis, his to evidence)

- The pleural piece hides inside the pneumonia showing: the cough and fever get the name, the pleuritic pain gets filed as "chest pain" and worked up as something else.
- Small effusions missed on exam; the stethoscope hears the lung, not the wrapping.
- His bother threshold (§114): the patient doesn't feel "pleura" — they feel pain on breathing, and the naming stops there.
- [H] throughout — the miss mechanisms are his to evidence; the paper's job is to name them so they can be looked for.

### Part 2 — How it feels (HIS to write)

His words: "I, as a clinical technical person, might have to actually write about what it actually feels like to have some of these things because between what I know and the people I've interviewed, I actually empathically and emotionally know what disease feels like."

- This section is his authorship. Nobody else's. The knife-on-breathing, the couldn't-laugh, the sleeping-sitting-up — whatever it actually felt like, in his words and his interviewees' words.
- The audience, his words: "a lot of doctors, if they haven't experienced it, would love to read it somehow."

### Part 3 — Symptomology

- The full symptom list, his way: what shows, in what order, what it means — the showing read like a text, not a checklist.

- **Rita Charon** (Columbia) — founded narrative medicine; doctors trained to read the patient's story as clinical text.

- **Oliver Sacks** — the clinical tale: the case history written as a human story.
- The illness-narrative genre broadly — patients and doctors writing the felt side.
