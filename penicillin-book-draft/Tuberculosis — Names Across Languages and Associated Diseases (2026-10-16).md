# Tuberculosis — Names Across Languages and Associated Diseases (2026-10-16)

*Compiled at Douglas's direction. Naming reference for the book — never medical advice.*

## One clarification first

There is no standard medical term **"tuberculosis vulgaris."** The *vulgaris*-form of TB is **lupus vulgaris** — tuberculosis of the skin — already filed in the vulgaris family. What follows is everything with a name-association to tuberculosis, across languages and across his three frames: what was felt (sickness), what was observed (illness), what medicine named (disease).

## The pathogen's names

| Name | Story |
|---|---|
| *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* | The binomial. *Mykes* (Greek) = **fungus** + *bacterium* — the "fungus-bacterium," named for its waxy coat that looked fungal under the microscope. A fungal thread inside the TB name itself. |
| Koch's bacillus / Koch's disease | Eponym for Robert Koch, who identified it March 24, 1882 (Nobel 1905). |

## The disease across languages

| Language | Name | Literal meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Latin (modern coinage, Schönlein 1832) | tuberculosis | from *tuberculum*, small nodule/swelling — named for the tubercles in infected tissue |
| Ancient Greek (Hippocrates, ~460 BCE) | phthisis (φθίσις) | wasting, decay |
| Ancient Latin | tabes | wasting, melting away |
| Ancient Hebrew | schachepheth | wasting (CDC's historical listing) |
| English (19th century) | consumption | from Latin *consumere*, to waste away — the patient seemed consumed from within |
| German | Tuberkulose; older *Schwindsucht* | *Schwindsucht* = "wasting sickness" |
| French | tuberculose; older *phtisie* | *phtisie* from Greek phthisis |
| Spanish / Italian | tuberculosis / tubercolosi | the Latin form, kept |
| Russian | туберкулёз (tuberkulyoz) | the Latin form, transliterated |
| Japanese | kekkaku (結核); older *rogai*, *haibyo* | *kekkaku* = nodule; *rogai* = "consuming and coughing"; *haibyo* = lung disease |
| Chinese | 肺结核 (fèi jié hé) | "lung nodule" — lung + nodule, the tuberculum translated |
| Arabic | السل (al-sull) | consumption, wasting |
| Hindi | क्षय रोग (kshay rog) | "wasting disease" |

## Site-specific TB names (the disease named by where it sits)

| Name | What it names |
|---|---|
| Lupus vulgaris | TB of the skin — *lupus* (wolf) for its wolf-like destruction; the vulgaris tag marks the common form |
| Scrofula | TB of the neck lymph nodes — Latin *scrofula*, "brood sow" (swollen glands like a sow's); long believed a *different* disease from lung TB |
| Pott's disease | Spinal TB — eponym for Percivall Pott, who described the collapsed-vertebra (*gibbus*) deformity |
| Miliary tuberculosis | Disseminated TB — Latin *milium*, millet seed; the lesions look like millet seeds |
| Tabes mesenterica | Abdominal TB — the old *tabes* (wasting) + mesentery |
| Tuberculosis verrucosa cutis ("prosector's wart") | Skin TB from handling infected tissue — anatomists', butchers', veterinarians' occupational hazard |
| Ghon's focus / Ghon's complex / Ranke's complex | The primary lung lesion and its lymph node (Anton Ghon); calcified form (Ranke) |
| Rich's focus | Brain granuloma that can rupture into tubercular meningitis (Arnold Rice Rich) |

## Folk and historical names (what people felt and saw)

- **Consumption** — the wasting body, consumed from within
- **White plague** — the pallor of sufferers; the "white" set against the Black Death
- **King's evil** — scrofula, believed curable by a king's touch
- **Wasting disease / decline** — the visible decline
- **"Captain of all these men of death"** — Bunyan's name for consumption

## Namology notes for the book

1. **The nodule word keeps returning.** *Tuberculum* (small nodule) names the disease; his "small protein nodules all bodies seem to have" speak the same Latin. Worth a deliberate echo in the manuscript.
2. **The fungus is inside the name.** *Myco-* = fungus. The TB bacillus was named a "fungus-bacterium" before anyone knew what it was — his fungal thread was there from 1882.
3. **Names changed what it felt like to be sick.** The Gresham history lecture notes the shift from "consumptive" to "tubercular": patients went from passive sufferers embracing fate to warriors enlisted against an invader — "culture, not nature, determines how people *feel* and *act*." That is his symptomology thesis wearing a historian's clothes: the name changed the feeling of the sickness.
4. **One disease, a dozen names, centuries of mistaken identity.** Scrofula was believed a different disease from lung TB; lupus was believed different again. The names split what the organism later proved was one — the exact failure mode his "common ground of names" doctrine is built to prevent.
