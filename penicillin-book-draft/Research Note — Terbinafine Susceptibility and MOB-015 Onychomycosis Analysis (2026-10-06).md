# Research Note — Terbinafine Susceptibility Breakpoints Did Not Predict Cure in MOB-015 Nail-Fungus Re-analysis

**Filed:** 2026-10-06 · For local peer review alongside *The Mycelial Transposition* manuscript
**Source:** Moberg Pharma press release (2026-10-05), announcing a peer-reviewed analysis in *Journal of Fungi* 2026, 12(10), 739, conducted with Prof. Mahmoud Ghannoum, Case Western Reserve University
**Link:** https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/new-analyses-on-terbinafine-susceptibility-and-mob-015-published-in-journal-of-fungi-302898140.html

## The analysis

- Re-analysis of 506 dermatophyte isolates from the completed North American Phase 3 trial of MOB-015 (a topical terbinafine formulation for onychomycosis, nail fungus), given daily for 48 weeks.
- Only 3.2% of baseline isolates showed elevated minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC ≥ 0.5 µg/ml) — i.e., reduced *in vitro* terbinafine susceptibility was rare in this dataset.
- Among MOB-015-treated patients with evaluable end-of-study results, **74.3% reached mycological cure across the full range of baseline terbinafine susceptibility** — including two of three patients whose isolates had elevated baseline MICs.
- The headline claim: baseline susceptibility did not gate the outcome; cure was achieved "across the full range" of susceptibility.

## Why it matters for the manuscript

- A live specimen of the classification argument running in reverse. Your thesis is that precise naming drives better prescribing. Here, the lab classification (susceptible vs. reduced susceptibility by MIC breakpoint) **did not predict the clinical outcome** — the naming did no work. That is worth engaging, not dodging: it sharpens the claim to *which* classifications earn their keep.
- The likely mechanism is itself a classification point: susceptibility breakpoints are calibrated for **systemic exposure**. A topical drug delivered at the nail bed reaches local concentrations far above systemic breakpoints, so "reduced susceptibility" on the lab report can still mean cure at the site. The breakpoint is an artifact of measurement context — a label that encodes an assumption about route of administration. That is squarely your territory: route and class determining what's tried where.
- Pairs with the two earlier notes: the mycobiome note (therapy reshaping ecology), the nanopore note (diagnostics shortening the empiric window). This one is the **taxonomy-of-resistance** note — what a resistance label actually claims, and when it fails to.

## Honest caveats (do not drop these)

1. **Company announcement.** This is Moberg Pharma publicizing its own product's data via press release. The analysis is stated to be peer-reviewed and published in *Journal of Fungi*, but independent reading of the full paper — methods, evaluable-patient definitions, dropout handling — is needed before citing it as established.
2. **Re-analysis, not a new trial.** Post-hoc look at isolates from a completed Phase 3; the cure endpoint is *mycological* (lab-confirmed clearance), not complete clinical cure of the nail.
3. **Terbinafine is a synthetic allylamine**, not a fungal-derived drug like the penicillins your reclassification centers on. The fit is to the classification/tiering argument (what susceptibility labels claim and when they mislead), not to the origin-based claim — do not oversell it as direct support.
4. The topical-vs-systemic exposure point cuts both ways: it does not refute susceptibility testing in general, only breakpoints applied outside their calibrated context. The peer-review question below is the real test.
