## 1. Quest's version of the 23-serotype test — found [D]

Quest Diagnostics offers the direct equivalent of Mayo's PN23M:

- **Test code 16963: Streptococcus pneumoniae IgG Antibody (23 Serotypes), MAID** — the Quest version of the 23-serotype panel.
- **Serotypes included:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9 (9N), 12 (12F), 14, 17 (17F), 19 (19F), 20, 22 (22F), 23 (23F), 26 (6B), 34 (10A), 43 (11A), 51 (7F), 54 (15B), 56 (18C), 57 (19A), 68 (9V), 70 (33F) — **the same 23 serotypes as Mayo's panel**, matching the PPSV23 composition. (The parenthetical numbers are the Danish nomenclature codes; Quest lists them the same way Mayo does.)
- **Method:** MAID — multi-analyte immunodetection using the Luminex flow cytometric system, measuring all analytes simultaneously. Calibrated against the FDA standard reference serum 89-S. Results reported in µg/mL (mcg/mL).
- Quest also offers smaller panels: **16374** (13 serotypes) and **19564** (14 serotypes), same MAID method.
- Mayo's PN23M, for comparison: bead-based multiplex immunoassay, serum specimen, results in mcg/mL, reference ≥1.0 mcg/mL per serotype.

**A documented difference between the labs, stated plainly:** Quest's own FAQ on these tests (FAQS.181, Version 1, **effective 10/19/2017 — squarely in his timeframe**) notes that while serologic correlates of protection are not rigorously established, WHO-aligned data suggest protection from invasive disease around ≥0.3–0.5 µg/mL for healthy children after conjugate vaccination, and that **expert opinion suggests a cutoff of ≥1.3 µg/mL may be more relevant after pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccines and for immunocompromised patients** — a higher bar than Mayo's ≥1.0. Same test, different lab, different interpretive numbers on the page. That is the documented record, not a verdict on anyone.

## 2. Quest's susceptibility testing for S. pneumoniae — found [D]

- **Test code 6641: Susceptibility Panel, Aerobic Bacterium** — susceptibility testing on a pure culture isolate; method varies (MIC via CPT 87186, disk via 87184, or E-test via 87181). Specimen: pure culture isolate on solid agar. Quest's stated clinical significance: susceptibility "can indicate the relative success or failure that may be expected if this agent is used to treat that organism" and can guide therapy choice.
- **Test code 651: Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)** — "Aerobic MIC," microbroth dilution method, on pure bacterial culture (room temperature, 48-hour stability; frozen rejected).
- **The honest limit:** no pneumococcus-specific susceptibility panel was found in Quest's directory. Susceptibility is organism-based — it runs on the cultured isolate, not on the serotype or the antibody test. The serotype antibody test (16963) measures the patient's IgG response; the susceptibility test measures the cultured bacterium's drug response. **Two different specimens, two different questions.** The antibody panel never reports susceptibility, and the susceptibility panel never reports serotypes.

## 4. Azoles and encephalitis: what the literature actually says — kept distinct [D]

Two separate bodies of fact. They do not blur.

**A. Azoles in fungal CNS infections — documented [D].**
Azoles inhibit ergosterol synthesis in fungal cell membranes — antifungals, not antivirals. Their documented CNS roles:
- **Fluconazole in cryptococcal meningitis** (IDSA guidelines): consolidation therapy 400 mg daily for a minimum of 8 weeks after induction, then maintenance 200 mg daily for at least 12 months. Fluconazole is the backbone of the consolidation and maintenance phases.
- **Voriconazole** has superior CNS penetration among azoles; the CDC's 2012 interim guidance for the contaminated-steroid fungal CNS outbreak recommended voriconazole (6 mg/kg every 12 hours, trough 2–5 mcg/mL) for fungal meningitis/parameningeal infection, with liposomal amphotericin B added for severe disease. Voriconazole is the documented azole for CNS aspergillosis.
- Itraconazole penetrates CSF poorly and is an inferior alternative in cryptococcal maintenance (higher relapse risk) — documented.

**B. Azoles in viral (encephalitis-panel) encephalitis — no established role [D].**
- **Herpes simplex encephalitis:** IV acyclovir, 14–21 days — the documented treatment. Not an azole.
- **Arboviral encephalitis** (West Nile, EEE, SLE, WEE, La Crosse/California serogroup — the organisms on the encephalitis panels): **supportive care; no established antiviral or azole treatment.** This is the documented state of the field.
- Autoimmune encephalitis: immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, plasma exchange, rituximab) — not azoles.

**The honest line between them:** azoles are documented treatment for *fungal* infections of the CNS. The encephalitis panels test for *viral* (and autoimmune) causes. A statement that azoles treat "each of those encephalitis positives" would only make documented sense if the positives were fungal — and no such Quest statement was found. His account stands as his.
