## Part I — Group 1, rows 1–20 (Tuberculosis through Anthrax)

### Tuberculosis
- **Fungal connections:** [D] Residual cavitary disease after treated pulmonary TB is the classic bed for *Aspergillus* colonization — aspergilloma ("fungus ball") and chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis, with hemoptysis as the signature complication [D — Wikipedia "Aspergilloma"; Uzair et al., SAGE Open Med Case Rep 2024]. [D] Histoplasmosis mimics TB clinically and radiographically and is a documented diagnostic confounder in endemic regions [D — IDSA histoplasmosis context, StatPearls]. No direct fungal co-pathogen of *M. tuberculosis* itself is documented at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection to tuberculosis per se at 88%. (Yeast/fungal opportunism in TB patients tracks with HIV co-infection, which is the AIDS row's territory, not TB's.)
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] *M. tuberculosis* is itself the bacterium of record; documented secondary bacterial pneumonias in TB patients (e.g., *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*) occur with cavitary lung disease [D — StatPearls/Merck Manual TB complications]. Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (*M. avium* complex) can co-exist in structurally damaged lungs [D — ATS mycobacterial guideline].
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC/ATS/IDSA 2016 updated treatment guideline; CDC 2025 update] Drug-susceptible TB: 2 months isoniazid + rifampin + pyrazinamide + ethambutol (2HRZE), then 4 months isoniazid + rifampin (4HR), total 6 months. New 4-month option: 2 months isoniazid + rifapentine + pyrazinamide + moxifloxacin, then 2 months isoniazid + rifapentine + moxifloxacin (2HPZM/2HPM) for adults/adolescents ≥12 years. MDR/rifampin-resistant TB: all-oral 6-month BPaLM (bedaquiline + pretomanid + linezolid + moxifloxacin) or BPaL if fluoroquinolone resistance.

### Streptococcus pneumoniae (drug-resistant invasive)
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal co-pathogen or mimic relationship with invasive pneumococcal disease at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Post-influenza secondary bacterial pneumonia is the classic pattern: *S. pneumoniae* follows influenza virus damage to airways, and it co-occurs with *S. aureus* (incl. MRSA), *H. influenzae*, and *M. catarrhalis* in community-acquired pneumonia [D — IDSA/ATS community-acquired pneumonia guideline context]. In meningitis it is one of several bacterial differentials (N. meningitidis, Listeria, H. influenzae) [D — IDSA bacterial meningitis guideline 2004].
- **Medication choices:** [D — IDSA meningitis guideline 2004; IDSA CAP summaries] Empiric meningitis: vancomycin (15–20 mg/kg IV q12h) + ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h (or cefotaxime 2 g IV q6h); add rifampin 600 mg BID in dual penicillin/cephalosporin resistance; duration 10–14 days. Non-meningitic invasive disease: high-dose amoxicillin (susceptible), or ceftriaxone/cefotaxime; resistant strains: respiratory fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), vancomycin, or linezolid [D — IDSA CAP guidance]. Prevention is vaccine-based (PCV/PPSV), but no antibiotic prophylaxis is standard.

### Measles
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection to measles at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Measles's documented bacterial sequelae are secondary bacterial pneumonia (often *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, *H. influenzae*) and acute otitis media — the leading causes of measles mortality in children [D — NEJM measles review, Strebel et al.]. The virus itself also causes giant-cell pneumonia directly.
- **Medication choices:** [D — NEJM measles review; CDC measles guidance] No specific antiviral; management is supportive plus vitamin A — AAP recommends two age-specific doses (200,000 IU ≥12 mo; 100,000 IU 6–11 mo; 50,000 IU <6 mo), with a third dose 2–4 weeks later if deficiency signs persist. Antibiotics only when secondary bacterial pneumonia, otitis media, or sepsis is diagnosed. Post-exposure prophylaxis: MMR within 72 hours of exposure, or immune globulin within 6 days for high-risk persons [D — CDC measles PEP guidance].

### AIDS
- **Fungal connections:** [D] AIDS-defining fungal opportunists: cryptococcal meningitis (*Cryptococcus neoformans*), Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP, a fungal pathogen), invasive aspergillosis, histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, and talaromycosis (penicilliosis) — all tracked in the DHHS opportunistic-infection guidelines [D — DHHS OI guidelines; IDSA 2016 cryptococcal guideline].
- **Yeast connections:** [D] Oropharyngeal and esophageal candidiasis are among the most common AIDS-associated yeast infections; recurrent vulvovaginal candidiasis is also documented [D — DHHS OI guidelines]. *Candida* esophagitis is an AIDS-defining condition.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Recurrent bacterial pneumonia (*S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*), tuberculosis and *M. avium* complex, non-typhoidal salmonellosis, and syphilis co-infection are all documented bacterial burdens in advanced HIV [D — DHHS OI guidelines].
- **Medication choices:** [D — DHHS antiretroviral guidelines; StatPearls AIDS ART review] Antiretroviral therapy (ART): preferred initial regimens are integrase-strand-transfer-inhibitor (InSTI)–based — bictegravir/tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine (BIC/TAF/FTC) or dolutegravir + TAF/FTC (or TAF/lamivudine). Drug classes: NRTIs, NNRTIs, protease inhibitors, InSTIs, CCR5/fusion/entry inhibitors. Second-line after InSTI failure: boosted protease inhibitor (e.g., darunavir/ritonavir) + 2 NRTIs, guided by resistance testing. Opportunistic fungal/yeast disease is treated with its own antifungals (e.g., fluconazole for candidiasis; amphotericin B + flucytosine induction then fluconazole for cryptococcal meningitis) [D — IDSA cryptococcal guideline].

### Meningococcal disease
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen of *N. meningitidis* invasive disease at 88%; it is typically a monomicrobial invasive infection.
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC meningococcal clinical guidance; CDC Pink Book ch. 14; IDSA meningitis guideline] Empiric therapy: extended-spectrum cephalosporin (ceftriaxone or cefotaxime). Once susceptibility is confirmed, penicillin G or ampicillin; treatment duration ~7 days per IDSA meningitis guidance. Chemoprophylaxis for close contacts (ideally <24 h): ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone, or rifampin (90–95% effective at eradicating nasopharyngeal carriage); since 2024, CDC advises preferring ceftriaxone/rifampin/azithromycin over ciprofloxacin in areas with ciprofloxacin-resistant strains [D — CDC MMWR 2024].

### Hepatitis B
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen or secondary bacterial infection of hepatitis B at 88%.
- **Medication choices:** [D — AASLD hepatitis B guidelines; standard-of-care reviews] Chronic HBV first-line: nucleos(t)ide analogues with high resistance barrier — entecavir, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), or tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), usually indefinite. Alternative: pegylated interferon-α for a finite 48-week course in selected patients (finite duration, higher seroconversion, but poorer tolerability; contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis). Older agents (lamivudine, adefovir, telbivudine) are supplanted by resistance concerns. Acute hepatitis B: supportive care only. HBV vaccine and hepatitis B immune globulin are the preventive/post-exposure tools.

### Rabies
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented bacterial co-pathogen connection to rabies encephalitis itself at 88%. (Wound management includes tetanus prophylaxis per wound-care protocol [D — CDC rabies PEP materials], but that is wound care, not a rabies–bacterium connection.)
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC/ACIP; Public Health Agency of Canada rabies guidance] No curative treatment once symptoms appear — care is supportive and the disease is nearly 100% fatal after onset. Prevention is everything: wound washing (15 min with soap and water), then post-exposure prophylaxis for the unvaccinated — human rabies immune globulin 20 IU/kg infiltrated into/around the wound (within 7 days of vaccine start) plus a 4-dose vaccine series (days 0, 3, 7, 14); previously vaccinated persons receive vaccine only (days 0 and 3), no immune globulin.

### Diphtheria
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Cutaneous diphtheria wounds are frequently polymicrobial, with *C. diphtheriae* co-isolated alongside *S. aureus* and *S. pyogenes* in chronic skin lesions [D — CDC diphtheria surveillance manual context]. Respiratory diphtheria itself is monomicrobial toxin disease.
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC diphtheria clinical guidance; CDC surveillance manual ch. 1] Respiratory diphtheria: diphtheria antitoxin (DAT, equine; obtained from CDC) given on clinical suspicion without awaiting culture, PLUS erythromycin or penicillin for 14 days (e.g., erythromycin 40 mg/kg/day max 2 g/day; procaine penicillin G IM). Cutaneous diphtheria: antibiotics (erythromycin or penicillin × 14 days); antitoxin rarely needed. Close contacts: antimicrobial prophylaxis — single IM benzathine penicillin G or erythromycin 7–10 days — plus toxoid vaccine updating.

### Poliomyelitis
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented bacterial co-pathogen connection at 88%.
- **Medication choices:** [D — BMJ Best Practice; NETEC polio guidance 2023] No FDA-approved antiviral or specific treatment for poliovirus infection. Management is supportive: monitoring for respiratory failure and rapid weakness progression, neurology/ID consultation, and early rehabilitation (physiotherapy, occupational, speech/swallowing therapy). Post-polio syndrome likewise has no disease-modifying drug. Prevention: inactivated (IPV, Salk) and oral (OPV, Sabin) poliovirus vaccines.

### Yellow fever
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] No characteristic co-pathogen, but secondary bacterial infections are documented complications of severe yellow fever and are treated with antibiotics per WHO/PAHO guidance [D — WHO yellow fever fact sheet; PAHO yellow fever topic page]. (Note the author's six-track interest: jaundice/severe cases can resemble leptospirosis, a bacterial disease, as a diagnostic differential [D — WHO].)
- **Medication choices:** [D — WHO yellow fever fact sheet; CDC Yellow Book] No specific antiviral; management is supportive — rest, hydration, management of liver and kidney failure, fever control, with hospitalization (often ICU) for jaundice/severe disease. Aspirin and NSAIDs are contraindicated (bleeding risk). A CDC systematic review found none of the candidate specific treatments (ribavirin, sofosbuvir, interferon-α, plasma exchange, liver transplant) improved outcomes [D — CDC Yellow Book]. Prevention: single-dose 17D vaccine gives lifelong immunity.

### Pertussis
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Secondary bacterial infections documented in pertussis — sinusitis, otitis media, bronchitis, pneumonia — with pathogens *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, *H. influenzae*, and *M. catarrhalis*, because *B. pertussis* denudes ciliated epithelium and impairs local phagocytosis [D — Mandell-type Bordetella reference, oncohemakey].
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC 2005 pertussis treatment guideline (MMWR RR-14); CDC pertussis clinical care] First-line: macrolides — azithromycin (preferred, incl. infants), clarithromycin, or erythromycin; alternative: trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Same agents/doses for post-exposure prophylaxis of high-risk contacts. Antibiotics eradicate nasopharyngeal carriage and shorten infectiousness (to ~5 days of treatment) but do not substantially alter the paroxysmal cough course once established. Ampicillin, amoxicillin, fluoroquinolones, and cephalosporins are NOT recommended.

### Tetanus
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented specific bacterial co-pathogen of tetanus toxin disease at 88%. (The portal is typically a soil-contaminated, polymicrobial wound, but no named co-pathogen relationship is documented in guidelines.)
- **Medication choices:** [D — Tintinalli's Emergency Medicine; Drugs.com TIG monograph; Hesperian/WHO-aligned protocols] Active tetanus: human tetanus immune globulin (TIG) to neutralize unbound toxin (treatment doses 3,000–6,000 U IM in classic texts; 500 IU commonly used in modern practice — the toxin already bound to neurons is unaffected), metronidazole 500 mg IV q6h preferred as the antibiotic (penicillin G is an alternative but may worsen spasms via GABA antagonism) for 10–14 days, benzodiazepines (diazepam/midazolam) for spasm control, wound debridement, and ICU support with ventilation. Recovery does NOT confer immunity — active tetanus toxoid vaccination is still required [D — Drugs.com TIG monograph].

### Mumps

- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.

### Hepatitis A

### Hepatitis C
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen or secondary bacterial infection at 88%.
- **Medication choices:** [D — AASLD/IDSA HCV guidance; WHO 2022 chronic HCV guideline; 2025 KASL guideline] Pan-genotypic direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) are the standard: sofosbuvir/velpatasvir × 12 weeks, or glecaprevir/pibrentasvir × 8 weeks for treatment-naïve patients without cirrhosis or with compensated cirrhosis. DAA classes: NS3/4A protease inhibitors, NS5A inhibitors, NS5B polymerase inhibitors. For DAA-experienced or resistant cases: sofosbuvir/velpatasvir/voxilaprevir. Sustained virologic response rates ≥95%; interferon-based therapy is obsolete. No vaccine exists.

### Smallpox
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] Secondary bacterial infections of skin lesions are documented smallpox complications (alongside sepsis, corneal ulceration, encephalitis) [D — emDocs ToxCard: Smallpox].
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC smallpox clinical treatment page; FDA smallpox preparedness page] Mainstay is supportive care (hydration, nutrition, secondary-infection prevention). FDA-approved antivirals: tecovirimat (TPOXX, oral, 14 days weight-based, approved 2018) and brincidofovir (Tembexa, two weekly oral doses on days 1 and 8, approved 2021) — both stockpiled in the Strategic National Stockpile; cidofovir has anti-orthopoxvirus activity but is not FDA-approved for smallpox. Vaccination within 2–3 days of exposure can prevent or lessen disease [D — DoD public-health reference sheet]. (Smallpox declared eradicated 1980; no natural cases since 1977 [D — WHO/FDA].)

### Cholera
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen at 88%. (Note: zinc supplementation, 20 mg/day × 10–14 days in children 6 mo–5 yr, is documented adjunct care for watery diarrhea [D — GTFCC/choleraoutbreak.org].)
- **Medication choices:** [D — WHO; Cochrane review "Antimicrobial drugs for treating cholera" (PMC4468928); GTFCC technical note 2022] Rehydration is the mainstay — oral rehydration salts for mild/moderate dehydration, IV Ringer's lactate for severe dehydration; antibiotics are adjunct for severe cases (halving stool volume/duration): first-line doxycycline single oral dose 300 mg (adults), or azithromycin 20 mg/kg single dose (max 1 g) where tetracycline resistance is documented; tetracycline 500 mg q6h × 3 days is the older alternative. Choice is guided by local susceptibility testing due to documented resistance to tetracyclines, cotrimoxazole, and fluoroquinolones.

### Plague
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen at 88%; secondary pneumonic plague is a progression of *Y. pestis* itself, not a second organism. Buboes respond to antibiotics and should not be incised/drained routinely [D — WikEM/CDC clinician guidance].
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC 2021 plague treatment guideline (MMWR); CDC clinician guidance] First-line: aminoglycosides (gentamicin 5 mg/kg IV/IM daily, or streptomycin) or fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin); doxycycline is first-line-acceptable especially for bubonic plague. Duration 10–14 days total (transition IV→oral when improving). Dual therapy with two distinct classes is recommended for bioterrorism scenarios with possible engineered resistance. Post-exposure prophylaxis: doxycycline or ciprofloxacin. Chloramphenicol or TMP-SMX are considered effective alternatives in special situations.

### Anthrax
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] No documented fungal connection at 88%.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] No documented yeast connection at 88%.
- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] No documented characteristic bacterial co-pathogen at 88%; anthrax is monomicrobial *B. anthracis* toxin disease.
- **Medication choices:** [D — CDC 2023 anthrax prevention/treatment guideline (MMWR RR-6); StatPearls anthrax infection] Uncomplicated cutaneous: ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO q12h or doxycycline 100 mg PO q12h (14 days if no systemic signs). Systemic/inhalational/GI/injection anthrax: IV combination therapy — one bactericidal agent (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, or meropenem) PLUS one protein-synthesis inhibitor (clindamycin or linezolid; doxycycline), ≥14 days IV then oral to complete 60 days total. Add antitoxin if toxemia is suspected: raxibacumab 40 mg/kg IV single dose, obiltoxaximab 16 mg/kg IV single dose, or anthrax immune globulin (Anthrasil). Post-exposure prophylaxis: doxycycline 100 mg q12h, ciprofloxacin 500 mg q12h, or levofloxacin 500 mg daily × 60 days plus anthrax vaccine series.

## Part II — Group 1, rows 21–42 (Haemophilus influenzae through Dysentery)

### Haemophilus influenzae (invasive)

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — No documented fungal co-pathogen or secondary fungal relationship in invasive H. influenzae disease (meningitis, bacteremia, epiglottitis) in the standard literature. Fungal coinfection is not a recognized feature of the disease course.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — No documented yeast connection (Candida or otherwise) specific to invasive H. influenzae disease.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — H. influenzae type b is itself a bacterium; invasive disease can follow viral upper-respiratory infection (e.g., concurrent otitis media), and beta-lactamase-producing strains historically co-circulated with pneumococci in meningitis cohorts [D — Merck Manual / StatPearls Hib chapters]. Post-meningitis bacterial superinfection is uncommon.
- **Medication choices:** [D] — Third-generation cephalosporins (cefotaxime or ceftriaxone) are first-line for Hib meningitis; empiric therapy adds ampicillin per IDSA bacterial meningitis guidance until the organism is identified [D — IDSA bacterial meningitis guidelines]. Non-meningeal invasive disease: ampicillin (if beta-lactamase negative), amoxicillin-clavulanate, or a fluoroquinolone for resistant strains [D — StatPearls]. Chemoprophylaxis for household contacts: rifampin for 4 days per CDC [D — CDC]. Dexamethasone is used adjunctively in meningitis. This is a reference listing, not a recommendation.

### Group A streptococcal disease (invasive)

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — No documented fungal co-pathogen in invasive Group A streptococcal disease (necrotizing fasciitis, streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, bacteremia).
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — No documented yeast connection specific to invasive GAS disease.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — Polymicrobial necrotizing fasciitis can co-involve GAS with Staphylococcus aureus or anaerobes [D — IDSA skin/soft-tissue infection guidance]; GAS pharyngitis has documented co-detection with other respiratory pathogens in surveillance studies, though clinical significance is unclear [D — CDC Group A strep pages].
- **Medication choices:** [D] — Penicillin or amoxicillin is first-line for all GAS infections; no penicillin-resistant GAS has ever been documented [D — IDSA / CDC]. For invasive disease (necrotizing fasciitis, toxic shock syndrome), guidelines add clindamycin to penicillin to suppress toxin production, and recommend aggressive surgical debridement [D — IDSA necrotizing fasciitis / STSS guidance]. Severe beta-lactam allergy: vancomycin is the alternative for invasive disease [D — IDSA]. This is a reference listing, not a recommendation.

### Salmonellosis

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — No documented fungal co-pathogen or secondary fungal relationship in Salmonella gastroenteritis or bacteremia.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — No documented yeast connection specific to salmonellosis.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — Bacteremic Salmonella can seed other sites (osteomyelitis, endovascular infection) as a documented complication, particularly in immunocompromised hosts [D — IDSA]; it is a consequence of the same bacterium rather than a second organism. Co-carriage with other enteric pathogens occurs but is not a defining feature.
- **Medication choices:** [D] — Uncomplicated Salmonella gastroenteritis: supportive rehydration only — antibiotics are NOT indicated and may prolong carriage [D — IDSA]. Severe or bacteremic disease: fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin), azithromycin, or third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone) are the documented classes [D — IDSA / WHO]. Resistance note: multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant non-typhoidal Salmonella are a documented global problem, narrowing fluoroquinolone reliability [D — WHO]. This is a reference listing, not a recommendation.

### Typhoid fever

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — No documented fungal co-pathogen in typhoid fever (Salmonella Typhi infection).
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — No documented yeast connection specific to typhoid fever.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — Intestinal perforation can produce polymicrobial peritonitis [D — surgical literature]; S. Typhi itself is the sole driver. No documented bacterial co-pathogen defines the disease course.
- **Medication choices:** [D] — Azithromycin is the documented first-line agent in regions with multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant S. Typhi (e.g., South Asia) [D — WHO / IDSA]. Ceftriaxone is the documented first-line where XDR has not reached [D — WHO]. Fluoroquinolones are documented only where the isolate is confirmed susceptible, after widespread resistance emerged [D — WHO]. Chloramphenicol is historical and largely displaced [D — literature]. Dexamethasone is documented adjunctively for severe typhoid with delirium or shock [D — literature]. Documented prevention: typhoid vaccines (Vi polysaccharide, Ty21a, typhoid conjugate) plus safe food/water practices [D — WHO / CDC]. This is a reference listing, not a recommendation.

### E. coli O157:H7

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — No documented fungal connection in Shiga toxin-producing E. coli O157:H7 disease (hemorrhagic colitis, hemolytic uremic syndrome).
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — No documented yeast connection specific to O157:H7 disease.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — Secondary bacterial complications (e.g., catheter-associated bloodstream infection during HUS care) are hospital-course risks, not co-pathogens of the disease itself; none is documented as characteristic.
- **Medication choices:** [D] — Antibiotics are generally CONTRAINDICATED in O157:H7 infection: they are documented to increase the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome, likely via increased Shiga toxin release [D — IDSA / CDC]. Antimotility agents (loperamide) are likewise documented as contraindicated in bloody diarrhea of unclear cause [D — IDSA]. Care is supportive: aggressive fluid management, monitoring for HUS, dialysis as needed [D — IDSA]. Documented prevention: thorough cooking of ground beef, pasteurized milk, and hand hygiene [D — CDC]. This is a reference listing, not a recommendation.

### Botulism

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — No documented fungal connection in botulism (Clostridium botulinum intoxication); it is a preformed-toxin disease, not an infection of the patient in most forms.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — No documented yeast connection specific to botulism.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — C. botulinum spores themselves colonize in infant botulism and wound botulism [D — CDC]; wound botulism may be polymicrobial at the wound site [D — CDC]. Antibiotics do not neutralize preformed toxin and play no role against it [D — CDC].
- **Medication choices:** [D] — The documented treatment is antitoxin plus intensive supportive care (airway management, mechanical ventilation): heptavalent botulinum antitoxin (HBAT, equine-derived, FDA-approved 2013) for non-infant botulism, obtained only through CDC/state health departments [D — CDC botulism clinical guidance]. Infant botulism: human-derived botulism immune globulin (BabyBIG) via the California Infant Botulism Treatment and Prevention Program [D — CDC]. Antitoxin binds only free toxin — it does not reverse established paralysis, so early administration is documented as critical [D — CDC / Poisoning & Drug Overdose]. Wound botulism adds penicillin or metronidazole AFTER antitoxin [D — CDC]. This is a reference listing, not a recommendation.

### Legionellosis

### Lyme disease

- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — No documented yeast connection in Lyme disease.

### Hansen disease (leprosy)

### Relapsing fever

### Toxic-shock syndromes (staphylococcal + streptococcal)

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — no documented primary fungal co-pathogen; TSS is toxin-mediated, not fungal. [D] Secondary fungal superinfections can follow the prolonged broad-spectrum therapy these cases require — documented general principle, not a primary link.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] primary; [D] secondary Candida infections are a documented consequence of broad-spectrum ICU antibiotic courses used in TSS management.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — TSS IS bacterial: staphylococcal (TSST-1–producing *S. aureus*) and streptococcal (group A *S. pyogenes*, often with necrotizing fasciitis). [D] Secondary gram-negative bacteremia can occur during shock states; empiric regimens therefore cover gram-negatives initially (StatPearls NBK459345; MSD Manual).

[D] Anti-toxin protein-synthesis inhibitor is mandatory: clindamycin 600–900 mg IV q8h (or linezolid) PLUS a bactericidal agent — vancomycin (MRSA risk) or nafcillin/oxacillin for MSSA; penicillin G + clindamycin for streptococcal TSS. [D] IVIG 1–2 g/kg single dose as adjunct in severe/refractory disease (observational evidence; IDSA-aligned practice). [D] Treat 7–14 days per StatPearls/MSD. ### Hemolytic uremic syndrome (postdiarrheal)

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — no documented fungal co-pathogen or secondary fungal role found for STEC-associated HUS.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — no documented yeast link.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — postdiarrheal HUS follows bacterial infection with Shiga-toxin–producing *E. coli* (O157:H7 most classic) or *Shigella dysenteriae* type 1 — the bacteria are the trigger, not a co-pathogen. [D] Antibiotics are NOT given for the STEC phase — documented to risk worsening outcomes (UK NHS guidance; nephrology consensus).

### Pediatric HIV infection

- **Fungal connections:** [D] — opportunistic fungal infections are documented hallmarks of pediatric HIV: *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PCP), cryptococcal disease, endemic mycoses (OIs section of DHHS pediatric HIV guidance; NIH OI guidelines).

### Brucellosis

### Malaria

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — no documented primary fungal co-pathogen; [H→D] partial: severe-malaria case literature contains rare secondary fungal infections (mucormycosis/aspergillosis case reports), but these are isolated, not a documented pattern — marked partial, not established.

[D] Second-line: atovaquone-proguanil; quinine + doxycycline (or clindamycin if doxycycline contraindicated). [D] Severe malaria: IV artesunate immediately, then complete an oral ACT course. [D] Resistance watch: Pfkelch13 artemisinin partial resistance documented in African countries (WHO 2024 strategy). Sources: WHO Guidelines for Malaria (30 Nov 2024); WHO MFT implementation guide (2024).

### Trichinosis

- **Bacterial connections:** [GAP] — trichinosis is helminthic (*Trichinella spiralis*); no documented bacterial co-pathogen role.

### Chancroid

- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — no documented yeast link.

### Ophthalmia neonatorum

### Coccidioidomycosis

- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — secondary bacterial superinfection of coccidioidal cavities is documented in clinical practice; the disease itself is not bacterial.
- **Medication choices:** [D] IDSA 2016 (reaffirmed in practice): mild–moderate pulmonary — fluconazole 400 mg/day PO or itraconazole 200 mg PO BID (preferred for skeletal disease), typically 3–6 months, up to 12+ months. ### Staphylococcus aureus disease
- **Fungal connections:** Documented fungal co-pathogens of *S. aureus* disease itself are uncommon [D]. In the literature, the documented mixed infections with this organism tend to involve *Candida* species rather than filamentous fungi — see the yeast line. Rare case reports exist of *S. aureus* plus *Aspergillus* in severe necrotizing pneumonia (including PVL-producing strains), but these are exceptional rather than systematic [D].
- **Yeast connections:** The documented *S. aureus*–*Candida albicans* mixed biofilm is one of the best-characterized polymicrobial pairings in the literature: the two organisms co-colonize indwelling catheters and devices, and mixed bloodstream infections show increased virulence and antimicrobial tolerance [D]. The documented *S. aureus*–*Candidozyma auris* biofilm pairing (see that row) is the same phenomenon across species [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** The disease IS the bacterium [D]. Documented polymicrobial wound, device, and bloodstream infections include co-pathogens such as streptococci and gram-negative rods [D]; documented toxin-mediated syndromes (toxic shock, scalded skin) are *S. aureus*-driven [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per IDSA guidance: methicillin-susceptible *S. aureus* (MSSA) — nafcillin or oxacillin IV, or cefazolin, first-line [D]. MRSA — vancomycin (with documented trough/AUC monitoring), daptomycin (documented as not for pneumonia due to surfactant inactivation), linezolid, or ceftaroline, per the IDSA 2011 MRSA guideline and subsequent updates [D]. Documented outpatient MRSA skin-infection options: TMP-SMX, doxycycline, or clindamycin [D]. Duration is syndrome-specific in the literature (weeks for bacteremia/endocarditis) [D]; documented decolonization strategies (mupirocin, chlorhexidine) exist for recurrent disease [D].

### Aspergillosis
- **Fungal connections:** The fungus IS the disease — invasive pulmonary, chronic, and allergic forms are caused by *Aspergillus* spp., chiefly *A. fumigatus* [D]. This is one of the manuscript's six-track fungus-track anchors [D]. Documented azole resistance (environmental TR34/L98H mutations in *A. fumigatus*) is a growing documented problem in parts of Europe and Asia [D].
- **Yeast connections:** Documented mixed fungal infections (*Aspergillus* plus *Candida* spp.) occur in severely immunocompromised patients, particularly in airway colonization contexts [D]; the yeast is a co-pathogen here, not the disease itself.
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented co-infection with *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* in cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis airways, where the two organisms interact in chronic biofilms [D]; documented secondary bacterial infections complicate severe viral-associated aspergillosis cases [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per the IDSA 2016 aspergillosis guideline: voriconazole is first-line for invasive aspergillosis (loading 6 mg/kg IV q12h × 2, then 4 mg/kg q12h, transitioning to oral; therapeutic drug monitoring documented as recommended) [D]. Documented alternatives: isavuconazole (non-inferior in the SECURE trial), posaconazole, or liposomal amphotericin B for voriconazole intolerance/failure [D]. Salvage/combination: an echinocandin (caspofungin) added to azole or polyene in select severe/refractory disease [D]. Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA): systemic corticosteroids plus itraconazole per documented regimens [D]. Minimum 6–12 weeks for invasive pulmonary disease in guidelines [D].

### COVID-19 — paired with SARS-CoV AND SARS-CoV-2 per §217 [H]
- **Fungal connections:** His pairing rule [H]: both viruses are presented together. Documented — COVID-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (CAPA) occurs in mechanically ventilated COVID-19 ICU patients, with reported incidence of roughly 2–28% across studies and elevated mortality (ECMM/ISHAM 2020 consensus criteria; APICOVID, UK, and other prospective cohorts) [D]. Documented — COVID-associated mucormycosis ("CAM"), a major documented epidemic in India during the Delta wave, linked in the literature to corticosteroid use, uncontrolled diabetes, and the viral illness [D]. Documented — cryptococcal and *Pneumocystis* co-infections in ventilated/severe COVID-19 cases [D]. On the paired SARS-CoV side, documented secondary *Aspergillus* infections occurred in SARS ICU patients in 2003 [D].
- **Yeast connections:** Documented candidemia and invasive *Candida* bloodstream infections in prolonged ICU COVID-19 stays [D]; documented *Candidozyma auris* outbreaks on COVID-19 wards and ICUs, facilitated by strain on infection-control resources [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented secondary bacterial pneumonia in severe COVID-19 — *S. aureus* (including MRSA), *S. pneumoniae*, *Klebsiella* spp., and ventilator-associated bacterial infections [D]; documented guidelines caution against routine antibacterials without confirmed/suspected bacterial infection [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per NIH COVID-19 guidelines: nirmatrelvir/ritonavir for early mild-moderate disease in high-risk patients [D]; remdesivir for hospitalized or early disease [D]; dexamethasone for patients requiring supplemental oxygen [D]; IL-6 receptor blockers (tocilizumab) and baricitinib for progressive severe disease [D]. Antifungals are documented for CAPA/CAM per the invasive-fungal guidelines above, and antibacterials only for confirmed/suspected secondary infection — not routine [D].

### Mpox
- **Fungal connections:** No documented systematic fungal co-pathogen connection in the literature [GAP]. The documented severe-disease literature focuses on bacterial superinfection and viral dissemination rather than fungal co-pathogens [D].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link in the literature [GAP].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented bacterial superinfection of mpox skin lesions — staphylococcal and streptococcal cellulitis, abscesses, and sepsis reported in the 2022–2024 outbreak literature [D]; documented severe proctitis and pharyngitis complications in the clade IIb outbreak [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per CDC: tecovirimat (TPOXX) is available under an expanded-access investigational protocol for severe mpox or patients at risk of severe disease [D]; the randomized STOMP trial of tecovirimat was documented as conducted during the outbreak [D]. Brincidofovir and cidofovir have documented investigational use in severe cases [D]; vaccinia immune globulin (VIG) is documented for severe disease in select cases [D]. Most cases are managed with supportive care per guidelines [D]; the JYNNEOS vaccine is documented for prevention and post-exposure prophylaxis [D].

### Mucormycosis
- **Fungal connections:** The fungus IS the disease — caused by Mucorales molds (*Rhizopus*, *Mucor*, *Lichtheimia* spp.) [D]. Documented clinical forms include rhino-orbital-cerebral, pulmonary, cutaneous, and disseminated disease [D]. COVID-associated mucormycosis (CAM) is the documented bridge to the COVID-19 row [D].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented systematic yeast pairing [GAP]; mixed mold infections (mucormycosis plus aspergillosis) are rarely documented [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** No primary documented bacterial co-pathogen pattern [GAP]; occasional polymicrobial wound infections are reported in cutaneous cases [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per the ECMM/MSG global guideline (2019): liposomal amphotericin B first-line (5 mg/kg/day; 10 mg/kg/day with CNS involvement) [D]. Documented step-down or salvage: isavuconazole or posaconazole [D]. Prompt surgical debridement is documented as essential alongside antifungals, and delayed surgery is documented to worsen outcomes [D]. Glycemic control, reversal of immunosuppression, and corticosteroid optimization are documented adjunctive measures [D].

### SARS — paired with SARS-CoV AND SARS-CoV-2 per §217 [H]
- **Fungal connections:** His pairing rule [H]: both viruses are presented together. Documented — on the SARS-CoV (2003 SARS) side, secondary *Aspergillus* infections were documented in SARS ICU patients, particularly with corticosteroid use [D]. On the paired SARS-CoV-2 side, CAPA and COVID-associated mucormycosis are documented (see the COVID-19 row) [D].
- **Yeast connections:** Documented candidemia in prolonged ICU courses on both the SARS (2003) and SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) sides [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented secondary bacterial infections in 2003 SARS — including MRSA and ventilator-associated pneumonia pathogens [D]; the SARS-CoV-2 side mirrors this with documented secondary bacterial pneumonia [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented — no specific proven therapy for SARS was ever established; 2003 management was supportive, with ribavirin and corticosteroids used empirically without demonstrated efficacy [D]. Lopinavir/ritonavir and interferon were studied afterward without becoming standard [D]. For the paired SARS-CoV-2 side, documented agents are nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, remdesivir, and dexamethasone per NIH guidelines [D]. No vaccine was ever licensed for SARS-CoV [D].

### Respiratory syncytial virus infection
- **Fungal connections:** Rarely documented — isolated case reports of invasive aspergillosis following severe RSV in immunocompromised hosts [D]; no systematic fungal connection exists [GAP].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link [GAP].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented secondary bacterial pneumonia complicating severe RSV — *S. pneumoniae*, *S. aureus*, *H. influenzae* [D]; documented bacterial otitis media following RSV in children [D]; documented bacterial co-infection increases severity in hospitalized infants [D].

Palivizumab is documented for prophylaxis in high-risk infants [D]; nirsevimab is documented as a newer long-acting monoclonal antibody for infant prophylaxis [D]. Ribavirin has documented limited use in severely immunocompromised patients [D]. Antibiotics are documented only for confirmed bacterial co-infection [D].

### Varicella / herpes zoster
- **Fungal connections:** No documented fungal co-pathogen connection [GAP].
- **Yeast connections:** No documented yeast link [GAP].
- **Bacterial connections:** Documented secondary bacterial skin infections of varicella lesions — *Streptococcus pyogenes* and *S. aureus*, including documented invasive group A streptococcal disease and necrotizing fasciitis as complications of chickenpox [D]; documented bacterial superinfection of zoster lesions in immunocompromised patients [D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per the IDSA 2018 VZV clinical practice guideline: acyclovir, valacyclovir, or famciclovir for herpes zoster, ideally started within 72 hours of rash onset [D]; valacyclovir or acyclovir documented for varicella in at-risk patients [D]. The recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) and varicella vaccines are documented preventive measures per CDC/ACIP [D]. Documented postherpetic neuralgia is managed with gabapentinoids, tricyclics, or topical agents — a documented non-antiviral medication layer [D].

### Candidozyma auris disease
- **Fungal connections:** The organism is the disease — *Candidozyma auris* (formerly *Candida auris*) is the yeast pathogen itself; no separate non-yeast fungal connection is documented [D]. Its clades (I–VI) carry documented clade-specific resistance patterns, with >90% of global isolates fluconazole-resistant [D]; documented pan-resistant isolates (resistant to all three antifungal classes) exist at low frequency (~4% in some datasets) [D].
- **Yeast connections:** The yeast IS the disease [D]. Documented co-colonization with other *Candida* species in outbreak settings [D]; documented skin colonization that persists after clinical cure, sustaining transmission [D].
- **Bacterial connections:** The documented *S. aureus*–*C. auris* mixed biofilm pairing — biofilm synergy documented in the literature — is the concrete bacterial connection [D]. This documented pairing is the foothold for his infection-track grouping of the three tracks together [H→D].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per CDC clinical treatment guidance: echinocandins are recommended initial therapy for clinical infection in adults — anidulafungin, caspofungin, or micafungin [D]. Liposomal amphotericin B is documented for echinocandin resistance or failure to improve after ~5 days [D]. Voriconazole is documented as a possible option only with confirmed susceptibility [D]. CDC documents that colonization alone (without clinical infection) should not be treated, and no decolonization regimen is documented as effective [D]. Infectious-diseases consultation and public-health reporting are documented as recommended [D].

### Cryptococcosis
- **Fungal connections:** The fungus IS the disease — *Cryptococcus neoformans* / *C. gattii* meningitis and pulmonary disease, the leading documented cause of adult meningitis in advanced HIV [D].
- **Yeast connections:** The pathogen is itself a yeast-like encapsulated fungus, so the disease sits on the yeast track by its documented taxonomy — the foothold for his grouping [H→D]. No separate yeast co-pathogen is documented [GAP for a distinct co-pathogen].
- **Bacterial connections:** No documented systematic bacterial co-pathogen pattern [GAP].
- **Medication choices:** Documented per WHO 2022 and the ECMM/ISHAM/ASM 2024 guideline (endorsed by IDSA): induction with single high-dose liposomal amphotericin B (10 mg/kg) plus 14 days of flucytosine and fluconazole for HIV-associated meningitis in resource-limited settings, per the AMBITION trial [D]; 14-day daily amphotericin B–based induction is recommended in high-resource settings per CDC/NIH/IDSA/HIVMA 2024 [D]; followed by fluconazole consolidation (800 mg daily × 8 weeks) then maintenance [D]. The older IDSA 2010 regimen documented amphotericin B deoxycholate (0.7–1.0 mg/kg/day) plus flucytosine for 2 weeks [D]. Documented cryptococcal antigen (CrAg) screening and deferred ART initiation are part of the documented management package [D].

### Pneumocystis pneumonia
- **Fungal connections:** The fungus IS the disease — *Pneumocystis jirovecii* (formerly *P. carinii*), an atypical fungus that cannot be cultured [D]; it was historically misclassified as a protozoan, a documented taxonomic correction [D].

- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — No documented fungal co-pathogen, secondary fungal infection, or fungal mimic is established for Colorado tick fever in the literature. ### Rheumatic fever
- **Fungal connections:** [GAP] — no documented fungal connection.
- **Yeast connections:** [GAP] — none documented.
- **Bacterial connections:** [D] — the bacterium IS the trigger: rheumatic fever is a post-infectious autoimmune sequela of Group A *Streptococcus pyogenes* pharyngitis (or skin infection), via molecular mimicry. ### Blastomycosis
- **Fungal connections:** [D] — the fungus IS the disease: *Blastomyces dermatitidis*, *B. gilchristii* (eastern North America), and *B. helicus* (western North America) are dimorphic fungi causing pulmonary and disseminated disease [D].
- **Yeast connections:** [H→D] — his grouping keeps yeast separate, and there is a documented foothold: *Blastomyces* is thermally dimorphic — it grows as a mold in the environment and converts to a yeast phase at body temperature, and the yeast form (broad-based budding yeast on microscopy) is what the pathologist sees in tissue [D].

- **Medication choices:** IDSA blastomycosis guidelines (2008, still referenced): mild-to-moderate disease — oral itraconazole 200 mg three times daily for 3 days then once or twice daily for 6–12 months (first-line) [D]. Moderately severe to severe, disseminated, CNS, immunocompromised, or pregnancy: lipid amphotericin B 3–5 mg/kg/day (or deoxycholate 0.7–1 mg/kg/day) for 1–2 weeks (4–6 weeks for CNS), then step-down to itraconazole to complete at least 12 months [D]. Azoles are contraindicated in pregnancy; amphotericin B is the drug of pregnancy [D]. Voriconazole is used for CNS disease for its CSF penetration [D]. Sources: IDSA Clinical Practice Guidelines (Clin Infect Dis 2008); AccessMedicine CMDT 2025.

### Scrofula

### Elephantiasis
- **Fungal connections:** [H→D] — elephantiasis itself is filarial, not fungal, but chronic lymphedematous limbs have a documented fungal relationship: toe-web intertrigo (dermatophyte and Candida colonization of skin folds) is a documented entry lesion that precipitates recurrent acute attacks, and fungal skin infection is part of the documented morbidity-management picture [D].

Established elephantiasis is managed by lymphedema care (hygiene, compression, elevation), not drugs — drugs clear microfilariae but do not reverse fibrosis [D]. Sources: WHO/PAHO lymphatic filariasis guidance.
