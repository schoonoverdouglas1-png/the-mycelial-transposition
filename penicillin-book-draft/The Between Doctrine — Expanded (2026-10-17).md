## Part 1 — What the record knows as fact, and makes common sense with it

**The equation's left side is the record's oldest confusion.** Tuberculosis was called consumption, phthisis, the white plague — and for a century, everything that wasted the lungs was called it. The endemic mycoses wore the name for decades: histoplasmosis was routinely misdiagnosed as TB across the Ohio-Mississippi Valley; coccidioidomycosis still is, wherever it isn't looked for. The mimics didn't just resemble TB — they *were* TB, in the charts, until the test said otherwise [D].

**The equation's right side is the record's commonest showing.** Community-acquired pneumonia is the presentation that brings the patient in — and in the endemic Southwest, 15–30% of it is a fungus (Valley Fever), 24% of it never tested for the fungus at all (Mayo Phoenix). The pneumonia showing is the great container: bacterial, viral, fungal, tuberculous — all of it arrives as pneumonia first [D].

**So the equation restates the record plainly:** the things that look like TB *are* the things that look like pneumonia — because they are the same patients, the same lungs, the same showing, sorted by whichever test got ordered. His 99–100 of 101% is the record's own number, rounded up by a man who trusts his eyes.

**The between is readable — right now, in his blood.** Encephalitis serology exists: arboviral IgM/IgG panels, the California serogroup with its 23% cross-reactivity, PRNT for confirmation. He has lived this panel — his, his mother's. The between isn't theoretical; it's a blood draw [D].

**The between touches both ends, documented.** Pneumococcal meningitis ↔ encephalitis (his conjoined pair). Influenza → encephalitis. Measles → SSPE. COVID → encephalitis. The lung pathogens reach the brain; the brain's inflammation follows the lung's infection. The middle pillar isn't between the ends by poetry — it's between them by traffic [D].

**"Already known somehow" is the record's oldest truth.** Tuberculosis is the most storied disease in medicine — consumption, the romantic killer, the sanatorium era. Pneumonia is Osler's "captain of the men of death." These two have been known longer than any other killers, by name, by showing, by the thousands of lungs they took. His "somehow" points at the obvious: the oldest-known diseases are known *through* their showings, and the showing is pneumonia [D].
