# Research Note — The Lab Reports the Name It Chooses

**Filed:** 2026-10-10 · For local peer review alongside *The Mycelial Transposition* manuscript
**Source:** Trade-press commentary, CAP TODAY (College of American Pathologists), March 2026; Isabella W. Martin, MD (then vice chair, CAP Microbiology Committee), summarizing a CAP Microbiology Committee opinion paper in *Clin Infect Dis* (Dien Bard J, et al., published online Sept 23, 2025; doi:10.1093/cid/ciaf474)
**Link:** https://www.captodayonline.com/what-the-evolving-microbial-nomenclature-means-for-the-clinical-microbiology-lab/

## The piece (in brief)

- Martin describes how rapid genomic sequencing is reshaping microbial classification and naming, and the downstream operational problems for clinical microbiology labs: updated names can appear in organism-ID systems before clinicians recognize them, creating confusion in result interpretation, antimicrobial decision-making, and recognition of recurrent infections. Inconsistent adoption across diagnostic platforms and electronic health records makes it worse.
- She distinguishes two kinds of rename: ones that clearly benefit care (newly defined species clarifying resistance patterns and enabling species-specific susceptibility breakpoints — e.g. *Staphylococcus pseudintermedius*) and ones "driven primarily by phylogenetic data" that may carry limited clinical value while disrupting established diagnostic and treatment workflows. Her example of the second kind is the genus *Candida* being split into multiple teleomorph genera — *Pichia, Debaryomyces, Torulopsis, Kluyveromyces*.
- Structural details: bacterial naming (ICNP) regulates names but designates no "official" taxonomy, so old and new names can both remain valid — which means laboratories must *deliberately choose* which name to report. Fungal naming (under the International Association for Plant Taxonomy) has changed particularly extensively since DNA-based classification and the "One Fungus, One Name" principle, sometimes outpacing clinical guidance and provider familiarity. Viral taxonomy, by contrast, is structured to largely preserve clinically familiar names despite reclassification.
- Governance response: an ad hoc committee on the nomenclature of clinical fungi was formed in 2023 (under ISHAM, the European Confederation of Medical Mycology, and the Fungal Diagnostics Laboratory Consortium) to build a consensus guideline for fungal name changes for clinical use. And CAP's own checklist requirement on taxonomy/nomenclature (MIC.11375, first implemented 2014) now emphasizes "consistency and thoughtfulness in nomenclature adoption, rather than mandatory adoption of every taxonomic update" — labs are told to evaluate whether a name change is scientifically valid, clinically meaningful, and operationally feasible before implementing it.

## The nomenclature hook

- This is the manuscript thesis stated by the lab trade press itself: reporting a name is a deliberate, operational act with downstream clinical consequences — the name does policy work in the laboratory, not just in journals. The split between "scientifically valid" and "clinically meaningful and operationally feasible" is exactly the axis the nomenclature chapters need.
- Direct lineage with the notes already filed: the *Candida* → *Candidozyma auris* rename (PDR case note 2026-10-07, mortality meta-analysis 2026-10-08, dual-naming cohort note 2026-10-09) and the *Candida glabrata* → *Nakaseomyces glabratus* retention note (2026-10-09) are instances of exactly the disruption Martin describes. The "name kept anyway" behavior now has a practitioner-side rationale: thoughtful non-adoption is institutional policy at CAP, not just authorial habit.
- The viral-taxonomy contrast is a usable foil: one kingdom's naming rules are engineered to preserve familiar clinical names through reclassification; fungal rules are not. That is a structural comparison the manuscript can use.
- The 2023 ad hoc clinical-fungi committee is worth a line: an institution was built to manage the rename workload — further evidence that naming is an administrative/clinical act, not merely a taxonomic one.
- Secondary specimen, same thread: a 2026 *Frontiers in Tropical Diseases* review of *Trichophyton indotineae* summarizes Tang et al. as finding the new name justified "mainly on genetic rather than practical grounds" — another instance of the validity-vs-utility split. (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/tropical-diseases/articles/10.3389/fitd.2026.1960706/full)

## Honest caveats (do not drop these)

1. **Commentary, not research.** This is a lab-trade magazine article summarizing a committee opinion paper — practitioner/consensus perspective, not peer-reviewed experimental evidence. Cite it as such.
2. **Not evidence for the reclassification argument.** Like the other notes, this is a specimen of naming practice doing work in the clinic — material for the nomenclature chapters, not proof of the penicillin-reclassification claim.
3. **The example genera are proposed.** Martin presents the *Candida* split into *Pichia, Debaryomyces, Torulopsis, Kluyveromyces* as an example of disruptive phylogeny-driven renaming; do not overclaim the universality of that split beyond what she states.
4. **Keep separate from personal medical care.** Manuscript material only.

## Open questions for peer review

- The CAP checklist's three-way test (valid, meaningful, feasible) gives the manuscript a ready-made rubric for evaluating renames. Should that rubric structure the chapter on how names move through practice?
- The bacterial case (both names valid; the lab *chooses*) versus the fungal case (extensive, sometimes outpacing guidance) versus the viral case (familiar names preserved) — is a three-kingdom comparison the right frame for the "names do policy work" argument?
