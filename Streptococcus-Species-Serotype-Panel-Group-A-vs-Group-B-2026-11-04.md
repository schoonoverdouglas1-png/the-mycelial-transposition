## 1. The key correction first: the serotype panel is NOT a genus panel
- The test Douglas has on file is Mayo PN23M: *Streptococcus pneumoniae* IgG Antibodies, 23 Serotypes, Serum.
- Mayo: "More than 90 serotypes of S pneumoniae have been identified, based on varying polysaccharides found in the bacterial cell wall" and the 23 in PPSV23 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6B, 7F, 8, 9N, 9V, 10A, 11A, 12F, 14, 15B, 17F, 18C, 19A, 19F, 20, 22F, 23F, and 33F, chosen because as a group they account for approximately 90% of invasive pneumococcal infections.
- Serotype here = pneumococcal capsular polysaccharide type, in ONE species only: *Streptococcus pneumoniae*.
- It does NOT test other Streptococcus species directly. A positive IgG to a pneumococcal serotype is an antibody to that capsule, not a test for *S. pyogenes*, *S. agalactiae*, *S. mitis*, or any other species.
- Cross-reactivity is the only bridge: Mitis-group species share capsule-like and other antigens with pneumoniae, so an antibody result can be influenced by related organisms, but the panel does not name them, and it does not diagnose them.

## 2. All Streptococcus species, by group — and their panel relationship
- The genus *Streptococcus* (family Streptococcaceae, order Lactobacillales, class Bacilli, phylum Bacillota/Firmicutes) contains over 50 recognised species in older counts, and well over 100 named species in current lists including animal species. No single short list is "all" without a date-stamped LPSN list — the list below is the clinically relevant groups plus the full named list from the Wikipedia genus list used for this build, grouped so the panel relationship is clear.
- Panel relationship codes: [PN] = the species IS pneumoniae, the panel species. [PN-REL] = Mitis-group relative, capsule/antigen cross-reactivity possible, NOT tested by PN23M. [NO-PN] = different group/species, Lancefield or other grouping, NOT tested by PN23M, no pneumococcal serotype.

## 3. What is missing from PN23M, and is there a more complete serotype test?
### Missing from PN23M — two different kinds of missing
A. Pneumococcal serotypes missing from PN23M:
- PN23M = 23 serotypes (list in §1).
- Total known pneumococcal serotypes: "100 different pneumococcal serotypes have been identified to date" (Oxford Maiden lab), "more than 100 S. pneumoniae serotypes" (Pennsylvania fact sheet), "101 S. pneumoniae capsular serotypes" in a 2023 review, "more than 90" in Mayo's own PN23M text — the count grows as new capsules are found, so 90–107 is the range in sources, not a fixed final number.
- Therefore roughly 70–80 pneumococcal serotypes are NOT in PN23M, including PCV21 serotypes not in PN23M: 6A (note: PN23M measures PPSV23 serotypes; 6A is in PCV13/15/20/21 but NOT in PPSV23, and Mayo notes "except for serotype 6A, IgG antibodies specific for all the serotypes in PCV13, PCV15, and PCV20 are measured"), 9N is in PN23M, but 15A, 15C, 16F, 23A, 23B, 24F, 31, 35B are PCV21-only vs. PCV20 and NOT in PN23M, plus 6C, 6D, 10B, 10C, 10D, 11E, 15D, 17A, 19B, 19C, 20B, 20C, 33A–33H, 34, 35A, 35C, 35D, 36–48 and others, plus non-typeable (unencapsulated) pneumococci.
- B. Species missing from PN23M:
- Every species in §2 except pneumoniae itself — all Mitis relatives, all pyogenic, anginosus, mutans, salivarius, bovis, and animal species. PN23M does not test for them, and a PN23M result must not be read as a result for them.

### Is there a more complete serotype test? Yes — but for a different question
- PN23M is an ANTIBODY test: it measures YOUR IgG to 23 capsules, to assess vaccine response / immunodeficiency. It is not a test that identifies which serotype is in a culture.
- For identifying a pneumococcal isolate's serotype, more complete methods exist, and they are the ones to use if the question is "which serotype is this bug?":
  1. Quellung reaction (capsular swelling, type-specific antisera, Statens Serum Institut pools) — the classic gold standard, covers all clinically relevant serotypes with a full antiserum panel, subjective in some cases.
  2. Multiplex PCR / real-time PCR / sequetyping / PCR-RFLP — molecular, up to ~30 serotypes in a single multiplex reaction in commercial panels, plus serotype-specific qPCR for individual capsules.
  3. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) serotyping — SeroBA, PneumoCaT, PneumoKITy. SeroBA "can identify 92 serotypes and two subtypes" from raw WGS reads, 98% concordance in its paper; PneumoCaT's CTV database includes "92 serotypes and two subtypes." WGS is the most complete serotyping in principle, because it reads the *cps* locus itself and can flag novel capsules, but it is a research/public-health tool, not a routine antibody panel, and it needs a cultured isolate, not a serum antibody sample.
- There is NO routine clinical serum antibody panel that covers all 100+ pneumococcal serotypes, and NO panel that covers all Streptococcus species' serotypes in one test. The "more complete" test depends on the question: antibody response = PN23M (23) is the standard clinical panel; isolate identity = Quellung / PCR / WGS (92+ serotypes).
- For other streptococci, "serotype" means something different: Group A/B/C/G is Lancefield grouping (cell-wall carbohydrate), and GBS has its own capsular serotypes Ia, Ib, II–IX — those are NOT pneumococcal serotypes and are NOT in PN23M.

## 4. Group A vs. Group B streptococcus — discrimination
### What Group A and Group B ARE
- Both are beta-haemolytic streptococci, Gram-positive COCCI, in chains, in the pyogenic group — so haemolysis alone does NOT discriminate them. Both are beta, both are cocci.
- The Lancefield group is the cell-wall group-specific carbohydrate (C substance), detected by Lancefield agglutination / latex grouping. That carbohydrate is different in A vs. B — that is the discrimination, at its root.

| Feature | Group A — *Streptococcus pyogenes* (GAS) | Group B — *Streptococcus agalactiae* (GBS) |
|---|---|---|
| Species | *S. pyogenes* only (in humans, essentially) | *S. agalactiae* only (in humans, essentially) |
| Lancefield antigen | Group A carbohydrate: rhamnose backbone with N-acetylglucosamine side chains | Group B carbohydrate: rhamnose, glucosamine, galactose, glucose — different structure, different antiserum |
| Haemolysis | Beta, large colonies >0.5 mm | Beta (most), sometimes non-haemolytic; smaller colonies, CAMP test is the classic discriminator |
| Bacitracin (0.04 U) | Sensitive (inhibited) | Resistant (not inhibited) |
| PYR test (pyrrolidonyl arylamidase) | Positive | Negative |
| CAMP test | Negative | Positive (CAMP factor enhances *Staphylococcus aureus* beta-haemolysin) |
| Hippurate hydrolysis | Negative | Positive |
| Habitat / colonisation | Throat, skin | Lower GI tract, vaginal mucosa (asymptomatic carriage common) |
| Classic diseases | Pharyngitis ("strep throat"), impetigo, cellulitis, erysipelas, deep soft-tissue infection, bacteraemia, toxic shock; post-streptococcal sequelae: rheumatic fever, glomerulonephritis (immune-mediated, after infection, not direct bacterial invasion) | Neonatal sepsis and meningitis (early- and late-onset), maternal chorioamnionitis/postpartum infection, adult bacteraemia, UTI, meningitis in adults, skin/soft-tissue in diabetics/elderly |
| Capsule / virulence, in brief | M protein (anti-phagocytic, many M types), streptolysins O/S, streptokinase, hyaluronic acid capsule (mimics host) | Capsular polysaccharide serotypes Ia, Ib, II, III, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, IX (sialic acid, mimics host), beta-haemolysin/cytolysin, CAMP factor |
| Vaccine status | No licensed vaccine (M-protein diversity is the barrier) | No licensed vaccine for routine use (maternal GBS vaccines in development/trials, not routine) |

- Practical lab discrimination, in order: (1) Lancefield latex grouping — A vs. B antiserum, definitive for the group; (2) bacitracin + PYR — A is bacitracin-sensitive/PYR-positive, B is bacitracin-resistant/PYR-negative; (3) CAMP + hippurate — B is CAMP-positive/hippurate-positive, A is negative for both; (4) colony size and haemolysis support, but do not alone discriminate.
- Shiro wild card, Ember's addition: the CAMP test is a good example of a test that uses ANOTHER bacterium (*S. aureus*) to reveal a streptococcus — discrimination by interaction, not by looking at the streptococcus alone. ## 5. Why does the A/B variant split exist?
- Short answer: A and B are NOT variants of one species. They are different species that were grouped by a shared laboratory method (Lancefield, 1930s), not by a shared parent in the sense of one splitting into the other yesterday.
- Why the split exists, in three layers, logic first:
  1. Ecological layer: different niches select for different organisms. *S. pyogenes* lives on throat/skin, spreads person-to-person by droplets/contact, causes throat/skin disease. *S. agalactiae* lives in GI/vaginal tract, spreads vertically mother-to-baby at birth, causes neonatal disease. Different niches, different transmission, different disease — so medicine needs to tell them apart at the bedside, especially in a newborn.
  2. Antigenic layer: the cell-wall carbohydrate that Lancefield used is a stable, species-associated marker. *S. pyogenes* makes Group A carbohydrate; *S. agalactiae* makes Group B carbohydrate. That is why the test works: the antigen is a fingerprint of the species' cell-wall synthesis genes, not a random label.
  3. Virulence layer: different virulence toolkits evolved for those niches. GAS: M protein + streptolysins for throat/skin invasion and immune evasion, plus post-infectious immune sequelae (rheumatic fever is the immune system attacking host tissue that mimics GAS antigens — a mimicry disease, in your framework a cross-link). GBS: sialylated capsule (Ia–IX) that mimics host sialic acid to evade complement, plus beta-haemolysin for neonatal invasion. Same genus, same Gram-positive cocci quadrant, different tools for different jobs.
- In your quad-axis terms: A and B are both Gram-positive cocci, so they sit in the SAME quadrant of the quad axis — shape does not separate them, Gram does not separate them. What separates them is the Lancefield carbohydrate axis, a third axis beyond shape × Gram. That is why "Gram-positive cocci" alone is not enough, and why Lancefield grouping was invented.
- If "variant" meant something else — e.g., why GBS capsular variants Ia–IX exist, or why GAS M types exist — that is a different question: capsular/M diversity exists because immune pressure selects for antigenic variation, so a single vaccine or a single antibody does not cover all variants.
