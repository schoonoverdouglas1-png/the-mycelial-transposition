# Diphtheria and Its Parallels — The Pseudomembrane Diseases (2026-10-31)

**A completed paper, built October 31, 2026, from the diphtheria parallels work of the same day.**

## How to read this paper

- **Tier labels on every claim:** [D] documented, from the published record · [H] his, the author's account · [H→D] his, with a documented foothold · [LOGIC] an inference, with its steps shown · [GAP] unknown, left empty, never invented.
- **A BREAKS verdict is the class working, not a failed theory.** This paper is built the author's way: postulates are posed, some deliberately wrong, so that testing them against the record can show what holds. A verdict against a postulate is the lesson, not a correction of the author.
- **A pseudomembrane is a showing, not a diagnosis.** The same white or gray membrane is made by many agents. This paper's whole argument is that the membrane is a slot, and different diseases take turns filling it.
- **Element columns.** Every disease below gets four element columns — bacterial, fungal, yeast, viral — following the author's three-group system (bacterial, fungal, and yeast kept separate [H]) plus the viral track. Where a column is empty it is marked [GAP]. Nothing is padded to fill a column.
- **Shiro: PENDING on every row.** The Shiro column is reserved in every table and never invented, per the author's standing rule.
- **Readings flagged, not silently corrected:** "diptheria" is the author's dictated spelling of diphtheria · "candidus" is read as *Candida* (Latin *candidus* means white, the root of the genus name) · "taphtheria" is read as "that diphtheria," the spoken elision of the two words.
- **Boundary.** This paper is general information only. It diagnoses no one. The author's childhood account (a white tongue, throat, and lung showing shared with his sister) is filed separately and is NOT used as evidence anywhere in this paper.

## Part I — The anchor: diphtheria

### §1. Respiratory diphtheria — *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*

- **Showing [D]:** a thick, firmly adherent grayish-white pseudomembrane in the pharyngeal, tonsillar, or nasal passage, which bleeds easily when touched. Old names: "malignant croup" and throat distemper [D].
- **Agent [D]:** toxigenic *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*. The toxin is an exotoxin encoded by the *tox* gene of lysogenic corynebacteriophages — the disease's weapon is viral in origin. Without the phage there is no toxin; without the toxin there is no diphtheria.
- **Extent [D]:** the membrane reaches the larynx, trachea, and even the lower respiratory tract, and satellite infections may occur in the esophagus, stomach, or lower airways.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** toxin absorbed into the bloodstream and distributed to the tissues — heart (myocarditis, arrhythmia, heart failure) and nerves (palatal palsy, polyneuropathy) — plus airway obstruction by the membrane itself.
- **Connection to everything below:** diphtheria taught medicine the membrane slot. Every disease in Part II is either a fellow tenant of that slot, a mimic of its showing, or a relative of its toxin.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *C. diphtheriae* [D]; co-present streptococci, *S. aureus* [D] | [GAP] as co-element (molds are mimics, §3–§4, not shown co-elements) | *Candida albicans* as mimic / possibly cocirculating [H→D]; as confirmed co-pathogen [GAP] | Corynephage β, *tox* gene [D]; mimic viruses adeno/EBV/HSV/CMV [D] | PENDING |

- **The membrane is polymicrobial [D].** Most *C. diphtheriae*-positive cultures are polymicrobial, including wound cultures from 36 (82%) patients and blood cultures from 6 (14%) patients in one 2020–2023 series. Dual infection is frequently observed, particularly with *Streptococcus* spp., and coinfection with *S. aureus* is also described. Any chronic skin lesion may harbor *C. diphtheriae* along with other organisms.
- **The 1967 count [D].** Of 116 children clinically diagnosed with diphtheria by white patches in the mouth, only 39 (33.6%) were culture-positive for diphtheria; 63 (55.8%) grew pyogenic organisms (*Streptococcus hemolyticus*, *Staphylococcus*, *E. coli*, *Pseudomonas*, *Aerobacter*); 8 had diphtheria combined with pyogenic organisms (including pneumococcus); 9 had combinations of monilia (various *Candida*) with pyogenic organisms. The authors concluded the majority of white-patch cases were due to bacteria other than *C. diphtheriae* or to mycotic organisms.
- **The outbreak count [D].** In the 2017–2019 Myanmar outbreak investigation, the *tox* gene was found in only 40% of the sample despite many reported pseudomembranes, and adenovirus, Epstein-Barr, herpes simplex virus, and *Candida albicans* — all causes of exudative pharyngitis — may have been cocirculating.
- **The official mimic list [D].** Public-health guidance lists the organisms that can cause a pharyngeal or tonsillar pseudomembrane besides *C. diphtheriae*: some streptococci, *Arcanobacterium haemolyticum*, *Candida albicans*, certain fusiform bacteria, Epstein-Barr virus, and cytomegalovirus.

- Simply put: Diphtheria's membrane is a gray-white leather that bleeds when you pull it, and it can grow down the windpipe until it chokes. Its poison is written by a virus living inside the bacterium. And the white patch was never one disease — in a room of 116 children with white patches, only a third had diphtheria.

## Part II — The parallels, fullest first

Diseases are ordered by information density: the fullest element inventories first, thinning as the paper goes, until the list runs out in Part IV.

### §2. Pseudomembranous necrotizing tracheitis — the roster disease

- **Showing [D]:** a rare, life-threatening cause of central airway obstruction; patients present with hoarseness, cough, dyspnea, wheezing, and inspiratory stridor. Endoscopy both diagnoses and removes the membrane.
- **Agents [D]:** the reported etiologies, named side by side, are *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*, *Corynebacterium pseudodiphtheriticum*, *S. aureus*, *Bacillus cereus*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Aspergillus* species, and *Haemophilus influenzae*. Reported cases include *S. aureus* secondary to influenza A virus and *Aspergillus fumigatus*.
- **Extent [D]:** trachea and central airways; obstruction is the danger.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** airway occlusion by membrane; underlying disease (immunosuppression, hematologic malignancy, transplantation, neutropenia) drives risk.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** this is diphtheria's slot with diphtheria as only one tenant among seven. The roster itself is the parallel.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *C. diphtheriae*, *C. pseudodiphtheriticum*, *S. aureus*, *B. cereus*, *Pseudomonas*, *H. influenzae* [D] | *Aspergillus* spp., incl. *A. fumigatus* [D] | [GAP] for tracheitis specifically | Influenza A (as antecedent to *S. aureus*) [D] | PENDING |

- Simply put: Seven different germs can build the same choking membrane in the windpipe. Diphtheria is one of the seven, not the whole list.

### §3. Pseudomembranous *Aspergillus* tracheobronchitis — the fungal membrane

- **Showing [D]:** three patterns of *Aspergillus* tracheobronchitis are described: obstructive (thick mucus plugs of hyphae), ulcerative (focal mucosal/cartilage invasion), and pseudomembranous — diffuse inflammation and invasion producing a pseudomembrane composed of necrotic debris from *Aspergillus* hyphae. Its differential includes viral infection (HSV, CMV, influenza B) and bacterial tracheitis (*S. aureus*, *H. influenzae*).
- **Agent [D]:** *Aspergillus* spp., usually *A. fumigatus*. A pseudomembrane partially occluding the tracheobronchial tree is most frequently associated with *C. diphtheriae* — and pseudomembranous tracheobronchitis from *Aspergillus* is the reported fungal counterpart.
- **Extent [D]:** the whole tracheobronchial tree, as intraluminal circumferential pseudomembrane and exudate, with only superficial mucosal invasion in the pseudomembranous pattern.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** airway occlusion, hemorrhage from bronchoarterial fistula in severe cases; risk factors are steroid and broad-spectrum antibiotic use, neutropenia, and malignancy. One immunocompetent case is reported.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the literature names diphtheria first and *Aspergillus* second for the same membrane. The fungal track's flagship parallel.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [GAP] as co-element (bacteria appear in its differential, not as shown co-elements) | *Aspergillus* spp., *A. fumigatus* [D] | [GAP] | HSV, CMV, influenza B in the differential [D] | PENDING |

- Simply put: A mold can build the same white curtain in the windpipe that diphtheria builds. It usually strikes people whose defenses are down, and its membrane is dead debris from the mold's own threads.

### §4. Airway and pulmonary mucormycosis — the vessel-invaders

- **Showing [D]:** invasive airway disease with sloughing mucosa, white pseudomembrane, and necrosis; a mixed case showed airways filled with mucus plugs and a white pseudomembrane occluding a lobe orifice.
- **Agents [D]:** Mucorales — *Rhizopus*, *Mucor*, *Cunninghamella bertholletiae*, *Absidia*. In the mixed case, lavage grew *Cunninghamella bertholletiae* while the pseudomembrane's pathology showed infarcted necrotic tissue containing *Aspergillus* elements — two molds in one membrane.
- **Extent [D]:** inhaled spores germinate in the airways, proliferate there, then penetrate the bronchial wall into blood vessels, causing thrombosis and pulmonary infarction.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** angioinvasion — the fungus eats through the airway wall into arteries. Host range is overwhelmingly debilitated or immunocompromised, especially diabetes.
- **Microscope distinction [D]:** Mucorales hyphae are broad (10–25 microns), ribbon-like, thin-walled, rarely septate, branching at right angles; *Aspergillus* hyphae are septate, acute-angle, dichotomously branching.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the membrane here is the least of it — diphtheria's membrane obstructs, mucor's membrane announces an invasion already through the wall.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [GAP] | Mucorales [D]; *Aspergillus* as co-mold in the mixed case [D] | [GAP] | [GAP] | PENDING |

- Simply put: These molds don't just coat the airway — they eat through its wall into the blood. The white membrane is the warning sign, not the whole disease.

### §5. Pseudomembranous and esophageal candidiasis — the yeast membrane

- **Showing [D]:** white curd-like plaques on tongue and mouth that wipe or scrape off (unlike diphtheria's adherent, bleeding membrane); esophageal disease adds pain and difficulty swallowing and food sticking.
- **Agent [D]:** *Candida albicans* most commonly; other *Candida* species occur.
- **Extent [D]:** mouth → throat → esophagus; in disseminated disease, through the bloodstream to organs including the lungs. Lung involvement is usually part of disseminated disease, not a primary lung membrane.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** tissue invasion through blood in disseminated disease; mucosal disease stays superficial. Risk rises with immune suppression, antibiotics, and inhaled steroids.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the great mimic and the great contrast — same white showing, opposite membrane behavior (wipes off vs. bleeds), and the organism the 1967 study found mixed with pyogenic bacteria in white-patch children.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pyogenic co-organisms in the 1967 combinations [D] | [GAP] (molds are neighbors, not shown co-elements) | *Candida* spp., *C. albicans* [D] | [GAP] | PENDING |

- Simply put: Yeast makes a white coating that wipes away. Diphtheria makes a leather that bleeds. That one touch tells them apart.

### §6. Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (CMC) — the childhood, familial yeast disease

- **Showing [D]:** severe, recurrent, or persistent infections of skin, nails, and mucosa by *Candida* organisms.
- **Agents and mechanism [D]:** defects in the IL-17 immune pathway — *STAT1, STAT3, CARD9, RORC, ACT1, IL-17RA, IL-17RC, IL-17F, AIRE*, and *CLEC7A*. CMC is often sporadic, although it may be familial, and has been reported in both autosomal dominant and recessive forms; it frequently presents during childhood.
- **Extent [D]:** mouth, throat, skin, nails; esophageal involvement occurs. Invasive spread is the exception, seen mainly in *CARD9*-type defects.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** a genetic immune defect, not a toxin. In the APS-1 form (*AIRE*, autosomal recessive), chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis is the most common initial manifestation, with a median age of onset between 1.7 and 3 years, followed by hypoparathyroidism and Addison disease.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the childhood white mouth, explained the opposite way — diphtheria is an acquired toxin disease, CMC is an inherited immune defect. Same showing in children, opposite logic.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [GAP] | [GAP] | *Candida* spp. [D] | [GAP] | PENDING |

- Simply put: Some children get the white mouth over and over because their immune pathway for fighting yeast is built differently. It can run in families, and it starts in early childhood.

### §7. The modern airway slot — croup, epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis

- **Showing [D]:** airway obstruction with stridor, barking cough, and distress; bacterial tracheitis shows tracheal pseudomembranes and purulent secretions on endoscopy, and lateral neck imaging may show a tracheal pseudomembrane with necrotic epithelium subdividing the lumen.
- **Agents [D]:** viral croup (parainfluenza and other respiratory viruses); epiglottitis (*Haemophilus influenzae* type b, classically); bacterial tracheitis (*S. aureus*, *H. influenzae*) — bacterial tracheitis is a secondary infection of a viral upper respiratory infection, where viral damage to the upper airway predisposes to bacterial seeding of the trachea. In children, postviral bacterial tracheitis is usually *S. aureus* or *H. influenzae*.
- **Extent [D]:** larynx, subglottis, and trachea.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** inflammatory swelling and membrane/slough narrowing the airway; no diphtheria-type toxin.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** diphtheria's old name was "malignant croup." The airway-emergency slot stayed; the agents changed. This is the clearest proof in the paper that the slot outlives its tenant.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *S. aureus*, *H. influenzae* [D] | [GAP] | [GAP] | Parainfluenza and other croup viruses; antecedent viral URI [D] | PENDING |

- Simply put: The choking emergency diphtheria used to cause still happens — now viruses and ordinary bacteria cause it instead. Same emergency, new cast.

### §8. The other corynebacteria — diphtheria's cousins

- **Showing [D]:** *C. pseudodiphtheriticum*, generally part of the normal flora of respiratory tract, skin, and mucous membranes, can cause diphtheria-like illness with exudative pharyngitis mimicking a diphtheria pseudomembrane — but it lacks the diphtheria toxin, so its mechanism differs from toxin-mediated diphtheria.
- **Agent [D]:** *C. ulcerans* is toxigenic and causes classical respiratory diphtheria, with an animal source — a documented case traced to a domesticated dog. Other complex members (*C. pseudotuberculosis*, *C. silvaticum*) are named in the literature; see Part IV for where their inventories run out.
- **Extent [D]:** pharynx, larynx, nose, skin — the same territories as diphtheria.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** toxin-mediated for *C. ulcerans*; non-toxin mechanisms for *C. pseudodiphtheriticum*.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the closest possible parallel — same genus, same showing, and the toxin is the difference between the killer and the mimic.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *C. pseudodiphtheriticum*, *C. ulcerans* [D] | [GAP] | [GAP] | Phage toxin gene in *C. ulcerans* [D]; absent in *C. pseudodiphtheriticum* [D] | PENDING |

- Simply put: Diphtheria has cousins that look exactly like it. The one with the toxin gene is diphtheria in all but name; the one without it is a harmless resident wearing the same coat.

### §9. *Arcanobacterium haemolyticum* pharyngitis — the coryneform mimic

- **Showing [D]:** exudative pharyngitis mimicking streptococcal infection, with a grayish-green thick membrane that may be difficult to dislodge and often leaves a bleeding surface when torn off — the diphtheria-like description, in a non-diphtheria organism. A scarlatiniform rash occurs in about half of patients, mostly adolescents and young adults. Both *C. diphtheriae* and *A. haemolyticum* can cause a "bull neck".
- **Agent [D]:** *Arcanobacterium haemolyticum*, a gram-positive bacillus that morphologically resembles coryneform rods. Its pathogenicity relates to hemolysin, neuraminidase, and a phospholipase D that damages mammalian cell membranes and promotes necrosis. It often occurs in polymicrobial infections along with common respiratory pathogens such as streptococci.
- **Extent [D]:** pharynx and tonsils; rarely deeper infection (pneumonia, pyomyositis, usually as a coinfecting agent).
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** membrane damage and necrosis by phospholipase D and hemolysin; a lethal systemic exotoxin is reported in the older literature, but no diphtheria-type toxin is established — mechanism column kept honest.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the closest throat mimic after the corynebacteria — same membrane behavior (hard to dislodge, bleeds), same bull neck, different organism, different toxin logic.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *A. haemolyticum*; streptococci as polymicrobial partners [D] | [GAP] | [GAP] | [GAP] | PENDING |

- Simply put: A germ that looks like diphtheria's cousin under the microscope makes a membrane that acts like diphtheria's — hard to pull off, bleeding underneath — plus a scarlet-fever-like rash in half of patients.

### §10. Vincent's angina — the fusospirochetal membrane

- **Showing [D]:** a necrotizing ulcerative-membranous lesion of the tonsil, usually unilateral, with pain on swallowing, foul breath, a grayish-dirty pseudomembrane, and an undermined tonsil ulcer. The tonsils show single or multiple greyish-white ulcers that bleed when touched or when the pseudomembrane is removed.
- **Agents [D]:** the "fusospirillary complex" — *Fusobacterium* (usually *F. nucleatum*/*F. necrophorum*) plus spirochetes (*Treponema*/*Borrelia vincentii*), normal oral flora that become pathogens when local defenses weaken. Its differential diagnosis includes diphtheria.
- **Extent [D]:** tonsils, gums (as necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis, "trench mouth"), soft palate; lesions can spread across oral mucosa, lips, tongue, tonsils, and pharynx.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** local necrosis by an endogenous polymicrobial overgrowth — no distant toxin syndrome.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the membrane that comes from your own mouth flora turning on you, rather than from an invader. Diphtheria arrives; Vincent's erupts.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Fusobacterium* spp. + spirochetes (*Treponema*/*Borrelia vincentii*) [D] | [GAP] | [GAP] | [GAP] | PENDING |

- Simply put: The germs already living in your mouth can gang up and build a dirty gray membrane on your tonsil. It bleeds like diphtheria's, but it comes from inside the house.

### §11. Group A streptococcal pharyngitis and scarlet fever — the phage-toxin kin

- **Showing [D]:** exudative pharyngitis with fever and tonsillar exudate; scarlet fever adds a scarlatiniform rash and strawberry tongue. Note honestly: scarlet fever is included as a toxin parallel, not as a pseudomembrane disease — its membrane column is [GAP].
- **Agents [D]:** *Streptococcus pyogenes* (Group A streptococcus). The erythrogenic (scarlet fever) toxins are carried by a lysogenic phage — the T12 phage entry in the classic lysogenic-conversion table, beside cholera toxin (CTX phage), Shiga-like toxin (lambda phage), botulinum toxin (clostridial phages), and diphtheria toxin (corynephage beta).
- **Extent [D]:** pharynx and tonsils; systemic effects by toxin (rash) and by immune-mediated complications (rheumatic fever, glomerulonephritis).
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** phage-carried erythrogenic toxin plus immune-mediated sequelae — the same viral-element logic as diphtheria, in a different organism.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the proof that diphtheria's trick is not unique. Five famous diseases, five phage-carried toxins, one mechanism.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *S. pyogenes* [D] | [GAP] | [GAP] | T12 phage, erythrogenic toxin genes [D] | PENDING |

- Simply put: Strep throat's scarlet-fever poison is also written by a virus inside the bacterium. Diphtheria's trick has a whole family.

### §12. Infectious mononucleosis — the viral exudate

- **Showing [D]:** exudative pharyngitis with tonsillar exudate that can resemble a pseudomembrane; listed among the organisms that can cause a pharyngeal or tonsillar pseudomembrane besides *C. diphtheriae*.
- **Agent [D]:** Epstein-Barr virus (EBV); cytomegalovirus and adenovirus and herpes simplex virus are named alongside it as exudative-pharyngitis agents.
- **Extent [D]:** pharynx and tonsils; systemic lymphoid disease (fatigue, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly).
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** viral infection of B lymphocytes; no toxin, no membrane invasion.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the viral tenant of the throat slot — same white showing, no membrane at all in the strict sense, which is why it sits low on this list: the showing overlaps, the mechanism does not.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [GAP] | [GAP] | [GAP] | EBV (CMV, adenovirus, HSV as co-listed agents) [D] | PENDING |

- Simply put: Mono can paint the tonsils white too, but there is no leather membrane underneath — just virus-swollen tissue wearing white.

### §13. Rhinoscleroma (scleroma) — the granuloma that narrows the airway

- **Showing [D]:** a chronic granulomatous disease of the upper airway; tracheal manifestations are seen late in the disease course rather than at presentation. Tracheal involvement is exceptional and the disease progresses slowly, leaving mutilating and stenosing scar tissue.
- **Agent [D]:** *Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis*, a gram-negative bacillus. Classic histopathology shows Mikulicz cells (foamy macrophages with bacilli), Russell bodies, and gram-negative bacilli.
- **Extent [D]:** nose and nasopharynx first, then pharynx, larynx, and trachea; three progressive stages ending in the sclerotic phase with extensive scarring and fibrosis.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** chronic granulomatous inflammation and scar stenosis of the airway — slow strangulation by scar, not by membrane.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the airway slot filled by scar instead of membrane. Included because the tracheitis literature names it beside diphtheria — and because its differential includes the granulomatous mimics (tuberculosis, actinomycosis, leprosy, syphilis; fungal histoplasmosis, sporotrichosis, paracoccidioidomycosis), which is the next rung down the ladder.

- Simply put: A slow germ that turns the nose and windpipe to scar over years. No membrane — but the same ending, a narrowed airway, reached the slow way.

### §14. Tracheal actinomycosis — the dozen-case tenant

- **Showing [D]:** tracheal or laryngeal actinomycosis, a dozen cases reported, some responsible for tracheal obstructions that required tracheostomy and prolonged penicillin therapy.
- **Agent [D]:** *Actinomyces* spp., filamentous bacteria forming chronic suppurative granulomatous lesions with sulfur granules.
- **Extent [D]:** trachea and larynx.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** chronic suppurative granulomatous inflammation with obstruction — mechanism detail beyond this is [GAP] in this pass.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** a name on the tracheitis roster with almost no inventory behind it. It is included to show where full entries thin into case counts.

### §15. Syphilitic gumma of the larynx, trachea, and bronchi — the tertiary tenant

- **Showing [D]:** secondary syphilis gives gray-white plaques, ulcers, or papillomatous areas on the larynx; tertiary disease gives gummas — granulomatous lesions with a necrotic center and fibrous rim — that lead to persistent stenosis. Gummatous ulceration of the trachea and bronchi may be extensive and superficial or localized and deep, commonly about the tracheal bifurcation, and may cause necrosis of one or more rings, in some few cases coughed up.
- **Agent [D]:** *Treponema pallidum*. Syphilitic disease of any form is rare in the trachea — three cases out of 1,145 in which the pharynx, larynx, or trachea was affected.
- **Extent [D]:** larynx, trachea, bronchi; late disease only, for practical purposes — secondary tracheal lesions are extremely uncommon and fleeting.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** tertiary granulomatous destruction and cicatricial stenosis; no membrane, no toxin.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the oldest tenant of the airway slot in the literature — a 1903 analysis of 128 recorded cases — and the one whose showing (gray-white plaques) most closely mimics diphtheria's color while sharing none of its mechanism.

- Simply put: Late syphilis can rot the windpipe's rings until they are coughed up, and scar what is left shut. Gray-white like diphtheria, nothing like it underneath.

### §16. *Bacillus cereus* pseudomembranous tracheobronchitis — the single-case tenant

- **Showing [D]:** pseudomembranous tracheobronchitis with fever, cough, chest pain, and dyspnea in a patient with aplastic anemia, reported by Strauss and colleagues in 2001, with a fatal outcome.
- **Agent [D]:** *Bacillus cereus*, a ubiquitous gram-positive rod usually dismissed as a contaminant; lower respiratory infections by it number fewer than 20 reported cases.
- **Extent [D]:** trachea and bronchi.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** necrotizing toxin-mediated disease; some *B. cereus* isolates carry plasmid-encoded *Bacillus anthracis* toxin genes, which induce toxin-mediated severe necrotizing pneumonia — noted as a related toxin parallel, not established for the tracheitis case itself.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** the thinnest full entry in the paper — one case, one membrane, one death — and the last disease that still has a membrane inventory before the list runs out.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|

- Simply put: A food-poisoning germ, in a patient with no marrow reserve, built a diphtheria-like membrane in the windpipe. One case is all there is — and one case is enough to keep the slot open.

### §17. Pseudomembranous colitis — the boundary case (same word, different site)

- **Showing [D]:** raised yellow-white plaques on the colonic mucosa, composed of fibrin, mucus, inflammatory cells, and necrotic debris — the same word, "pseudomembrane," built from the same materials (fibrin, neutrophils, necrotic epithelium) in a different organ.
- **Agent [D]:** *Clostridioides difficile* infection following antibiotic disruption of gut flora; established in 1977 as caused by toxins produced by *C. difficile*, toxin A (enterotoxin) and toxin B (cytotoxin).
- **Extent [D]:** colon; severe cases progress to toxic megacolon, perforation, and sepsis.
- **Systemic mechanism [D]:** toxin-mediated colonic inflammation — contrast with diphtheria, whose toxin gene rides a phage [LOGIC: two toxin diseases, two toxin logics — one phage-carried, one produced by the bacterium after its competitors are cleared].
- **Why it is last [D]:** pseudomembranous colitis is a pattern of injury typically associated with *C. difficile* but not always caused by it — other causes include *E. coli* O157, *Shigella*, and other Shiga toxin–producing organisms, as well as acute ischemia, acute radiation injury, and several drugs. The pattern is bigger than any one agent, which is the paper's thesis restated in the colon.
- **Connection to diphtheria:** none by agent, everything by concept — the membrane is what injured tissue plus fibrin plus white cells looks like, wherever it happens.

| Bacterial | Fungal | Yeast | Viral | Shiro |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *C. difficile*; also *E. coli* O157, *Shigella* as pattern causes [D] | [GAP] | [GAP] | [GAP] | PENDING |

- Simply put: The colon can grow the same kind of membrane the throat grows, from the same ingredients, for a different reason. The word travels; the disease does not.

## Part III — The connections

### The membrane slot

One showing — fibrin, white cells, dead epithelium, stuck to a mucosal surface — is filled by a bacterium with a phage toxin (diphtheria), a mold (*Aspergillus*), a second mold family (Mucorales), a yeast (*Candida*), a coryneform mimic (*Arcanobacterium*), a mouth-flora duet (Vincent's), and a colon bacterium (*C. difficile*). The membrane is not a disease. It is what injured mucosa looks like when fibrin and white cells pave it. [LOGIC: same composition across sites and agents, listed in §§1–17.]

Diphtheria is not the exception here. It is the type specimen.

### The airway slot

Diphtheria's old name, "malignant croup," is the whole story in two words: the emergency was croup, and diphtheria was its malignant form. Modern croup (viral), epiglottitis (Hib), and bacterial tracheitis (*S. aureus*, *H. influenzae*) now fill the slot. The slot outlives its tenant. [LOGIC: §§1 and 7.]

### The polymicrobial lesson

The 1967 count (§1) and the modern culture data (§1) agree across sixty years: most white-patch clinics and most diphtheria cultures are polymicrobial. Single-agent stories are the exception in the membrane clinic, not the rule. The author's standing thesis — every bacterial disease has a viral element, and sicknesses travel as company, not alone — finds its sharpest documented form here: in diphtheria, the viral element is not a companion. It is the toxin gene itself. [H→D]

- Simply put for the whole paper: The white membrane is a stage, not an actor. Diphtheria was the first actor on it, and the most famous — but molds, yeasts, cousins, and colon bacteria have all played the same stage since. And in diphtheria's own play, the deadliest line was written by a virus.

## Part IV — Where the information runs out

The list stops here. What follows has a name but no element inventory in hand, and the paper will not pad past this point.

- ***Fusarium* spp. and *Scedosporium*/*Lomentospora* spp. as pseudomembrane-makers [GAP].** They are hyaline septate look-alikes of *Aspergillus* that require culture for identification — but they sit in the *Aspergillus* differential, not in the documented pseudomembrane roster. Name and microscope shape only; no membrane inventory.
- **"Certain fusiform bacteria" as pseudomembrane causes [GAP].** Named as a group on the official pseudomembrane list, without species-level inventory in hand.
- ***C. silvaticum* and *C. pseudotuberculosis* as diphtheria parallels [GAP].** Named as members of the diphtheriae species complex; no element inventory for them was in hand for this paper.
- **Candida as diphtheria's own co-pathogen [GAP].** Candida in the white-patch clinic is documented (§§1, 5); Candida combined with confirmed diphtheria specifically is not established in this pass.
- **Viral co-infection inside diphtheria cases specifically [GAP].** Viral mimics are documented; a virus riding along inside a diphtheria case is not established in this pass.
- **A modern sequencing inventory of one diphtheria membrane [GAP].** Culture plus metagenomics, bacteria plus fungi plus viruses, in one table — the study this paper's theory asks for, not yet in hand.

**The list ends here, by design.** Seventeen numbered diseases (§§1–17) carry complete entries — showing, agent, extent, mechanism, and element columns. No single disease among them has all four element columns fully documented: the fullest are diphtheria itself (bacterial and viral documented, yeast partial, fungal [GAP]) and the tracheitis roster syndrome (§2: bacterial, fungal, and viral documented, yeast [GAP]). That empty column is the paper's honest ending — and the next paper's beginning.
